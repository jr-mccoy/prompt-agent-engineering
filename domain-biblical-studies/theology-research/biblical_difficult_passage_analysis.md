# Hard / Difficult Passage Analysis

**Objective:** Take a passage that creates a genuine difficulty — an apparent contradiction with another text, an ethical problem, or an obscure meaning — and work it honestly: define the difficulty precisely, survey the proposed resolutions, weigh them, and say plainly what remains hard.

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt.** Difficult passages tempt fabricated harmonizations, invented background "facts," and cited-but-unverifiable scholars. This prompt references by address, labels confidence, and refuses to manufacture resolutions or sources.

**When to use:**
- A passage seems to contradict another, raises an ethical problem, or is genuinely obscure.
- You want honest options, not a glib answer or a forced harmonization.

**When NOT to use:**
- The passage is simply contested in interpretation (not a "problem") — use `biblical_multiview_interpretation_map.md`.

**Audience:** Group leaders (G), pastors (P), seminary/academic (A).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The passage and the difficulty.** Reference and text (user-supplied), plus what makes it hard (and any conflicting text, by address).
2. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground that tradition's resolution; alternatives and honest difficulty still presented.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- State the difficulty **precisely** (what exactly is the problem, and on what assumptions).
- Survey the **proposed resolutions** (textual, contextual, historical, theological, translational), attributed to streams where they differ, each with strengths and weaknesses.
- Reference all texts **by address** and mark background claims confidence-labeled / verify-required.
- Be honest: if a difficulty remains genuinely hard, say so; do not force a resolution.

### Must Not
- Invent harmonizing "facts," background, manuscripts, or scholars/quotations.
- Present a contested or speculative resolution as certain.
- Dismiss the difficulty glibly or pretend it's not real.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present resolutions descriptively and attribute them; preserve honest difficulty.
- **Must Not:** assert one tradition's resolution as the settled answer.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Define the difficulty
State precisely what the problem is and the assumptions it rests on (sometimes the assumption is the issue).

### Step 2 — Survey resolutions
List the proposed resolutions by type (textual/contextual/historical/theological/translational), attributed to streams, each with strengths and weaknesses. Mark background/textual claims verify-required.

### Step 3 — Weigh
Assess which resolutions are stronger and which are weak or speculative — without declaring a final verdict where the evidence doesn't allow one.

### Step 4 — Residual difficulty
State honestly what remains hard and what would help resolve it.

---

## Verification

- [ ] Difficulty stated precisely, with its assumptions.
- [ ] Resolutions surveyed by type, attributed to streams, with strengths/weaknesses.
- [ ] Texts by address; background claims confidence-labeled/verify-required.
- [ ] No fabricated harmonizations, background, or sources.
- [ ] Residual difficulty acknowledged honestly; no forced verdict.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Invent a historical "fact" that conveniently dissolves the problem.
- Present a speculative harmonization as the certain answer.
- Wave the difficulty away or, conversely, overstate it.

✅ **DO:**
- Pin down the difficulty and the assumptions behind it.
- Survey resolutions with honest strengths/weaknesses, attributed to streams.
- Mark background/textual claims verify-required and admit what stays hard.
