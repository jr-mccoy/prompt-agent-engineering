# Biblical Basis for a Worship Practice — Cross-Tradition Survey

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt.** Models assert one tradition's worship practice as "the biblical one," fabricate what traditions teach about their own practice, present contested readings as plain meaning, and invent historical claims about liturgical development. Every claim about what a tradition practices or teaches is verify-required against that tradition's own sources. The model describes and attributes — it does not endorse, rank, or adjudicate.

**Objective:** Examine the biblical material relevant to a specific worship practice and present how different traditions use that material to ground their own practice in Scripture — so the user can see the full landscape of biblical reasoning, not just one tradition's case.

**When to use:**
- You want to understand why Christians practice baptism, communion, prayer, music, ordination, etc. so differently — and what biblical reasoning each tradition offers.
- You are preparing to teach or preach on a worship practice and need to present the biblical case fairly across traditions.
- You are evaluating your own tradition's practice against the biblical material and want to see the strongest case for alternatives.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want a broad overview of how traditions differ on a practice (not focused on the biblical basis) — use `biblical_learner_compare_traditions_on_practice.md`.
- You want to study a doctrine (not a practice) across traditions — use `biblical_doctrine_study_neutral.md`.
- You want to compare interpretive positions on a single disputed passage — use `biblical_interpretive_views_comparison.md`.
- You want to argue that your tradition's practice is correct — this prompt presents the biblical case for each, it does not rule.

**Audience:** Pastor (P), academic (A), group leader (G).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The worship practice.** Stated specifically (e.g., "baptism by immersion vs. pouring vs. sprinkling," "weekly vs. monthly vs. quarterly communion," "exclusive psalmody vs. hymns and songs," "use of musical instruments in worship," "ordination of women," "head coverings," "speaking in tongues in corporate worship," "infant dedication vs. infant baptism," "liturgical vs. free-form prayer").
2. **Traditions of interest (optional).** Which traditions to foreground. If omitted, cover the major positions represented across identifiable traditions.
3. **Declared tradition (optional).** The user's own tradition — may be foregrounded but does not suppress other readings.
4. **Depth.** Survey (map the positions and key texts) or deep (trace the full hermeneutical argument for each position).

---

## Constraints

### Must
- For each tradition's practice: state the practice, identify the biblical texts it cites (by address), explain the hermeneutical move that connects text to practice, and present the strongest objection the practice faces from other traditions.
- Distinguish between texts that directly address the practice (if any), texts applied by inference or analogy, and texts cited from broader theological principles.
- Note where the biblical material is genuinely ambiguous or silent — and how traditions handle that silence differently.
- Present each tradition's practice as that tradition's own advocates would describe it — not as outsiders or critics characterize it.
- Flag all historical claims about liturgical development as verify-required.

### Must Not
- Assert that one practice is "the biblical practice" — present each as a position grounded in biblical reasoning by an identifiable tradition.
- Fabricate what traditions teach about their own worship practices, invent denominational positions, or attribute practices to traditions that do not hold them.
- Present one tradition's reading of a text as "the plain meaning" and others as departures from it.
- Collapse genuine differences ("they all basically agree") or exaggerate minor ones.
- Over-read modern worship forms back into the New Testament text (e.g., reading a contemporary worship service into Acts 2:42).

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present each tradition's biblical case with equal depth, charity, and seriousness. High-church and low-church, liturgical and free, sacramental and ordinance-based all receive proportional treatment.
- **Must Not:** use language that subtly favors one tradition's practice (e.g., "the historic practice" vs. "the innovative practice" — both are "practices held by [tradition] on the basis of [texts]").

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Confirm the practice and scope
Restate the specific practice, which traditions will be covered, any declared tradition, and the depth requested. If the practice is ambiguous (e.g., "baptism" could mean mode, subjects, meaning, or all three), clarify the scope with the user.

### Step 2 — Map the positions
Identify the major positions on this practice held by identifiable traditions:
- Name the practice variant and which traditions hold it.
- State each in 2-3 sentences as its proponents would state it.

### Step 3 — Biblical case for each position
For each position, trace the biblical argument:
- **Direct texts:** Passages that directly address the practice (by address). Note if the tradition considers these prescriptive, descriptive, or normative — and why.
- **Inferential texts:** Passages applied by analogy, theological principle, or inference (by address). Explain the interpretive step.
- **Broader theological framework:** If the position rests on a larger theological structure (covenant theology, dispensationalism, regulative principle, normative principle, etc.), identify it.
- **Handling of counter-texts:** How does this tradition respond to the texts cited by other positions?

### Step 4 — Where the biblical material is ambiguous or silent
Identify:
- Aspects of the practice where the NT is genuinely ambiguous (e.g., mode of baptism, frequency of communion).
- Aspects where the NT is silent and traditions fill the gap from other sources (tradition, reason, experience, pragmatics).
- How different traditions handle ambiguity and silence differently — and what hermeneutical principle drives that handling.

### Step 5 — Common ground and real differences
Summarize:
- Where traditions agree on the biblical material (even if they draw different practical conclusions).
- Where the real disagreements lie — and whether they are driven by different texts, different hermeneutics, or different theological frameworks.
- What a user would need to investigate further to form their own conviction on the practice.

## Verification

- [ ] Each tradition's practice is presented as its own proponents would recognize it.
- [ ] Biblical texts are cited by address for each position — direct, inferential, and framework texts distinguished.
- [ ] Counter-text handling is included for each position.
- [ ] Ambiguity and silence in the biblical material are honestly identified.
- [ ] No tradition's practice is asserted as "the biblical practice."
- [ ] No fabricated denominational positions, liturgical history, or tradition attributions.
- [ ] Language is evenly charitable across all positions — liturgical and free, sacramental and ordinance-based.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Assert that one practice is "the biblical one" — present each as a position grounded in biblical reasoning by an identifiable tradition.
- Present one tradition's reading as "the plain meaning" and others as departures.
- Over-read modern worship forms back into the NT text.

DO:
- Distinguish direct texts, inferential texts, and broader theological frameworks for each position.
- Identify where the biblical material is genuinely ambiguous or silent — and how traditions handle that differently.
- Flag all historical claims about liturgical development and all tradition attributions as verify-required.
