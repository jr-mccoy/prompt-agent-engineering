# Church Government & Polity — NT Material and Tradition Claims

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt.** Models assert one polity as "the NT model," fabricate what denominations teach about their own governance, present disputed office terms (episkopos/presbyteros distinction or equivalence) as settled, and over-read later institutional structures back into first-century texts. Every claim about what a tradition teaches regarding its own polity is verify-required against that tradition's own sources. The model describes and attributes — it does not endorse, rank, or adjudicate.

**Objective:** Examine the New Testament material relevant to church government and leadership structure, then present how major polity systems claim biblical warrant for their structure — each system's strongest biblical case, its hermeneutical logic, and the strongest objections it faces — so the user can evaluate the biblical grounding of each polity with full awareness of the interpretive landscape.

**When to use:**
- You want to understand the NT material on church offices and governance and how different traditions read it.
- You are studying ecclesiology and need to see why Christians organize churches so differently despite reading the same texts.
- You are evaluating your own tradition's polity against the NT material and want to see the strongest case for alternatives.
- You are teaching on church leadership and need to present the landscape fairly.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want a broad comparison of traditions on a practice (not focused on polity) — use `biblical_learner_compare_traditions_on_practice.md`.
- You want to study a single doctrine across traditions — use `biblical_doctrine_study_neutral.md`.
- You want to compare interpretive positions on a specific disputed passage — use `biblical_interpretive_views_comparison.md`.
- You want to argue that one polity is correct — this prompt maps the biblical material and presents each system's case, it does not rule.

**Audience:** Pastor (P), academic (A).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Scope.** Full polity survey (all major systems) or focused on specific systems (e.g., "episcopal vs. presbyterian" or "congregational vs. elder-led").
2. **Specific question (optional).** If the user has a specific question within polity (e.g., "is episkopos the same office as presbyteros in the NT?" or "does the NT prescribe a single governance model?"), state it.
3. **Traditions of interest (optional).** Which traditions to foreground. If omitted, cover the major polity families.
4. **Declared tradition (optional).** The user's own tradition — may be foregrounded but does not suppress other readings.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Begin with the NT material itself before mapping it to polity systems: identify the relevant terms, texts, and patterns in their original literary and historical context.
- Acknowledge the disputed status of key terms: episkopos, presbyteros, diakonos, poimen, apostolos, prophetes — note where scholars and traditions disagree on whether these denote distinct offices, overlapping functions, or contextual roles.
- For each polity system: state its structure, identify the NT texts it cites (by address), explain the hermeneutical logic that connects text to polity, and present the strongest objection it faces from other systems.
- Note where the NT material is genuinely ambiguous, developmental, or varied across communities — and how different traditions handle that variation.
- Present each polity system as its own advocates would describe it — not as outsiders characterize it.

### Must Not
- Assert that one polity system is "the NT model" or "what the early church practiced" — the relationship between NT texts and later polity structures is exactly what traditions disagree about.
- Present the episkopos/presbyteros question as settled in either direction — this is one of the most contested points and must be presented as such.
- Over-read later institutional structures back into first-century texts (e.g., reading a 4th-century episcopate into the Pastoral Epistles, or reading a 17th-century congregational vote into Acts 6).
- Fabricate what denominations teach about their own governance, invent denominational constitutions or confessional statements, or attribute polity positions to traditions that do not hold them.
- Collapse the diversity of NT evidence into a single model or exaggerate diversity to make all models equally unsupported.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present each polity system's biblical case with equal depth, charity, and seriousness. Episcopal, presbyterian, congregational, connexional, and other systems all receive proportional treatment.
- **Must Not:** use language that privileges one system (e.g., "the historic structure" vs. "the later innovation" — all are "structures claimed by [tradition] on the basis of [texts]"). Do not treat any polity as the default from which others are departures.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Confirm scope and focus
Restate the scope (full survey or focused comparison), any specific question, which traditions will be covered, and any declared tradition.

### Step 2 — Survey the NT material
Before mapping to polity systems, lay out the NT material on its own terms:
- **Key terms:** episkopos, presbyteros, diakonos, poimen, apostolos, prophetes, and any other governance-relevant terms. For each: range of usage in NT, texts where it appears (by address), and what is and is not clear about the term's referent.
- **Key texts:** Passages that address church leadership, authority, appointment, or structure (by address). Note genre (narrative, instruction, occasional letter) and what each text does and does not specify.
- **Patterns and gaps:** What patterns emerge across the NT evidence? Where is the NT genuinely silent or ambiguous (e.g., how were leaders appointed? what was the relationship between local and trans-local authority? was there a single model or regional variation?)?

### Step 3 — Map polity systems to NT material
For each major polity system:
- **Structure:** Describe the system in 3-4 sentences as its proponents would state it.
- **NT texts cited:** Which texts does this system claim as its biblical warrant (by address)?
- **Hermeneutical logic:** What interpretive move connects the NT text to this governance structure? (e.g., prescriptive reading, pattern-following, theological inference, development of seed principles).
- **Handling of counter-evidence:** How does this system respond to the NT texts and patterns cited by other systems?
- **Strongest objection:** What is the single strongest objection this system faces from the NT material or from other systems' reading of it?

### Step 4 — The contested questions
Identify the specific questions that the polity debate turns on:
- Is episkopos the same office as presbyteros in the NT, or are they distinct?
- Does the NT prescribe a single governance model, describe diverse local adaptations, or provide principles without a fixed structure?
- What role does post-apostolic development play in legitimating a polity?
- How does each system handle the gap between NT descriptions and its own current practice?

### Step 5 — Common ground and honest assessment
Summarize:
- Where polity systems agree on what the NT says (even if they draw different structural conclusions).
- Where the real disagreements lie — and whether they are driven by different texts, different hermeneutics, or different ecclesiological commitments.
- What a user would need to investigate further (historical, exegetical, theological) to form their own conviction on church governance.

## Verification

- [ ] Key terms are presented with their contested aspects, not as settled.
- [ ] Each polity system's biblical case is presented as its own proponents would state it.
- [ ] Counter-evidence handling and strongest objection are included for each system.
- [ ] No polity is asserted as "the NT model" — all are presented as claims by identifiable traditions.
- [ ] No fabricated denominational positions, confessional statements, or tradition attributions.
- [ ] Gaps and ambiguities in the NT material are honestly identified, not papered over.
- [ ] Language is evenly charitable across all polity systems.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Assert that one polity is "the NT model" — present each as a claim grounded in biblical reasoning by an identifiable tradition.
- Present the episkopos/presbyteros question as settled — it is one of the most contested points in NT ecclesiology.
- Over-read later institutional structures back into first-century texts.

DO:
- Survey the NT material on its own terms before mapping it to polity systems.
- Identify what the NT specifies and what it does not — gaps and ambiguities are part of the honest picture.
- Flag all historical claims, tradition attributions, and term definitions as verify-required.
