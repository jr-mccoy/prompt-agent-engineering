# How Jewish Tradition Reads a Hebrew Bible Text

**Objective:** For one Hebrew Bible text the user names, lay out how Jewish tradition reads it — in its own terms, through identifiable reading streams, with its internal disagreements intact — and, only if asked, set Christian readings of the same text beside it as a separate, parallel map.

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt — never invent Jewish sources.** This prompt **never** quotes or paraphrases midrash, Talmud, Mishnah, Targum, or a medieval or modern commentary from memory, and **never** supplies a tractate and folio, a midrash collection and section, a Targum verse rendering, or a commentary location the user did not supply. Where such a source bears on the text, it leaves a slot naming the *kind* of source to check — e.g. `[VERIFY: locate in a midrash collection on this book]`, `[VERIFY: locate the commentator's remark at this verse in a rabbinic Bible or translated edition]`. Well-known figures (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ramban, Radak and others) may be **named as people**; what any of them says at a given verse is always `[VERIFY]`. It never invents rabbis, scholars, dates, books, Dead Sea Scroll sigla, Josephus or Philo references, or archaeological claims, and never quotes Scripture wording from memory — verses are cited by address and the user supplies the translation (a named Jewish translation, a Christian one, or both).

> **No supersessionist framing.** Jewish reading is presented as a living interpretive tradition with its own logic — not a "pre-Christian stage", not "blind" to a meaning Christians see, not a foil, not "Old Testament religion". Christian theologies of Israel may be **described and attributed** where the user's question requires it; they are never the frame and never presented as what Jews believe. Where the stakes are real (teaching, publication, an actual dialogue), check the result with a rabbi, Jewish dialogue partner, or Jewish-studies scholar.

**When to use:**
- You are preparing to teach or preach a Hebrew Bible text and want to know how Jewish readers have read it, not only Christian ones.
- You have a Jewish–Christian study group, joint event, or conversation partner and want an honest map of both communities' readings of one text.
- You are writing an academic paper and need a structured plan of which Jewish sources to locate and read.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are preparing for dialogue with any religion on a topic (salvation, prayer, ethics) rather than reading one text — use `domain-biblical-studies/apologetics-engagement/biblical_apologetics_other_religions_dialogue.md`. This prompt is distinct: one text, Jewish reading streams in depth.
- You want the competing *Christian* (and critical) interpretations of a contested verse — use `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/biblical_multiview_interpretation_map.md`. This prompt is distinct: Jewish tradition is the primary subject, not one view among a list.
- Your question is where the book sits in the Tanakh versus Christian canons, or why chapter and verse numbers differ — use `domain-biblical-studies/original-languages/biblical_language_canon_versification_differences.md`. This prompt only flags such differences and routes there.
- You need the first-century Jewish setting of a *New Testament* passage — use `domain-biblical-studies/jewish-christian-dialogue/biblical_dialogue_second_temple_context.md`.

**Audience:** Pastors and preachers (P); seminary and academic researchers (A); self-directed learners (S).

## Inputs / Context

1. **The text** by address (book, chapter, verses), in the user's numbering — and which numbering that is (Hebrew or an English edition).
2. **Translation(s)** the user will read from: ideally a named Jewish translation and the user's usual one. The user pastes wording if any phrase-level discussion is wanted.
3. **Any sources already in hand** — a commentary, a rabbinic anthology, a Jewish study Bible, a synagogue handout — with page or location as the user has it. These are echoed as **USER-SUPPLIED** and still verify-required.
4. **Purpose:** personal understanding, teaching, a joint study, a paper, or dialogue preparation.
5. **Christian comparison wanted?** Yes / no. If yes, which Christian streams matter to the user (optional declared tradition).
6. **Jewish context of the user's partner or setting**, if any (e.g. a Reform congregation, an Orthodox study partner) — to foreground, never to treat as the whole.

## Constraints

### Must
- Use Jewish terminology for Jewish reading: Tanakh (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim), Torah, parashah, haftarah, midrash, peshat, derash, machloket, and names for texts as Jewish tradition uses them (e.g. the Akedah for Genesis 22).
- Map reading streams separately: plain-sense (peshat) and homiletical/expansive (derash) **as categories**; classical rabbinic literature; medieval commentators; Targumim; liturgical and calendar use; Jewish philosophical and mystical streams; modern movements (Orthodox, Conservative/Masorti, Reform, Reconstructionist, and others the user names); modern academic Jewish studies.
- Show **internal Jewish disagreement** — rabbinic literature preserves debate and multiple readings side by side; medieval commentators argue with each other; modern movements differ. Never collapse these into "the Jewish view".
- Tag every source-dependent claim with provenance: **USER-SUPPLIED**, **GENERAL** (category-level description safe to state, e.g. "rabbinic literature often reads by juxtaposing verses"), or **[VERIFY: …]** naming the kind of source to check.
- Flag canon-order and versification differences that affect the text and route detail to the canon/versification prompt.
- Keep the Christian comparison (if requested) in a **separate section**, side by side, using convergence/divergence language only.
- End with questions to ask a Jewish dialogue partner.

### Must Not
- Quote, paraphrase, or summarise a specific midrash, Talmudic passage, Targum rendering, or commentator's remark from memory.
- Supply a tractate, folio, midrash section, or commentary location the user did not give.
- Invent rabbis, scholars, books, dates, sigla, or historical claims; or state what "most Jews believe".
- Quote Scripture wording from memory.
- Describe Jewish reading as preliminary, partial, veiled, or superseded; or call the text "Old Testament" when describing Jewish reading of it.
- Present a Christian theology of Israel, or a Messianic Jewish reading, as a Jewish reading.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** attribute positions within Judaism to the stream or movement that holds them, and positions within Christianity to identifiable Christian streams; show both communities' internal plurality.
- **Must:** describe Christian theologies of Israel (supersessionist variants, post-supersessionist, two-covenant, Messianic Jewish, and others) only when the question needs them, and attribute each as a Christian position.
- **Must Not:** adjudicate between Jewish streams, between Christian streams, or between the two communities; rank one reading as "more faithful to the text".

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Fix the text
Confirm the address, the numbering system, and the translation(s). Note the book's place in the Tanakh (Torah, Nevi'im, or Ketuvim) and flag any known canon-order or versification issue as `[VERIFY: compare Hebrew and English numbering — see canon/versification prompt]`. Echo user-supplied sources as **USER-SUPPLIED**.

### Step 2 — Plain sense and homiletical reading, as categories
Explain what a peshat question about this text would ask (grammar, context, narrative logic) and what a derash approach would ask (gaps, juxtapositions, repetitions, ethical and theological expansion). Pose these as the questions the traditions ask, not as answers attributed to anyone.

### Step 3 — Classical rabbinic literature
Describe, at category level, the kinds of rabbinic material that typically engage a text of this kind (halakhic, aggadic, midrash collections on the book, Talmudic discussion). For each likely line of reading, leave `[VERIFY: locate in …]` naming the type of collection. Note that rabbinic sources often preserve several readings and dissent (machloket) rather than one answer.

### Step 4 — Targumim
State whether a Targum tradition covers this book (`[VERIFY: which Targum covers this book]`) and what a reader should compare — where an Aramaic rendering expands, interprets, or softens. Never supply the rendering.

### Step 5 — Medieval commentators
List the commentators a reader would typically consult for this book (named as people), with the kind of question each is known for engaging — and `[VERIFY: read the remark at this verse in a rabbinic Bible or translated edition]` for every content claim. Show where they are known to disagree only if the user supplies the remarks.

### Step 6 — Liturgy and calendar
Note whether and where the text is read or recited (weekly Torah portion, haftarah, festival reading, daily or festival liturgy) — every item `[VERIFY: check a Jewish calendar, Chumash, siddur, or machzor]`. Explain why liturgical setting shapes how a community hears a text.

### Step 7 — Philosophical, mystical, and modern streams
Sketch, at category level, how philosophical and mystical streams and the modern movements tend to approach a text of this kind, attributing each and noting disagreement between movements. Leave academic Jewish scholarship as a slot: `[VERIFY: locate a Jewish study Bible or academic Jewish commentary on this book]`.

### Step 8 — Christian readings, side by side (only if requested)
In a separate section, list Christian readings by stream (e.g. typological, historical-critical, liturgical) and set each against the Jewish streams with **convergence / divergence** notes. Neither column completes or corrects the other. If the user's question touches a Christian theology of Israel, describe and attribute it as Christian.

### Step 9 — Questions for a Jewish dialogue partner
Write 4–6 open questions that ask the partner how *their* community reads and lives with this text — never questions designed to lead toward a Christian conclusion.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Write "the Talmud says…" with a folio that sounds right — a confident citation is the most damaging fabrication here.
- Summarise "Rashi's comment" on a verse from memory; name Rashi as a commentator to consult and slot the content.
- Present Jewish reading as a set of "insights" that culminate in the Christian reading.
- Treat Reform, Orthodox, Conservative/Masorti, and Reconstructionist readings as interchangeable.

✅ **DO:**
- Prefer a clearly labelled gap over a plausible-looking source.
- Describe how rabbinic literature holds multiple readings together as a feature of the tradition, not a defect.
- Let the Christian column stand beside the Jewish one, with its own internal variety.

## Techniques Used

- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** Every rabbinic, Targum, liturgical, or commentary claim is either user-supplied or a `[VERIFY: locate in …]` slot naming the kind of source.
- **RT-23 (Input Provenance Tagging):** Each line carries USER-SUPPLIED, GENERAL, or VERIFY so the reader sees what rests on what.
- **DP-04 (Must-Not Constraints):** Explicit prohibitions on folio-style citations, remembered commentary, and supersessionist framing.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** Category-level descriptions are labelled as such; the map recommends checking with a Jewish partner or scholar.
- **NE-16 (Non-Judgmental Comparison):** Jewish and Christian readings are set side by side with convergence/divergence language, neither completing the other.
