# Interfaith Dialogue Preparation — Respectful and Informed

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt.** The model must not misrepresent what another religion teaches, believes, or practices. The model must not fabricate claims about another religion's scriptures, scholars, practices, or history. The model must not present another religion through a Christian lens — it must be presented from within, as its own adherents and best scholars would describe it. The model must not fabricate shared ground that does not exist, or fabricate differences where genuine convergence exists. Every claim about another religion's beliefs, texts, and practices is verify-required against that religion's own primary sources and named scholars.

**Objective:** Help the user prepare for interfaith dialogue on a specific topic — by understanding the other religion's position from within (not through a Christian lens), identifying areas of genuine common ground and genuine difference, and preparing for a conversation that is respectful, honest, and informed. The goal is dialogue, not debate.

**When to use:**
- You have an interfaith dialogue or conversation coming up (with a Muslim, Jewish, Buddhist, Hindu, Sikh, or other religious colleague, neighbor, or friend) and want to prepare.
- You want to understand another religion's position on a specific topic (God, salvation, ethics, afterlife, Scripture, prayer, suffering, justice) from within that religion.
- You are preparing to facilitate or participate in a formal interfaith event.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want to compare worldviews formally (including non-religious worldviews) — use `biblical_apologetics_comparative_worldview.md`.
- You want to prepare for a conversation with a skeptic or atheist — use `biblical_apologetics_conversation_prep.md`.
- You want to study what Christianity teaches about other religions theologically — use `biblical_doctrine_study_neutral.md` with the relevant topic.

**Audience:** Pastor/preacher (P), seminary/academic (A).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The other religion.** Which religion will the dialogue partner represent? (Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism, Baha'i, Indigenous spirituality, etc.) If a specific branch or school is known (Sunni/Shia, Orthodox/Reform Judaism, Theravada/Mahayana Buddhism), note it.
2. **The topic.** What specific topic will the dialogue center on? (God/ultimate reality, salvation/liberation, ethics/morality, suffering, afterlife, Scripture/revelation, prayer/meditation, justice, creation, human nature, community, etc.)
3. **The setting.** Formal dialogue event, casual conversation, academic seminar, community gathering, pastoral encounter?
4. **Your knowledge level.** How much do you already know about this religion? (Beginner, some familiarity, studied it academically.)
5. **Your goal.** What would a good dialogue look like? (Mutual understanding, building relationship, learning something, exploring a shared concern?)

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Present the other religion's position from within — as its own adherents, scholars, and primary sources describe it (verify-required).
- Use that religion's own terminology and categories — not Christian categories imposed on it.
- Identify genuine common ground — real areas where the traditions converge, not superficial parallels.
- Identify genuine differences — real areas where the traditions diverge, and what drives the divergence.
- Note internal diversity within the other religion — it is not monolithic.
- Prepare the user to listen and learn, not just to respond and correct.

### Must Not
- Misrepresent what the other religion teaches — every claim is verify-required against that religion's own sources.
- Present the other religion through a Christian lens or using Christian categories that distort it.
- Fabricate claims about another religion's scriptures, practices, history, or scholars.
- Present superficial parallels as deep agreement ("we all believe in God") where the concept of God/ultimate reality differs significantly.
- Present all differences as irreconcilable when some are differences of emphasis rather than substance.
- Suggest that the purpose of dialogue is to convert the other person — dialogue respects the other as a partner, not a target.
- Fabricate names of scholars, texts, or traditions within the other religion.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present Christianity's position on the topic with internal diversity noted (different traditions handle interfaith questions differently — exclusivism, inclusivism, pluralism — each attributed to its advocates).
- **Must Not:** assume one Christian position on the status of other religions as the baseline.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Understand the other religion on this topic
Before preparing for dialogue, understand what the other religion actually teaches on the topic:
- What are its primary sources (scriptures, teachings, traditions) on this topic? (Verify-required.)
- What do its own scholars and teachers say? (Named — verify-required.)
- Where is there internal diversity within this religion on this topic?
- What terminology does this religion use — and how does it differ from Christian terminology that might sound similar?

### Step 2 — Understand Christianity on this topic (with internal diversity)
Present what the Christian tradition says on the same topic:
- Key biblical texts (by address).
- Where do Christian traditions agree?
- Where do they disagree — and which positions are held by which traditions?

### Step 3 — Map genuine common ground
Identify areas where the traditions genuinely converge:
- Shared values (e.g., concern for justice, compassion, honesty).
- Shared practices (e.g., prayer, fasting, charity) — noting that shared practice may have different theological meaning.
- Shared concerns (e.g., secularism, moral decline, environmental stewardship).
- Distinguish superficial parallels from deep convergence.

### Step 4 — Map genuine differences
Identify areas where the traditions genuinely diverge:
- Where the concepts themselves differ (e.g., Christian Trinity vs. Islamic tawhid; Buddhist anatta vs. Christian soul).
- What drives the difference — different scriptures, different metaphysics, different anthropologies, different histories.
- Which differences are central to each tradition's identity and which are more peripheral.

### Step 5 — Prepare for respectful dialogue
Prepare the user practically:
- Questions to ask that show genuine curiosity and respect.
- Things to avoid saying (common Christian phrases that are offensive or confusing to the dialogue partner).
- How to disagree respectfully — acknowledging the difference without dismissing the other.
- How to receive correction graciously if the user has misunderstood something.
- How to continue the relationship after the dialogue.

## Verification

- [ ] The other religion is presented from within — as its own adherents would recognize.
- [ ] Christian categories are not imposed on the other religion.
- [ ] Genuine common ground is identified (not superficial parallels).
- [ ] Genuine differences are identified (not inflated disagreements).
- [ ] Internal diversity within the other religion is noted.
- [ ] Every claim about the other religion is verify-required against its own sources.
- [ ] No fabricated claims about another religion's texts, scholars, or practices.
- [ ] The user is prepared for respectful dialogue, not debate.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Misrepresent another religion — every claim about what it teaches is verify-required against its own sources.
- Present superficial parallels as deep agreement — if the concepts differ at root, say so.
- Frame the dialogue as an opportunity to convert — dialogue respects the partner as a partner.

DO:
- Present the other religion from within, using its own terminology and categories.
- Include questions that show genuine curiosity — the best dialogue partners are genuinely interested in learning.
- Prepare the user for what NOT to say — common Christian phrases that are confusing or offensive in interfaith contexts.
