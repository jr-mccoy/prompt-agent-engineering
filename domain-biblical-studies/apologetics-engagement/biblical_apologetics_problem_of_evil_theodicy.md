# Problem of Evil & Theodicy — Major Positions Presented Fairly

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt.** The model must not fabricate philosophical arguments or attribute positions to philosophers/theologians who never held them. The model must not misrepresent the logical or evidential problem of evil as stated by its best advocates. The model must not invent historical examples of suffering or theological responses to them. The model must not fabricate citations, publications, or scholarly attributions. Every philosophical argument, historical claim, and thinker attribution is verify-required. The problem of evil involves real human suffering — the model does not treat it as an abstract puzzle to solve.

**Objective:** Present the major theodicies and the strongest objections to each, fairly and proportionally, so the user sees the full philosophical and pastoral landscape. This includes both the intellectual problem (logical, evidential) and the existential/pastoral dimension (what does a person in suffering actually need?). The model does not declare a winner.

**When to use:**
- You are preparing to engage with the problem of evil in a teaching, preaching, counseling, or academic context.
- Someone has raised the problem of evil as an objection and you want to understand the full range of responses before formulating your own.
- You want to see the theodicy landscape honestly — including where each theodicy has genuine limitations.

**When NOT to use:**
- Someone is in acute suffering and needs pastoral care — this is a philosophical/theological survey, not a crisis resource. Route to pastoral care.
- You want to engage with a different specific objection — use `biblical_apologetics_objection_engagement.md`.
- You want to study what the Bible says about suffering as a doctrinal topic — use `biblical_doctrine_study_neutral.md`.

**Audience:** Pastor/preacher (P), seminary/academic (A).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Form of the problem.** Which form of the problem of evil? The logical problem (is God's existence logically compatible with evil?), the evidential problem (does the amount/distribution of evil make God's existence unlikely?), the existential problem (how do I live faithfully in a world with this much suffering?), or all three.
2. **Specific focus (optional).** Is there a specific type of suffering in view? (Natural evil, moral evil, animal suffering, innocent suffering, gratuitous suffering, systemic injustice, etc.)
3. **Depth.** Survey (major positions mapped) or deep (philosophical arguments traced with named advocates).
4. **Context (optional).** Academic paper, sermon, pastoral conversation, personal study?

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Present the problem of evil at its strongest — as its best philosophical advocates have formulated it (named thinkers, verify-required).
- Present each major theodicy attributed to identifiable thinkers and traditions.
- Present the strongest objection to each theodicy — including objections from other Christian theologians, not just atheist critics.
- Include the pastoral/existential dimension — theodicies that work on paper may fail in the hospital room, and that matters.
- Acknowledge that the problem of evil involves real human suffering — never treat it as merely an intellectual puzzle.
- Flag every philosophical argument and thinker attribution as verify-required.

### Must Not
- Fabricate philosophical arguments or attribute positions to thinkers who never held them.
- Present one theodicy as having "solved" the problem — present the landscape.
- Minimize the force of the problem — the problem of evil is genuinely challenging, and honest engagement requires acknowledging this.
- Treat suffering abstractly when the user's context involves real pastoral situations.
- Fabricate examples of suffering to illustrate points — use well-known, verifiable examples only.
- Fabricate citations, book titles, or scholarly attributions.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present theodicies from Augustinian, Irenaean, Reformed, Open Theist, Catholic, Orthodox, liberation theology, and other traditions, each attributed to its tradition.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's theodicy as THE Christian answer to suffering.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — State the problem clearly
Present the problem of evil in its relevant form(s):
- **Logical problem** — the deductive argument (as formulated by whom? — verify-required).
- **Evidential problem** — the probabilistic argument (as formulated by whom? — verify-required).
- **Existential problem** — the lived experience of suffering and its challenge to faith.

### Step 2 — Map the major theodicies
Present each major theodicy, attributed to named thinkers/traditions:
- **Free will theodicy** — evil results from the misuse of genuine freedom.
- **Soul-making theodicy** — suffering enables moral and spiritual growth.
- **Greater good theodicy** — evil is permitted because it serves a greater good.
- **Skeptical theism** — we lack the cognitive capacity to assess God's reasons.
- **Protest theodicy** — the faithful response is lament and protest, not explanation.
- **Christological/cruciform theodicy** — God enters suffering through the cross.
- **Eschatological theodicy** — final redemption recontextualizes present suffering.
- **Others relevant to the user's question** (Open Theist, process theology, liberation theology).

For each: core argument, key advocate(s) (verify-required), strongest objection.

### Step 3 — Note the pastoral dimension
For each theodicy, note:
- Does this work pastorally — in the hospital room, at the graveside, with the abuse survivor?
- Which theodicies are better suited to the classroom and which to the bedside?
- Where does the Book of Job's own approach (refusing neat answers) challenge all theodicies?

### Step 4 — Present the strongest counter-arguments
Present the strongest atheist/skeptic counter-arguments to theodicy in general:
- Who has formulated them most rigorously? (Verify-required.)
- What do these counter-arguments target — a specific theodicy or the entire enterprise?

### Step 5 — Summarize the landscape
- Where is there convergence among Christian thinkers?
- Where are the genuinely unresolved tensions?
- What would the user need to investigate to form their own position?

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Fabricate philosophical positions or attribute arguments to thinkers who never made them.
- Minimize the force of the problem of evil — it is one of the most challenging questions in philosophy of religion.
- Treat suffering abstractly when pastoral context is present.

DO:
- Present the problem at its strongest before presenting responses.
- Include the pastoral dimension — not every good philosophical answer is a good pastoral answer.
- Acknowledge where genuine tensions remain unresolved — honesty about difficulty is more trustworthy than false confidence.
