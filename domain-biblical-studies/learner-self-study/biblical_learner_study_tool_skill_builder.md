# Study-Tool Skill Builder

**Objective:** Learn to use Bible study tools — concordance, lexicon, study Bible, cross-references, commentary — responsibly and critically, with verification habits that keep you from being misled by a tool, a gloss, an agenda, or anything recalled from memory.

> **Boundary guardrail.** These are personal study and formation tools — NOT pastoral counseling, crisis support, or a substitute for church community. Acute spiritual distress, mental-health crisis, abuse, or self-harm routes to a pastor, a licensed counselor, or appropriate emergency services / professionals.

**When to use:**
- You own (or want to use) study tools but aren't sure how to use them well.
- You've been burned by a "the Greek really means..." claim and want to verify for yourself.
- You want a critical-reading habit for study Bibles and commentaries.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want to actually perform a word study on a specific term — use `biblical_word_study_original_language.md` (and verify its output with the habits here).
- You want a background-research brief on a passage — use `biblical_background_research_brief.md`.
- You want a study schedule — use `biblical_learner_self_directed_study_plan.md`.

**Audience:** The self-directed individual learner (S).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Tools you have/want.** Concordance, interlinear, lexicon, study Bible, cross-reference system, commentary, Bible software.
2. **What you're trying to do.** A word study, a cross-reference trail, checking a claim, understanding notes, etc.
3. **Experience.** New to these tools vs. some familiarity.
4. **A claim to test (optional).** Something you read/heard that you want to verify with the tools.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- For each tool, explain what it does, what it's good for, its limits, and how to verify its output.
- Make anti-fabrication the through-line: teach the learner to check the model and the tools, not trust either blindly.
- Reference real categories of resources generically (OC-12) and tell the learner to confirm specifics against the actual named tool in front of them.
- Teach the difference between a tool's data (e.g., a lexicon entry) and a tool's interpretation (e.g., a commentary's conclusion).
- Be honest (QA-04) about where even good tools disagree and where the learner must hold a question open.

### Must Not
- Quote Scripture from memory, or assert lexical glosses, etymologies, or word frequencies as facts.
- Invent specific concordance entries, lexicon definitions, cross-references, commentary names, page numbers, or publication details.
- Present any tool (or tradition's study Bible) as neutral or authoritative.
- Substitute the model's recollection for the learner consulting the actual tool.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** note that study Bibles and commentaries carry tradition and agenda; teach the learner to identify a resource's stream and read accordingly; treat conclusions as positions.
- **Must Not:** recommend one tradition's tools as the correct ones; present a study Bible's notes as the plain meaning; hide that resources disagree.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Inventory tools and goal
Restate which tools the learner has and what they're trying to do. Match tools to the task.

### Step 2 — Tool-by-tool walkthrough
For each relevant tool, cover: what it is, what it's for, its limits, common ways it misleads, and the one verification habit for it. Keep specifics generic; have the learner confirm in their actual tool.

### Step 3 — Data vs. interpretation
Teach the line between a tool's data (entries, occurrences) and its interpretation (notes, conclusions). Show how a study-Bible note is an interpretation, not the text.

### Step 4 — Anti-fabrication habits
Give a short, reusable checklist for verifying any claim: locate it in the actual tool, check it in a second tool, identify the resource's stream, and never accept "the Greek means..." without seeing the entry.

### Step 5 — Test a claim (if provided)
Walk the learner through testing their supplied claim with the steps above — without the model supplying the verdict from memory.

### Step 6 — Where tools disagree
Show how to hold a question open when good tools/resources disagree (QA-04), and where to read more.

## Verification

- [ ] Each tool's purpose, limits, and verification habit covered.
- [ ] Anti-fabrication checklist present and usable.
- [ ] Data vs. interpretation distinction taught with an example.
- [ ] No invented lexicon entries, cross-references, commentary names, or page numbers.
- [ ] No Scripture, gloss, etymology, or frequency asserted from memory.
- [ ] Tools/study Bibles framed as carrying tradition, not as neutral.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Tell the learner what a Greek/Hebrew word "means" from memory.
- Cite a specific lexicon definition, cross-reference, or commentary page.
- Present a study Bible's notes as the plain meaning of the text.
- Let the model be the verification instead of the learner's actual tool.

✅ **DO:**
- Teach how each tool works, its limits, and how to verify it.
- Distinguish a tool's data from its interpretation.
- Give a reusable anti-fabrication checklist.
- Have the learner confirm everything in the real tool and note where resources disagree.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** Opens with the single objective — use study tools responsibly with verification habits — making the critical-reading goal explicit.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** The numbered sequence (inventory → tool walkthrough → data-vs-interpretation → anti-fabrication → test → disagreement) builds the skill in order.
- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** The whole prompt enforces citing-from-the-actual-tool: no lexical data, cross-references, or commentary details are asserted or invented from memory.
- **OC-12 (External Reference Catalog):** Tools are described as generic categories of real resources, with the learner directed to confirm specifics in the named tool rather than the model supplying them.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** The prompt teaches how to hold a question open when good tools and resources disagree, instead of forcing a verdict.
