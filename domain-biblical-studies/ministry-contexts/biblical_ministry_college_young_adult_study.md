# College / Young Adult Study Designer

**Objective:** Design a Bible study for college students or young adults (18-30) that pairs genuine textual depth with honest engagement of the questions this life stage actually raises — identity formation, vocational discernment, doubt and deconstruction, relationships, justice, suffering, and how childhood faith either grows up or gets left behind.

**When to use:**
- You are leading a college ministry, campus group, or young-adult small group.
- The study needs to take hard questions seriously — doubt, deconstruction, "why should I believe this?"
- You want depth that respects young adults' intelligence without assuming seminary-level knowledge.

**When NOT to use:**
- Your group is teens/minors — use `biblical_ministry_youth_bible_study_designer.md`.
- Your audience is exploring Christianity for the first time — use `biblical_ministry_seeker_intro_to_bible.md`.
- You only need discussion questions for an existing study — use `biblical_smallgroup_discussion_guide.md`.

**Audience:** Ministry-context teachers (M) and group leaders (G) — campus ministers, young-adult pastors, small-group leaders, retreat planners.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The passage or theme.** Scripture reference plus the text in a named translation (pasted by the user).
2. **Group profile.** College vs. post-college young adults. Spiritual background: churched, dechurched, mixed, or mostly explorers. Approximate size and how long the group has been meeting.
3. **Life-stage emphasis (optional).** If the leader wants to foreground a specific young-adult concern (vocation, doubt, relationships, identity, justice, suffering), state it. Otherwise the model reads the passage and connects naturally.
4. **Format.** Single session, multi-week series, or retreat. Session length.
5. **Tradition (optional).** If the group belongs to a specific tradition, the model shifts emphasis but preserves alternative readings.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Take the text seriously — the study is exegetically grounded, not a life-lesson with a verse attached.
- Welcome doubt and hard questions as part of the study, not as problems to fix. Deconstruction is a real experience; the study creates space for it without either dismissing it or treating it as the goal.
- Address young-adult concerns (identity, vocation, doubt, relationships, justice) as they genuinely arise from the text, not as forced topical overlays.
- Present contested readings (on sexuality, gender, atonement, hell, science/faith, political engagement, etc.) with multi-view honesty and tradition attribution.
- Include application that respects the diversity of young-adult life situations (single, dating, married; employed, job-seeking, in school; various living situations and economic realities).

### Must Not
- Patronize ("these young people just need to...") or moralize without textual warrant.
- Assume participants have or should have "settled" faith positions — many are forming them for the first time.
- Treat doubt as rebellion or deconstruction as inherently hostile to faith.
- Fabricate commentary claims, scholar attributions, cultural-background assertions, or original-language data.
- Force "practical application" that doesn't arise from the text (e.g., adding a dating lesson to a passage about discipleship).
- Assume uniform political, economic, or social backgrounds within the group.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** when the passage touches contested ground (atonement theories, sexuality, gender, science/faith, social justice, hell/afterlife, political engagement), present the range of faithful readings with attribution.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's reading as "the biblical answer" — young adults are forming their own positions; the study equips rather than decides.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Confirm passage, group, and approach
Restate the passage, group profile, any life-stage emphasis, format, and tradition. Flag if the passage touches contested ground frequently raised by young adults.

### Step 2 — Exegetical foundation
Brief exegetical scan: genre, context in its book, key interpretive questions, and how the passage has been read across traditions. Identify where the passage naturally connects to young-adult concerns without forcing the connection. All claims verify-required against the user's supplied text.

### Step 3 — Design the study
Build the study session(s):
- **Opening:** an entry point that meets young adults where they are — an honest question, a real tension, or an observation that earns the right to go deeper. Not a gimmick.
- **Text engagement:** guided reading that teaches young adults *how* to read the Bible (observation, context, interpretation) as well as *what* the passage says. Many in this age group are learning to read Scripture for themselves for the first time.
- **Discussion questions:** tiered:
  - *Observation* — what does the text say?
  - *Interpretation* — what does it mean, and where do interpreters disagree?
  - *Honest engagement* — where do I push back, and is that pushback fair?
  - *Application* — what does this ask of me, and do I want to do it?
- **Leader notes:** where participants may challenge the text or each other; how to facilitate without shutting down or losing the thread.

### Step 4 — Handle doubt and deconstruction honestly
If the passage or theme intersects with common deconstruction triggers (authority of Scripture, suffering, exclusivism, sexuality, science, institutional failures):
- Name the trigger honestly.
- Present the strongest version of the objection (steelman it).
- Present the strongest version of the response.
- Let the group sit with the tension — do not force resolution in one session.

### Step 5 — Multi-session arc (if series)
If the study is a multi-week series, map the arc: how sessions build, what the series moves toward, and how to sustain engagement with young adults' erratic attendance patterns.

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Treat doubt as rebellion or deconstruction as the enemy.
- Force a life-stage topic onto a passage that doesn't raise it.
- Assume young adults need simpler content — they need honest content.

DO:
- Steelman objections before responding to them — young adults can tell when you haven't.
- Teach how to read the Bible, not just what a passage says — many are learning for the first time.
- Let tension sit. Not every hard question resolves in one session. That's fine.
