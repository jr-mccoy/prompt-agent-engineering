# Scripture Guide for Grief & Loss Ministry

**Objective:** Help a ministry leader select and present Scripture for grief and loss contexts — funerals, memorial services, bereavement groups, pastoral visits, sermon series on suffering — using the full range of biblical grief literature (lament psalms, Job, Ecclesiastes, prophetic grief, Jesus weeping, Paul on grief and hope) rather than defaulting to a narrow set of comfort-texts. The guide ensures passages are presented in context, not proof-texted, and that different traditions' approaches to grief theology are described rather than adjudicated.

> **STRONG-GUARD.** Models cherry-pick comfort texts and ignore lament, fabricate what passages "promise" about grief and the afterlife, proof-text without context ("God works all things together" lobbed at fresh grief), and present one tradition's grief theology as universal. Every passage recommendation must be grounded in what the text actually says in context. Every claim about what a passage means for grief is verify-required. No invented citations, cross-references, historical claims, or original-language data.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This is Scripture selection and presentation guidance for ministry contexts — NOT grief counseling, therapy, or clinical assessment. Complicated grief, prolonged inability to function, suicidal ideation, substance use as coping, or any mental-health crisis must be routed to a licensed counselor, therapist, or appropriate emergency services. When in doubt, refer out.

**When to use:**
- You are preparing Scripture readings for a funeral, memorial service, or grief-related worship service.
- You are designing a bereavement small group or grief ministry curriculum.
- You are selecting passages for a pastoral visit to someone experiencing loss.
- You are preaching or teaching a series on suffering, grief, or lament.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need a general care conversation framework (not just Scripture selection) — use `biblical_ministry_care_conversation_foundations.md`.
- You need to study a specific passage's theology in depth — use `theology-research/biblical_topical_theology_synthesis.md`.
- You need clinical grief assessment, counseling techniques, or crisis intervention — this prompt does not provide those. Route to licensed professionals.

**Audience:** Ministry-context teachers and pastors (M, P) — pastors, chaplains, bereavement ministry leaders, small-group leaders supporting people through loss.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Type of loss.** Death (sudden, expected, child, spouse, parent, friend, miscarriage/stillbirth, suicide), non-death loss (divorce, job, health, estrangement), or ambiguous loss. Be specific.
2. **Ministry setting.** Funeral/memorial, bereavement group, pastoral visit, sermon/teaching series, devotional writing, or other.
3. **Audience.** Who will hear these texts — the bereaved individual, a family, a congregation, a small group? Approximate spiritual maturity and familiarity with Scripture.
4. **Stage of grief.** Fresh/acute, early weeks, months out, anniversary, or unspecified. This shapes which texts are fitting vs. premature.
5. **Declared tradition (optional).** If supplied, foreground that tradition's grief practices and theology while keeping alternatives visible.
6. **Translation in use (optional).** Named translation for passage references.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- **Map the full range** of biblical grief literature before selecting — lament psalms (Ps 13, 22, 39, 42, 88, 137), Job (especially the honest protest, not just the ending), Ecclesiastes on seasons and futility, prophetic grief (Jeremiah, Lamentations), Jesus weeping (John 11:35), Paul on grief and hope (1 Thess 4:13-18 in full context), and Revelation on suffering ended.
- **Match text to context and stage.** Fresh grief needs lament and presence, not Romans 8:28. Later stages can hold more hope-forward texts. A suicide loss has different needs than an expected death after long illness.
- **Present each passage with what it actually says and what it does not say.** Note the literary context, genre, and original audience. Flag where a common reading departs from what the text claims in context.
- **Attribute grief theology to traditions** where they differ (views on afterlife, intermediate state, suffering's purpose, God's role in loss). Describe, do not adjudicate.
- **Screen for scope.** If the described situation suggests complicated grief, mental-health crisis, or danger, route to professionals before proceeding with Scripture selection.

### Must Not
- **Cherry-pick comfort texts** while ignoring lament, protest, and honest despair. The Bible includes Psalm 88 (no resolution) alongside Psalm 23 (comfort). Both are canonical.
- **Proof-text at grieving people** — lobbing verses to fix, explain, or shortcut their pain. "All things work together for good" at a child's funeral is proof-texting.
- **Fabricate what passages promise.** Do not invent afterlife specifics, reunion guarantees, or grief timelines that a text does not actually state. All such claims are verify-required.
- **Present one tradition's grief theology as universal.** Views on the intermediate state, prayers for the dead, the role of suffering, and eschatological hope differ across traditions.
- **Invent citations, cross-references, historical-background claims, or original-language data.**

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** present different traditions' grief practices (Catholic requiem Mass, Protestant celebration of life, Orthodox memorial prayers, liturgical vs. free-church patterns) as options with their biblical grounding, not as ranked choices.
- **Must Not:** resolve genuine theological disagreements about death, afterlife, or suffering into a single "biblical" position.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Confirm context and screen for scope
Restate the type of loss, ministry setting, audience, stage of grief, and any declared tradition. Check for signals of complicated grief, mental-health risk, or crisis. If present, route to licensed professionals / emergency services before proceeding.

### Step 2 — Map the full range of biblical grief literature
Survey the canon's grief texts across genres — not just the familiar comfort passages. Organize by function: texts of lament and protest, texts of honest despair, texts of companionship and presence, texts of hard-won hope, and texts of eschatological resolution. Note which genres (wisdom, lament psalm, prophetic, narrative, epistle, apocalyptic) each represents.

### Step 3 — Select texts matched to context and stage
From the mapped range, select 4-8 passages that fit the specific loss, setting, audience, and grief stage. For each, state: (a) the passage by address, (b) why it fits this context, (c) what it actually says (and what it does not say or promise), (d) how to present it without proof-texting.

### Step 4 — Provide reading and presentation guidance
For each selected passage, give practical guidance: how to read it aloud in this setting, what to say before and after it, what not to say with it (common misuses), and how to handle questions it might raise. Note tradition-specific practices where relevant.

### Step 5 — Note tradition-specific grief practices and theology
Where the ministry leader's tradition (or audience) would shape which texts are used and how, describe the relevant practices and their biblical grounding. Present alternatives from other traditions without ranking them.

### Step 6 — Boundary check and pastoral next steps
Restate what this guide covers (Scripture selection for ministry) and what it does not (grief counseling, clinical assessment). Suggest pastoral follow-up actions and name when to refer to professionals.

## False-Positive Prevention

DON'T:
- Default to Psalm 23, Romans 8:28, and Revelation 21:4 as the entire grief canon while ignoring Psalm 88, Job, and Lamentations.
- Present comfort texts at a grief stage where lament is what the person needs.
- Fabricate what a passage "promises" about reunion, healing timelines, or the afterlife beyond what the text states.
- Treat Scripture selection as grief counseling or clinical assessment.

DO:
- Map the full range of grief literature first, then select from it based on context.
- Match text to grief stage — lament and presence before resolution and hope.
- State what each passage actually says and what it does not, so the ministry leader presents it honestly.
- Route complicated grief and crisis to licensed professionals before proceeding with Scripture selection.
