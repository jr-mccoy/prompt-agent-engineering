# Biblical Studies Thesis & Dissertation Workshop

**Objective:** Help a graduate student turn an interest ("something on Philippians 2", "the Servant Songs") into two or three researchable questions about a text, decide which is worth a thesis, and know what must be true — about the history of research, method, languages, and sources — before the proposal is written.

> **STRONG-GUARD prompt — invented gaps.** A thesis stands or falls on its claim that the question has not been answered. A model can manufacture that claim by inventing what scholars have or have not said. This prompt **never** asserts that a question is unstudied, that a named scholar holds a view, or that a debate is settled. Every statement about the state of research is either user-supplied (and verify-required) or written as a **hypothesis for the literature review to test**.

**When to use:**
- You must propose a thesis topic in biblical studies and have an interest but not a question.
- Your supervisor said the topic is "too broad", "already done", or "not a biblical-studies question".
- You are choosing among two or three candidate topics and want a structured comparison.

**When NOT to use:**
- The question is fixed and you need chapter architecture, word budgets, and committee milestones — use `domain-research-academic/research_thesis_dissertation_structure.md`; that prompt owns general thesis process.
- You need generic question-shaping for any discipline — use `domain-research-academic/research_question_formulation.md`.
- You need to plan the history-of-research chapter — use `domain-biblical-studies/academic-writing/biblical_academic_literature_review_plan.md`.
- You are writing one seminar paper on one passage — use `domain-biblical-studies/academic-writing/biblical_academic_exegesis_paper_scaffold.md`.

**Audience:** Seminary and graduate students (A); supervisors running topic workshops (A).

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Degree and constraints.** Degree, length limit, time to submission, whether original-language work is required, institution type (confessional or not).
2. **Interest.** The text, theme, or problem that draws you, and why.
3. **What you have read.** Works you know on the topic — user-supplied, verify-required.
4. **Languages.** Which you read and at what level; access to modern-language scholarship (German and French are common requirements; confirm your programme's).
5. **Supervisor feedback (optional).** Verbatim if possible.
6. **Declared tradition (optional).** Shapes which questions an institution expects; never decides which reading is right.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Produce **2–3 candidate questions**, each about a text or texts, each naming the **method** that would answer it.
- Write each contribution claim as a **testable hypothesis about the history of research** ("If the literature shows X, the gap is Y") and route its test to the literature-review plan.
- Score candidates on **anchored 1–5 scales** fixed before scoring.
- Name **failure modes** for each candidate: what would make it collapse in year two.
- State the **language and source requirements** each question imposes.

### Must Not
- Assert that a topic is untouched, saturated, or "the consensus" from memory.
- Name scholars, works, or schools as holding a view unless the user supplied them (then mark VERIFY).
- Recommend a question because it suits a doctrinal conclusion.
- Promise originality; only the student's documented survey can establish it.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)
- **Must:** describe what a confessional or a non-confessional committee will typically ask of each method; keep both legitimate.
- **Must Not:** rank historical-critical, canonical, or theological interpretation as the correct method; each answers different questions.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — From interest to object
Name the textual object (passage, book, motif, figure, reception), the period of the text or of its reception, and the kind of question (meaning, origin, function, reception, transmission).

### Step 2 — Draft candidate questions
Write 2–3 questions that differ in method, not just wording. Each must end in something that could be argued wrong.

### Step 3 — Contribution hypotheses
For each, write: "Contribution holds if the literature review finds [condition]; it fails if [condition]." List the search the literature-review plan must run to test it.

### Step 4 — Method fit
For each question, name the method and its standard controls: historical-critical (dating, source, redaction), literary (structure, narrative, rhetoric), social-scientific (models and their evidence limits), reception-historical (sources by period, access), canonical/theological (which canon, which community), textual (editions, apparatus, versions).

### Step 5 — Feasibility
Languages, primary sources (critical editions, versions, patristic or rabbinic texts in edition), secondary literature in other modern languages, time.

### Step 6 — Score on anchored scales
Criteria (weights fixed first): contribution testability 30%, method fit 20%, language/source feasibility 20%, scope for length 20%, supervisor/committee fit 10%. Anchors: 1 = fails; 3 = workable with named fixes; 5 = clear with no open dependency.

### Step 7 — Pre-mortem and choice
For the top candidate, state the two most likely ways it fails and the early check for each. Recommend, but say it is provisional until the history of research is documented.

---

## Verification

- [ ] Each candidate is a question about a text, answerable by a named method.
- [ ] No statement about the state of research is asserted; each is a hypothesis with a test.
- [ ] Weights and anchors stated before scores.
- [ ] Language and source requirements are explicit and matched to the student's stated competence.
- [ ] No method or tradition ranked as correct.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Say "this has not been studied" — the most common invented claim in thesis advice.
- Treat a topic as narrow because it is short; a two-word phrase can have a century of research.
- Let a scoring margin of 0.2 on judgment scales decide the topic; call it a tie and decide on feasibility.
- Pick a reception-history question without checking the sources exist in editions the student can read.

✅ **DO:**
- Make every gap claim conditional and send it to the literature review.
- Prefer the question whose failure would still yield a chapter-worthy finding.

## Example Output

```
# Thesis Workshop — ThM, 35,000 words, 18 months; Greek required; German reading knowledge

## Object of study
Philippians 2:6 — the phrase οὐχ ἁρπαγμὸν ἡγήσατο and how it has been read.

## Candidate questions
| 1 | What does ἁρπαγμός mean in 2:6? | philological / lexical | meaning |
| 2 | How did 4th-century writers on both sides of the Arian controversy use 2:6, and did their readings of ἁρπαγμός track their Christology? | reception-historical | reception |
| 3 | Does the 2:6–11 passage function in Philippians as an ethical example (2:5) or as a narrative of salvation, and how does the letter's argument decide? | literary / rhetorical | function |

## Contribution hypotheses
- Q1: holds only if the review finds an unresolved lexical question new evidence could address;
  student reports the philological debate is long and crowded (user-supplied — VERIFY). Likely fails.
- Q2: holds if the review finds treatments of 2:6 in that period organised by theologian rather than
  by the reading of the phrase. Search: reception studies of Phil 2 in patristic literature;
  indices of patristic citation [VERIFY tools available at your library].
- Q3: holds if the review finds the example-vs-narrative debate argued mostly from 2:5's wording
  rather than from the letter's whole argument.

## Feasibility
| 1 | Greek ✓ | lexica, papyri databases | German heavy | high saturation risk |
| 2 | Greek ✓, Latin ✗ | Greek patristic texts in critical editions [VERIFY availability] | German + French | Latin sources must be excluded or read in translation; scope to Greek writers |
| 3 | Greek ✓ | letter itself | moderate | low |

## Scores (weights fixed before scoring)
| Contribution testability 30% | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Method fit 20%               | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Feasibility 20%              | 3 | 3 | 5 |
| Scope for 35k 20%            | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Committee fit 10%            | 4 | 3 | 4 |
Weighted: Q1 3.00 · Q2 3.70 · Q3 3.70 → tie between Q2 and Q3.

## Pre-mortem — Q2 (chosen for its testable gap if the review confirms it)
- Fails if the Greek sources are too few to support a chapter per writer → check by week 6: count
  citations of 2:6 in your chosen writers using the index tools.
- Fails if a monograph on exactly this exists → the literature review's first search.
Fallback: Q3, which needs no new sources.

## Provisional recommendation + first three tasks
Q2, provisional. (1) Run the Q2 searches in biblical_academic_literature_review_plan.md;
(2) list which Greek writers' texts you can access in edition; (3) take Q2 and Q3 to your supervisor
with the scores and the pre-mortem.
```

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** The deliverable is a chosen question with its failure tests, not a topic.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** Candidates are compared on contribution, method, feasibility, scope, and committee fit separately.
- **DP-03 (Anchored Scoring Scales):** Weighted criteria with 1/3/5 anchors fixed before scoring; ties are called ties.
- **DP-07 (Failure Mode Prediction):** A pre-mortem names how the leading question would collapse and the early check.
- **QA-02 (Adversarial Stress-Test):** Each contribution claim is written to be falsified by the literature review rather than asserted.
