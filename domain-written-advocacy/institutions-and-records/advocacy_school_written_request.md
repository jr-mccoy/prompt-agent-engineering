**Purpose:** Help you write **your own** dated request to a school, district, or college — asking for an evaluation or assessment, requesting support or accommodations, asking for records, or asking for a grade or decision to be reviewed. Dated, specific, addressed to someone who can actually act, and written so the request exists on the record rather than in a hallway conversation.

**When to use:** You need something from a school or college for your child or yourself, you want it considered formally, and you want a record of what was asked and when.

**When NOT to use:** You want to prepare for a meeting rather than send a letter → `domain-parenting/parenting_school_accommodation_conversation_prep.md`. The matter involves a child's immediate safety, abuse, or a serious incident → the Boundary & Routing Block, immediately. You are in a formal statutory dispute or considering legal action → route to an attorney or an education advocacy service. You want broad institutional records rather than your own child's → `advocacy_public_records_request.md`.

## Boundary & Routing Block

Use a different pathway if:
- **A child is unsafe, has been harmed, or you are reporting abuse or a serious incident** → this is not a written request. Contact the school's designated safeguarding lead and the appropriate authority immediately, and emergency services if a child is in danger. `[VERIFY: the reporting route for your jurisdiction from an official source.]`
- **The matter concerns bullying that is ongoing and affecting safety or wellbeing** → use the school's safeguarding or behaviour route rather than a general request, and escalate promptly rather than corresponding.
- **You are in a formal statutory process, appealing a placement or exclusion, or considering legal action** → these have specific routes, evidence requirements, and short deadlines. Route to an attorney, **legal aid**, or a specialist education advocacy service. `[VERIFY: locate an education advocacy or advice service in your jurisdiction from an official source.]`
- **You suspect a disability or additional need has not been identified** → a request for assessment is a reasonable and normal thing to ask for, but **this prompt does not assess the child, suggest a diagnosis, or tell you what they need.** That is for qualified professionals. Ask for the assessment; do not assert its conclusion.
- **A deadline is stated in something the school sent you** → find it in that document and act on it; this prompt does not state deadlines and assuming one is a common error.
- **The student is in mental-health distress** → seek clinical support; a support request to a school is not a substitute. `[VERIFY: locate the crisis support line for your country from an official source.]`

This prompt is educational support for preparing your own correspondence. It is not a substitute for legal, medical, safeguarding, or specialist educational services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This **drafts your own written request to a school or college for you to send**. It is **not legal advice, medical advice, educational-psychology advice, a diagnosis, a legal filing, or a substitute for an attorney, a clinician, a qualified assessor, or your jurisdiction's law.** It will **not** tell you what a school, district, or college is required to provide, assess, or fund; name a statutory process, plan, or entitlement, or cite, quote, or number any education statute or regulation as authority; state any timeline, deadline, or required response time; tell you whether a child qualifies for anything, or assess, diagnose, or characterize their needs; tell you what support or accommodation a child should receive; predict whether the request will be granted; assess how strong it is; or invent a policy term, assessment content, grade, or record. Education systems, statutory processes, entitlements, and timelines **vary entirely by country, state, district, and institution and change over time.** Where such a concept appears it is flagged *verify with the institution and an advocacy service*.

## Core Principles

1. **Put it in writing and date it.** Verbal requests to teachers are made in good faith and lost constantly. A dated written request to a named person is the single most useful thing a parent or student can do, and it costs one email.
2. **Send it to someone who can act.** A class teacher usually cannot authorise an assessment. Ask who the right recipient is — the head of year, the SEN or special-education coordinator, the head, the disability services office — and address it to them, copying the teacher.
3. **Ask for the assessment; do not assert the conclusion.** "I am requesting an assessment because of [observations]" is a request a school can act on. "My child has [condition] and needs [provision]" asserts two things you are not positioned to assert and often gets a defensive reply.
4. **Describe observations, not diagnoses.** What you have seen, when, how often, with dates and examples. Observations from home and from school reports are evidence; conclusions about their cause are for qualified assessors.
5. **Ask what the process is and who decides.** Institutions have processes that are rarely explained unprompted. Asking makes the next step visible and shows where a request has stalled.
6. **Keep a request separate from a complaint.** If you are unhappy about how something was handled, that is a different letter. Combining them tends to route the request into a complaints procedure and delay it.
7. **You request and document; the professionals assess.** What a child needs, and what anyone is required to provide, are for qualified assessors, an advocacy service, and an attorney. Stop at the boundary.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Screen for Safety and Frame the Matter
Screen **first** for safeguarding concerns, a child's safety, ongoing bullying affecting wellbeing, a statutory dispute or exclusion, a mental-health crisis, and any deadline in a document the school sent → route each per the Boundary & Routing Block. Restate the jurisdiction and institution type and the boundary: this drafts a request; assessment and entitlement are for professionals.

### Stage 2 — Identify the Right Recipient
Establish who can actually act on this type of request — the special-education or SEN coordinator, head of year, head, disability services, or examinations officer. Where the user does not know, build the letter to ask who handles it, and flag `[NEED RECIPIENT:]` rather than guessing a role that may not exist in that system.

### Stage 3 — State the Request Precisely
Convert what the user wants into a specific request: an assessment of a named area of concern, a named accommodation, specified records, or a review of a specified decision or grade with its date and reference.

### Stage 4 — Build the Observational Support
Record what the user has observed — with dates, frequency, and concrete examples — alongside anything the school itself has reported or recorded. Keep it observational throughout. Where a professional is already involved, note that a report exists without characterizing its content.

### Stage 5 — Add the Process Questions
Ask what the process is for a request of this kind, who decides, what information is needed from the user, and when they can expect a response. These questions are what make a stalled request visible later.

### Stage 6 — Draft the Request and Close
Compose the user's own dated request, labeled as theirs to send, with the Sending Log. Note that a complaint about handling is a separate letter, and point to an advocacy service for statutory or contested matters, flagged `[VERIFY:]`. Route assessment, entitlement, and legal questions onward.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Request an IEP evaluation — they must respond within 60 days" | Name no statutory process and state no timeline; ask what their process is |
| "This is clearly dyslexia — say so in the letter" | Describe observations; asking for an assessment is the point of the letter |
| "Under education law they are required to assess" | Cite no law; make the request and route entitlement to an advocacy service |
| "Ask for extra time and a scribe — that's what they need" | Do not prescribe provision; request assessment or a named accommodation the user chose |
| "The school is legally obliged to fund this" | State no obligation |
| "They'll approve it — the observations are compelling" | Make no prediction or strength assessment |
| Send it to the class teacher | Ask who can act; a teacher usually cannot authorise an assessment |
| Add two paragraphs about the school's poor handling | Keep the complaint separate; combining them delays the request |
| Tell the parent what the clinician's report should conclude | Note a report exists; its content is the professional's |
| Write a letter about a safeguarding concern | Stop — safeguarding lead and the appropriate authority, immediately |

## Adaptations

**By request type:**
- **Assessment or evaluation:** Observations plus what the school has recorded; ask what assessment they can arrange and what the process is. Do not name a statutory route.
- **Support or accommodation:** Name the specific accommodation and the context it applies to (lessons, homework, exams); ask what evidence they need and whether a trial is possible.
- **Records:** Specify which records and for what period; ask what their process is for providing them — for a college this may be a formal route.
- **Grade or decision review:** Give the assessment, date, and reference, state specifically what you are asking to be reviewed, and ask what the review process and any deadline is.

**By institution type:**
- **Primary or elementary school:** Usually a single point of contact plus a coordinator; keep it short and ask who to address it to.
- **Secondary or high school:** Head of year plus the coordinator; copy the class teacher so the request is visible where it is felt.
- **College or university:** The disability or student services office is generally the actor, not the tutor; students usually make the request themselves and the process is often formalised.
- **Where a district or authority is involved:** Ask whether the request goes to the institution or the authority, and to which; sending to the wrong one is a common source of delay.
