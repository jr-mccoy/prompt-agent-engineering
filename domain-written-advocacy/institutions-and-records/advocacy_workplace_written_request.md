**Purpose:** Help you write **your own** dated request to your employer — a workplace adjustment or accommodation, a period of leave, a flexible or remote arrangement, or an exception to a policy. It states clearly what you are asking for and what it would enable you to do, keeps disclosure to what the request actually requires, and asks how the request will be handled.

**When to use:** You need something from your employer, you want it considered properly rather than decided in a corridor conversation, and you want a record of what you asked and when.

**When NOT to use:** You are raising a complaint about treatment, harassment, discrimination, or retaliation → `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/workplace/legalprep_hr_complaint_narrative_preparer.md`; a complaint and a request are different documents and should not be combined. The request is specifically about a mental health condition and you want that framing → `domain-psychology/client-self-use/communication-system/clientself_workplace_accommodations_request.md`. You have been disciplined, dismissed, or threatened with either → route to an attorney.

---

## Boundary & Routing Block

Use a different pathway if:
- **You are experiencing discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or unfair treatment** → that is a complaint, not a request, and combining the two weakens both. See `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/workplace/`, and route to an attorney or **legal aid** where the treatment may be unlawful.
- **You have been disciplined, put on a performance plan, dismissed, or threatened with any of these** → route to an attorney or legal aid before writing anything. A request written in that context can be read very differently than intended, and timing matters.
- **You are weighing whether to disclose a health condition, disability, pregnancy, or caring responsibility at all** → that decision has real consequences in both directions and depends on your jurisdiction, your employer, and your circumstances. **This prompt cannot advise you on it.** Route to an attorney, a disability advocacy service, or a union representative before disclosing. `[VERIFY: locate an advocacy or advice service in your jurisdiction from an official source.]`
- **You are a union member** → your representative can usually advise on approach and be present, and may know how similar requests have been handled. Contact them before sending.
- **The request concerns a safety hazard at work** → raise it through the safety route, which is usually distinct and often protected, rather than as a general request.
- **You are unwell and need medical advice or time off on health grounds** → speak to a clinician; this prompt does not assess health needs or what you should ask for medically.

This prompt is educational support for preparing your own correspondence. It is not a substitute for legal, medical, or union representation.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This **drafts your own written workplace request for you to send**. It is **not legal advice, medical advice, HR advice, a legal filing, or a substitute for an attorney, a clinician, a union representative, or your jurisdiction's law.** It will **not** tell you what you are entitled to, or what an employer must provide, consider, or grant; cite, quote, or number any employment, disability, leave, or equality statute or regulation as authority; tell you whether you meet any legal definition; advise you on whether to disclose a health condition, disability, pregnancy, or caring responsibility, or on the risks of doing so; tell you what medical evidence you need or what a clinician should write; state any deadline or required response time; predict whether the request will be granted; assess how strong it is; or invent a policy term, job duty, medical fact, or entitlement. Employment rules **vary by country, state, employer, and contract and change over time.** Where such a concept appears it is flagged *verify with an attorney, a union representative, or an advice service*.

## Core Principles

1. **A request is not a complaint.** Keep them separate. A request asks for something forward-looking; a complaint alleges something about the past. Mixing them tends to get the request routed into a grievance process and slows everything down.
2. **Lead with what you need, not with why.** "I am requesting a start time of 10am" is the request. The reason supports it and usually needs far less detail than people supply.
3. **Disclose what the request requires, and no more.** You can usually describe the functional need — a quieter space, a modified schedule, a piece of equipment — without a diagnosis or a medical history. What to disclose is a decision with consequences, and it is one to take with advice, not with a template.
4. **Say what it would enable.** Connecting the adjustment to doing the job — "this would let me complete the reporting task without interruption" — is what makes a request easy to consider and hard to dismiss.
5. **Propose something specific and workable.** A concrete proposal, ideally with an alternative and a trial period, is far more likely to be engaged with than an open-ended ask.
6. **Ask how it will be handled.** Who decides, by when, and what happens next. A request that disappears into an inbox is the common failure mode, and asking the question prevents it.
7. **You request and document; the professional handles rights and disclosure.** What you are entitled to, and whether to disclose, are for an attorney, a union representative, or an advocacy service. Stop at the boundary.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Require the jurisdiction; use only the facts the user supplies.
- Screen for discrimination, harassment, retaliation, discipline, and dismissal, and route those to a complaint or an attorney.
- Route any unresolved disclosure decision to an attorney, advocacy service, or union representative **before** drafting.
- Direct union members to their representative first.
- Keep the request separate from any complaint.
- Lead with the specific proposal and what it would enable.
- Limit disclosure to what the user has decided to disclose, and never expand it.
- Include an alternative and a trial period where the user offers them.
- Ask who decides, by when, and what the process is.
- Include a Sending Log and label the output `MY OWN REQUEST — NOT A LEGAL FILING`.

**Must Not:**
- State what the user is entitled to, or what the employer must provide, consider, or grant.
- Cite, quote, or number any employment, disability, leave, or equality statute or regulation.
- State whether the user meets any legal definition.
- Advise on whether to disclose a condition, disability, pregnancy, or caring responsibility, or characterize the risk either way.
- Tell the user what medical evidence to obtain, or what a clinician should write.
- Assess a health condition, capability, or any clinical question.
- State any deadline or required employer response time.
- Predict whether the request will be granted, or assess how strong it is.
- Invent a policy term, job duty, medical fact, diagnosis, or entitlement.
- Combine a request with an allegation of unlawful treatment.
- Add detail about a health condition beyond what the user chose to include.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Screen for Safety and Frame the Matter
Screen for discrimination, harassment, retaliation, active discipline, dismissal risk, an unresolved disclosure decision, union membership, and safety hazards → route each per the Boundary & Routing Block **before** drafting. Where the disclosure decision is unresolved, stop and route it. Restate the jurisdiction and the boundary: this drafts a request; entitlement and disclosure are for an attorney, advocate, or union.

### Stage 2 — Separate Request From Complaint
Confirm the user wants a forward-looking request. Where they also have concerns about treatment, note that those belong in a separate document and point to the complaint prompt, so the request is not absorbed into a grievance process.

### Stage 3 — Build the Specific Proposal
Convert what the user needs into a concrete, workable proposal: what exactly, how often, from when, for how long. Add an alternative and a trial period where the user offers them — both make a request substantially easier to say yes to.

### Stage 4 — Connect It to the Work
State what the proposal would enable in terms of the job — a task, a duty, an output. Keep this functional. This is what turns a personal request into an operational one.

### Stage 5 — Set the Disclosure Level the User Chose
Draft using only what the user has decided to disclose. Where they have chosen functional-need-only, describe the need without diagnosis or history and do not hint at more. Never expand disclosure, and never suggest that more would help.

### Stage 6 — Draft the Request and Close
Compose the user's own dated request, labeled as theirs to send, with the Sending Log. Include questions about who decides, by when, and the process. Point to the response analyzer and the follow-up tracker. Route entitlement, disclosure, and legal questions onward.

[The specific proposal, stated plainly — e.g. "I am requesting a start time of 10:00 rather

## What this would enable
[Functional, work-connected — e.g. "This would allow me to complete the morning reporting cycle

## Background
[Only what the user has decided to disclose. Functional-need framing where chosen — e.g. "I have
a health condition that affects [functional impact]. I am not including further detail here and
am happy to discuss what information would help you consider this."]

## An alternative I could work with
[If offered — e.g. "If the full arrangement is not possible, [n] days per week would also work."]

## Trial period
[If offered — e.g. "I am happy for this to run as a trial for [n] weeks, reviewed on
[YYYY-MM-DD], if that helps."]

## Relevant policy
[If the user has seen one — e.g. "I understand from [policy name] that [what it states]."]

[If any — e.g. "I raised this with [name] on [YYYY-MM-DD]. [Outcome.]"]

1. Who will consider this request.
2. By when I can expect a decision.
3. What information, if any, you need from me to consider it.

[Your name], [role], [YYYY-MM-DD]

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Under the ADA they must provide a reasonable accommodation" | Cite no law; make the request and route entitlement to an attorney |
| "You clearly meet the legal definition of disability" | State no legal definition; describe the functional need the user chose to share |
| "Disclose your diagnosis — it strengthens the request" | Never advise on disclosure; route that decision to an advocate or attorney |
| "Get a doctor's note saying you cannot work mornings" | Do not direct medical evidence or script a clinician |
| "They have 10 days to respond" | State no deadline; ask when a decision can be expected |
| "They'll approve this, it's a reasonable ask" | Make no prediction |
| Add "and I've been treated unfairly for months" to the request | Keep the complaint separate; combining them slows and weakens both |
| Include the full medical history to be thorough | Include only what the user chose; offer to discuss what would help |
| "Just ask for whatever you need and see what they say" | Propose something specific, with an alternative and a trial period |
| Draft a request while the user is on a performance plan | Stop, use the Boundary & Routing Block, route to an attorney |

## Adaptations

**By request type:**
- **Workplace adjustment:** Lead with the functional need and the specific adjustment; offering a trial period materially helps and costs nothing.
- **Leave:** State dates, the type of leave if you know which applies, and any handover you propose — the handover offer is frequently what gets it agreed.
- **Flexible or remote working:** Address coverage and communication directly, since those are the usual objections; propose how you would handle both.
- **Policy exception:** Name the policy, state what you are asking to vary and for how long, and ask what the process for an exception is rather than assuming one exists.

**By situation/profile:**
- **Union member:** Representative first; they can advise on approach, be present, and often know precedent within the organization.
- **Small employer with no HR:** Send to the owner or manager and keep it short, practical, and specific; formal process language can be counterproductive.
- **Previously refused informally:** Note the prior discussion with its date, and ask for the reason in writing — that is a reasonable and useful ask.
- **Uncertain about disclosure:** Boundary & Routing Block — get advice before sending anything; disclosure is difficult to undo.
