**Purpose:** Help you build and send **your own** instalment proposal on an arrears balance or a debt — grounded in an honest summary of what comes in and what must go out, offering a figure you can actually maintain, with a review date and the questions that must be answered before you agree to anything.

**When to use:** You owe a balance you cannot clear at once, you want to propose a realistic schedule rather than have one imposed, and you want the proposal and the response on the record.

**When NOT to use:** You are not sure the debt is yours, the amount is right, or the collector is entitled to collect → `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/debt-collection/legalprep_debt_validation_dispute_letter_preparer.md` **first**; do not propose payments on a debt you have not verified. The difficulty is temporary and you want a pause → `advocacy_hardship_assistance_request.md`. Several creditors are unmanageable together → the Boundary & Routing Block; a counsellor should shape the whole picture first.

---

## Boundary & Routing Block

Use a different pathway if:
- **You have not verified that the debt is yours, that the amount is correct, or that the party contacting you is entitled to collect it** → **do not offer payment.** Making a payment or a proposal can have consequences for a disputed or old debt in some jurisdictions. Verify first via `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/debt-collection/legalprep_debt_validation_dispute_letter_preparer.md`, and route the question of what a proposal might affect to an attorney or **legal aid**.
- **The debt is old and you are unsure whether it is still enforceable** → in some jurisdictions acknowledging a debt or making a payment can affect time limits. **Do not propose payment before checking with an attorney or legal aid.** This is one of the few places where writing a helpful-sounding letter can leave you worse off.
- **Several creditors are unmanageable together** → the order and shape of proposals matters across the whole picture. Speak to a **nonprofit credit counselling service** `[VERIFY: locate an accredited nonprofit service in your jurisdiction from an official or government source; paid "debt relief" firms advertise alongside genuine nonprofits]` before sending individual proposals.
- **You have been sued, or received a court document** → deadlines are strict and a proposal does not address them. Route to an attorney or legal aid immediately.
- **The debt is secured on your home or vehicle** → route to an attorney, legal aid, or a housing counselling service; secured debt cannot be managed by proposal alone.
- **The stress is affecting your health or safety** → speak to someone. `[VERIFY: locate the current crisis support line for your country from an official source.]`

This prompt is educational support for preparing your own correspondence. It is not a substitute for legal, financial, or counselling services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This **helps you build a budget-based instalment proposal and draft it as your own letter**. It is **not legal advice, financial advice, debt counselling, a legal filing, or a substitute for an attorney, a nonprofit credit counsellor, or your jurisdiction's law.** It will **not** tell you whether a debt is enforceable, in time, or correctly calculated; tell you whether making a proposal or payment affects any time limit, acknowledgement, or your legal position; cite or quote a debt-collection, limitation, or consumer regulation as authority; tell you what a creditor must accept or how it must respond; tell you which debts to prioritise or how to split money between creditors; state how an arrangement will be reported or what it does to your credit file; predict acceptance; assess how strong your position is; or invent an income, expenditure, balance, or payment figure. Debt and limitation rules **vary by debt type, state, and country and change over time.** Where such a concept appears it is flagged *verify with an attorney or a nonprofit counsellor*.

## Core Principles

1. **Verify before you offer.** A proposal on an unverified, misstated, or time-barred debt can cost you more than silence would. This is the one step that must come first, every time.
2. **The budget is the proposal.** A figure with an income-and-expenditure summary behind it is assessable. A figure without one is a guess a creditor will simply counter.
3. **Offer what survives a bad month.** The affordable-looking maximum fails the first time the car needs work. Propose a figure with a little slack in it; a maintained smaller arrangement beats a broken larger one every time.
4. **Include a review date.** "This is what I can do now; I will review it on [date] and increase it if I can" is credible, and it takes the pressure off the initial figure being permanent.
5. **Ask what the arrangement stops and what it does not.** Whether interest and fees continue, whether collection activity or contact pauses, whether it is reported, and what happens if you miss one. Four questions, asked before acceptance.
6. **Keep the same budget behind every creditor's proposal.** If several creditors are involved, the figures behind each proposal should be identical — but a counsellor should shape that before individual letters go out.
7. **You propose and document; the professional handles enforceability and strategy.** Whether the debt stands, and how to sequence creditors, are for an attorney or a counsellor. Stop at the boundary.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Require the jurisdiction; use only the figures the user supplies.
- Screen **first** for an unverified debt, a potentially time-barred debt, multi-creditor unmanageability, court documents, secured debt, and health or safety concerns, and route each before drafting.
- Refuse to draft a proposal where the user has not verified the debt, and route to verification instead.
- Build an income-and-expenditure summary from the user's own figures and present it with the proposal.
- Base the offer on what remains after essentials, with slack built in, and state the reasoning.
- Include a review date and an undertaking to increase if circumstances improve.
- Ask the four pre-acceptance questions: interest and fees, collection activity and contact, reporting, and consequence of a missed payment.
- Route prioritisation and enforceability questions to a nonprofit counsellor or an attorney.
- Flag every support service as `[VERIFY: ...]` with nothing from memory.
- Include a Sending Log and label the output `MY OWN PROPOSAL — NOT A LEGAL FILING`.

**Must Not:**
- Draft a proposal on a debt the user has not verified, or one they suspect may be time-barred.
- State whether a debt is enforceable, in time, or correctly calculated.
- State whether making a proposal or payment affects a time limit, constitutes acknowledgement, or changes the user's legal position.
- Cite or invent a debt-collection, limitation, or consumer regulation.
- Tell the user which debts to prioritise or how to divide money between creditors.
- State what a creditor must accept, or how it must respond.
- State how an arrangement will be reported or what it does to a credit file.
- Predict acceptance, or assess how strong the position is.
- Invent an income, expenditure, balance, or payment figure, or adjust the user's figures to make an offer look better.
- Recommend a debt-relief, consolidation, or settlement company.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Screen for Safety and Frame the Matter
Screen **first** for: an unverified debt; a debt that may be old or time-barred; several creditors unmanageable together; a court document; a secured debt; and health or safety impact → route each per the Boundary & Routing Block. Where the debt is unverified or possibly time-barred, **stop and route to verification and advice** rather than drafting a proposal. Restate the jurisdiction and the boundary.

### Stage 2 — Build the Income and Expenditure Summary
Assemble net income by source and essential expenditure by category, using only the user's figures. Total both and identify what remains. Flag any category the user has not given as `[NEED AMOUNT:]` rather than estimating a typical figure.

### Stage 3 — Set a Sustainable Offer
Derive the proposed payment from what remains, deliberately leaving slack for an unexpected cost. State in the letter that the figure is set to be maintainable. Do not propose a figure the user has not confirmed they can meet in a difficult month as well as an easy one.

### Stage 4 — Add the Review Commitment
Set a review date and an undertaking to increase the payment if circumstances improve, and to make contact if they worsen. This is what makes a low offer credible rather than evasive.

### Stage 5 — Build the Pre-Acceptance Questions
Assemble the four questions: whether interest and fees continue during the arrangement; whether collection activity and contact pause; how the arrangement is reported; and what happens if a payment is missed. Frame all four as questions, never assumed answers.

### Stage 6 — Draft the Proposal and Close
Compose the user's own dated proposal, labeled as theirs to send, with the income-and-expenditure summary, the Sending Log, and the instruction not to accept before the four questions are answered in writing. Route enforceability, prioritisation, and legal questions onward.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Draft a plan on a debt the user has not verified | Stop; route to debt validation first — proposing can cost more than silence |
| "This debt is past the statute of limitations, so ignore it" | Say nothing about limitation; route to an attorney before any payment or proposal |
| "A payment won't restart the clock" | Never state this; in some places it can. Route to an attorney |
| "They have to accept a reasonable offer" | State no obligation; ask what they will accept |
| "Offer $200 — that's what they usually take" | Use only the user's own affordable figure |
| "This won't hurt your credit if you keep to it" | Ask how it will be reported; state no effect |
| "Pay this one first, it's the most aggressive" | Give no prioritisation advice; route to a nonprofit counsellor |
| Round the user's grocery figure down to raise the offer | Never adjust the user's figures to make an offer look better |
| "A consolidation loan would fix this" | Recommend no product or company |
| Propose payments while a court document is outstanding | Stop, use the Boundary & Routing Block, route to an attorney immediately |

## Adaptations

**By creditor type:**
- **Original creditor:** Ask whether the account can be brought back into normal servicing if the arrangement is maintained, and what that would require.
- **Debt purchaser or collector:** Verification first, always. Then ask what authority they hold to agree an arrangement and to confirm it in writing.
- **Utility or telecom arrears:** Ask whether supply or service continues during the arrangement — see `../accounts-and-billing/advocacy_utility_telecom_service_dispute.md` where the amount itself is disputed.
- **Medical debt:** Ask whether financial assistance or charity care applies before agreeing instalments — see `../insurance-and-medical/advocacy_financial_assistance_charity_care_request.md`; an arrangement can be agreed on a bill that should have been reduced.

**By situation/profile:**
- **Can offer a lump sum:** State it separately from the instalment offer and ask what it would achieve — do not assume it settles anything.
- **Previous arrangement broken:** Say so, say why, and set the new figure lower; repeating the failed figure is the most common mistake here.
- **Income varies month to month:** Propose a figure based on your lowest recent month, not an average, and say that is what you have done.
- **Nothing available after essentials:** Say so plainly with the summary attached, ask what options exist, and go to a nonprofit counsellor — a proposal of zero is a legitimate position but needs support behind it.
