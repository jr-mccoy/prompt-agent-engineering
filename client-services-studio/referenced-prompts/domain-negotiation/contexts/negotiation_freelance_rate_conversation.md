# Freelance and Consulting Rate Conversation — Defend the Number, Trade Scope, Raise on Existing Clients

**Objective:** Independents typically know what they should charge and discount it anyway — not from a failure of analysis but from a failure of the thirty seconds after the number is spoken. The pause, the raised eyebrow, the "that's more than we had in mind" all produce an immediate unilateral discount that no one asked for. This prompt handles the live conversation: stating the number without hedging, responding to a discount request by **trading scope rather than cutting price**, handling the specific moves independents face (the cheaper-alternative comparison, the exposure offer, the promise of future volume), the scope-creep repricing conversation, and the hardest case of all — raising rates on a client you already have and do not want to lose.

The number itself is set upstream: `domain-personal-development/prompts/solo-dev/solo_dev_pricing_value_confidence.md` derives a defensible rate. This prompt defends it in the room.

**When to use:**
- About to state a rate to a prospective client.
- A client has asked for a discount and you need a response that is not a discount.
- Scope has grown and the fee has not.
- You need to raise rates on existing clients.

**When NOT to use:**
- You have not set a defensible rate yet — `solo_dev_pricing_value_confidence.md` first; you cannot defend a number you cannot justify.
- The engagement is large enough to be a commercial contract negotiation — use `preparation/` and `at-the-table/` in full.
- The question is whether to take the work at all rather than at what price.

**Audience:** Freelancers, consultants, contractors, and independent professionals discussing their own rates.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Your rate.** The number, and the basis you can state for it.
2. **Your floor.** The rate below which you decline — from `preparation/negotiation_batna_analysis.md`.
3. **Pipeline state.** How full you are, which determines how real your floor can be right now.
4. **The client.** Size, sophistication, whether they buy this regularly, and the relationship's length.
5. **The scope.** What is actually being asked for, and where it is ambiguous.
6. **What has already happened.** Any number, range, or signal already given.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- State the rate **without hedging, apology, or an immediate softener**. The sentence ends at the number.
- Respond to discount requests by **adjusting scope**, not price — the unit rate is the thing being defended.
- Hold a **floor** and be prepared to decline. A rate with no floor is a starting bid.
- Distinguish a **real budget constraint** from a routine test. Most discount requests are asked reflexively and folded immediately if the number holds.
- Price **scope creep explicitly** when it appears, rather than absorbing it and resenting it.
- Plan rate increases on existing clients with **notice and a reason**, not as a surprise at renewal.
- Recognize that **pipeline state determines what the floor can be** this month, and say so honestly to yourself.

### Must Not
- Discount unprompted after stating the number. The silence following a rate is normal and is not a rejection; filling it with a reduction is the single most expensive habit in independent work.
- Justify the rate with your costs. Clients buy outcomes; your overheads are not their concern and citing them invites a discussion about whether you need that much.
- Accept exposure, portfolio value, or future volume as payment. Future work is a promise with no enforcement, and it is almost always offered instead of money rather than alongside it.
- Absorb scope creep silently. It reprices the engagement downward without either party acknowledging it, and it recurs.
- Match a cheaper competitor's rate. It concedes that the offering is identical, which is the only ground on which you lose that comparison.
- Raise rates on an existing client without notice. It reads as opportunism regardless of how justified the increase is.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — State the number and stop
Write the exact sentence: "My rate for this is X." Then nothing. No "but there's some flexibility," no "I know that might be more than you expected," no immediate move to justification. The pause afterward is normal and it is where the number either holds or does not — and it holds far more often than independents expect. Rehearse it aloud, because this is a delivery problem rather than an analysis one.

### Step 2 — Have the basis ready but do not lead with it
Prepare a one-sentence basis for when it is asked for — outcome-based, not cost-based: "That reflects the scope and the level of work involved; it's consistent with what I charge for comparable engagements." Offer it if asked. Leading with justification signals the number needs defending.

### Step 3 — Trade scope, never price
When a discount is requested, the response is a scope conversation:

> *"I can work to that budget — it would mean [reduced scope / longer timeline / fewer revisions / you handling X]. Would that work?"*

This holds the unit rate, gives the client a genuine route to their budget, and keeps the value of your work intact. Cutting the rate instead teaches this client — and, through referral, others — that your stated price is provisional. Have two or three specific scope reductions ready in advance, because inventing them live produces vague ones.

### Step 4 — Handle the standard moves
- **"We can get this cheaper elsewhere."** Do not match. "That may well be true — different people work differently. If the price is the deciding factor, I completely understand." Matching concedes equivalence, which is the only basis on which cheaper wins.
- **"It's great exposure."** "I appreciate that, but I'm not able to take exposure as payment." Once, plainly, no elaboration.
- **"There'll be more work after this."** Price this engagement on its own terms. "Happy to look at a rate for ongoing work when we get there — for this piece it's X." Future volume offered as a reason for a present discount is a discount with no consideration.
- **"That's more than we had in mind."** "I understand. What did you have in mind?" — then Step 3. This is a question, not a rejection, and treating it as one is what produces the unforced discount.

### Step 5 — Distinguish a real constraint from a test
A **real budget constraint** is specific, comes with a number, and is usually stated early. A **test** is general, arrives after the rate, and folds immediately when the rate holds. Address real constraints with scope. Let tests pass by simply not moving — most do.

### Step 6 — Price scope creep when it appears
When the work grows, raise it at the moment it happens, factually and without grievance: "Happy to take that on — it's outside what we scoped, so it'd be an additional [amount/days]. Want me to proceed?" Immediate and neutral repricing is easy; raising three months of accumulated creep at the end is a confrontation, and the resentment that builds in the meantime damages the work. The word "outside what we scoped" does the whole job without accusation.

### Step 7 — Raise rates on existing clients
The hardest case. The approach that works: **advance notice** (one to three months, in writing), **applied at a natural boundary** (renewal, new project, year start), **stated as a fact rather than an ask** — "my rate is moving to X from January" — and **without over-explaining**, since a paragraph of justification invites negotiation of the justification. Expect to lose some clients; that is the mechanism by which a rate increase works, and losing none usually means the increase was too small. Decide in advance which clients you would keep at the old rate, if any, and why.

### Step 8 — Set the floor honestly against your pipeline
Write the rate below which you decline. Then be honest about pipeline: a floor is only real if you can afford to hold it this month. If you cannot, say so to yourself explicitly rather than discovering it mid-conversation — and treat "I took work below my floor because I needed the work" as a pipeline problem to solve, not a rate problem. The rate does not change; the willingness to decline does.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If they accept immediately with no discussion, was the rate too low?
- Did you discount before being asked, or fill a silence with a reduction?
- Are you treating a routine test as a real budget constraint because holding the number is uncomfortable?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The unprompted discount.** Reducing the number in the silence after stating it. No one asked. The pause is normal, it is not disapproval, and filling it is the single most costly habit in independent work.
2. **Cost-based justification.** Explaining the rate through your overheads, hours, or expenses. Clients buy outcomes; costs invite a conversation about whether your costs are necessary, which is not a conversation you can win.
3. **Price cutting instead of scope trading.** Meeting a budget by reducing the rate rather than the work. It reprices your time permanently for this client and, via referral, for others.
4. **Exposure acceptance.** Taking visibility, portfolio value, or a testimonial as consideration. These are offered instead of money, not in addition, and the promised value materializes rarely.
5. **Future-volume discounting.** Reducing this engagement's price against work that has not been agreed. There is no enforcement and frequently no second engagement.
6. **Competitor matching.** Meeting a cheaper quote. It concedes that the two offerings are equivalent, which is the only premise on which you lose.
7. **Silent creep absorption.** Doing the extra work without repricing it. It reduces the effective rate without acknowledgment, recurs because it was free, and produces resentment that shows up in the work.
8. **Unannounced increases.** Raising a rate at renewal without prior notice. Even a fully justified increase reads as opportunism when it arrives as a surprise, and it costs clients the notice would have kept.

## Verification

- [ ] The rate sentence written exactly, ending at the number, with nothing following.
- [ ] Basis prepared as outcome-based and held in reserve rather than led with.
- [ ] At least two specific scope trades prepared in advance.
- [ ] Responses drafted for cheaper-elsewhere, exposure, future-volume, and more-than-expected.
- [ ] Real constraint distinguished from test, with the signal stated.
- [ ] Scope-creep script prepared for immediate, neutral use.
- [ ] Existing-client increase includes advance written notice and a natural boundary.
- [ ] Increase stated as fact rather than request, without over-explanation.
- [ ] Expected attrition acknowledged as the mechanism rather than a failure.
- [ ] Floor stated, with an honest assessment of whether pipeline allows holding it.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests for the unprompted discount and the too-low rate.
- [ ] No cost-based justification anywhere.
- [ ] No competitor rate matched.
