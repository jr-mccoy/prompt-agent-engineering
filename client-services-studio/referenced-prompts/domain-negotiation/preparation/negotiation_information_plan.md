# Information Plan — What to Ask, What to Reveal, What to Protect

**Objective:** Information asymmetry decides more negotiations than skill does, and most of it is settled before anyone makes an offer. This prompt plans the information side deliberately in three passes. **Acquire:** what you need to learn, ranked by how much the strategy depends on it, and where each item can be sourced before the conversation rather than during it. **Protect:** what you hold that would cost you if disclosed — above all your reservation point and your deadline — with a prepared, honest, non-revealing answer for each of the three questions you least want asked. **Exchange:** the sequence of reciprocal disclosure, since information sharing is the mechanism by which trust and value creation actually get built, and refusing to share anything forecloses both.

This is the input to `at-the-table/negotiation_question_sequencing_live.md`, which handles live questioning once the conversation starts. This one governs what happens before, and sets the protection rules that hold throughout.

**When to use:**
- Before a negotiation where you know less than the counterpart — especially against a repeat player.
- You have been asked directly about your budget, deadline, or alternatives before and answered badly.
- The strategy rests on assumptions you have not verified.
- Value creation requires disclosure and you need to share without over-exposing.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need the live question sequence for the room itself — that is `at-the-table/negotiation_question_sequencing_live.md`.
- The negotiation is purely distributive, one-shot, and single-issue, where the only relevant information is the range — `negotiation_batna_analysis.md` covers it.
- You are modelling the counterpart's reasoning rather than planning information flow — `negotiation_counterpart_simulation.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals preparing for a negotiation where they are on the wrong side of an information gap.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The negotiation.** What is being negotiated, with whom, by when.
2. **Your current assumptions.** Everything the strategy rests on that you have not verified.
3. **What you hold.** Information about your position, constraints, alternatives, and timeline.
4. **What they likely hold.** Distributional data, comparable deals, internal budget, other bidders.
5. **Available sources.** People, documents, public data, prior deals, colleagues who have negotiated with them.
6. **Relationship horizon.** Whether a reputation for candour pays off later.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Rank information needs by **strategic dependence** — how much of the plan collapses if the assumption is wrong — not by how interesting or easy to obtain the item is.
- Distinguish what can be learned **before** the conversation from what can only be learned **in** it, and route each accordingly. Pre-negotiation research is cheaper and safer than live probing.
- Explicitly protect the **reservation point** and the **real deadline**. These two are the highest-value items you hold, and disclosure of either collapses your range.
- Prepare an **honest, non-revealing answer** for each protected item. Answers must be truthful; the plan is to decline gracefully or redirect, never to lie.
- Design **reciprocal disclosure**: what you offer first to open exchange, and what you require before going further.
- Name what you will **never volunteer**, and what triggers reconsidering that.
- Note where a disclosure creates a **record** — written channels, anything a counterpart may repeat to others, anything that sets precedent for future negotiations.

### Must Not
- Plan to lie. Beyond the ethical problem, discovered dishonesty destroys the deal and the relationship, and negotiators over-estimate how well fabrications survive follow-up questions.
- Refuse all disclosure. Total opacity blocks value creation, signals bad faith, and provokes reciprocal opacity — leaving both sides bargaining positionally in the dark.
- Answer a question about your budget or deadline with a number because a number was requested. The request does not create the obligation.
- Confuse "I can't share that" with "I won't." The former is a claim about authority that invites escalation; the latter is a boundary that ends the line of inquiry.
- Volunteer your constraints as rapport-building. Disclosed constraints become the counterpart's targets.
- Treat information from the counterpart as verified. Anything they tell you about their alternatives, budget, or deadline is a negotiating input, not a fact.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Audit the assumptions
List everything the current strategy depends on that has not been verified: their budget, their timeline, whether other bidders exist, whether the stated policy is real, who actually decides. Mark each with what breaks if it is wrong.

### Step 2 — Rank the acquisition list by strategic dependence
Order the list by how much of the plan collapses if the assumption fails. High-dependence items get research effort; low-dependence items get a question if convenient. Note the difference between *nice to know* and *load-bearing* explicitly — most information plans over-invest in the former.

### Step 3 — Route each item: research vs. ask
For each item, decide where it comes from. **Research** (before): public filings, published benchmarks, colleagues who have dealt with them, prior contracts, market data, their public commitments. **Ask** (during): anything only they know. Anything obtainable by research should never be spent as a question — questions are scarce and each one reveals what you care about.

### Step 4 — Inventory what you hold and classify it
Three categories. **Protected:** reservation point, real deadline, internal constraints, the weakness of your alternatives, how much you want this. **Tradeable:** information you would share in exchange for theirs — priorities, general timing, non-critical constraints. **Free:** things you can share at no cost that build goodwill and invite reciprocity — context, non-sensitive rationale, genuine appreciation of their position.

### Step 5 — Prepare answers to the three worst questions
Identify the three questions you least want asked — typically "what's your budget," "when do you need this by," and "what other options are you considering." For each, write a truthful answer that does not reveal the protected item. Working patterns: redirect to the standard ("I'd rather work from what the scope actually justifies — here's the benchmark I'm using"), name the boundary ("I'd rather not anchor on a number before we've scoped it"), or answer the adjacent question honestly ("I have flexibility on timing; what's driving yours?"). Rehearse them aloud — these are the answers most likely to collapse under pressure.

### Step 6 — Design the reciprocal exchange
Write the sequence: what you disclose first (from the Free category), what you expect back, and what you release from the Tradeable category once reciprocity is established. Set the stop condition — the point at which non-reciprocation means you stop disclosing. Reciprocal disclosure is the mechanism that makes value creation possible; it needs to be deliberate, not spontaneous.

### Step 7 — Set the never-volunteer rules and the record check
Write the short list of things you will not raise unprompted regardless of how the conversation goes, with the condition under which you would reconsider each. Then check each planned disclosure for record risk: is this in writing, will it be repeated to their colleagues, does it set a precedent for the next negotiation with them?

### Step 8 — Plan for their information tactics
Note the common extraction moves and your response: the casual pre-meeting question, the assumed-fact statement inviting correction ("so you're looking at end of quarter, right?"), the sympathetic invitation to share your constraints, and the direct budget question early. For each, the prepared move.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Which protected item are you most likely to disclose under friendly pressure, and why that one?
- What are you assuming about them that you have not sourced, and what happens if it is false?
- If they know everything on your protected list already, does the strategy still work?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Curiosity-ranked acquisition.** Researching what is interesting rather than what the strategy depends on. Rank by dependence, and accept that the most load-bearing item is often mundane.
2. **Spending questions on researchable facts.** Asking what a public filing or a colleague could have told you. Each question spends limited attention and signals what you care about; do not spend one on a fact you could have looked up.
3. **Reciprocal over-disclosure.** Matching their candour item for item without checking value. Sophisticated counterparts trade cheap information for expensive information, and the exchange feels balanced because the *count* is balanced.
4. **Budget-question compliance.** Answering "what's your budget?" with a number because it was asked directly. It is the single most valuable extraction in most negotiations, and the request creates no obligation.
5. **Constraint-sharing as rapport.** Volunteering your deadline or approval limits to seem open. Every disclosed constraint becomes a target — a known deadline is a lever they will use on the last day.
6. **Lying as a plan.** Building the plan on a fabrication. It fails under follow-up more often than expected, and its discovery costs the deal and the relationship. Protection is achieved by declining, not by inventing.
7. **Counterpart claims taken as facts.** Recording "they have two other bidders" as established. Everything they say about their alternatives and deadlines is a negotiating input to be tested, not evidence.
8. **Record blindness.** Disclosing in writing, or to someone who will repeat it, what you would only have said once in a room. Written disclosures persist into the next negotiation and set precedent.

## Verification

- [ ] Every strategic assumption listed with what breaks if it is wrong.
- [ ] Acquisition list ranked by strategic dependence, not by interest or ease.
- [ ] Each item routed to research or ask, with researchable items never assigned to questions.
- [ ] Holdings classified protected / tradeable / free, with reservation point and real deadline in protected.
- [ ] All three worst questions have truthful, non-revealing prepared answers.
- [ ] No prepared answer relies on a falsehood.
- [ ] Reciprocal exchange sequenced with an explicit stop condition.
- [ ] Never-volunteer list written with reconsideration conditions.
- [ ] Record check applied to each planned disclosure.
- [ ] Their likely extraction tactics anticipated with prepared responses.
- [ ] Adversarial check names the item most likely to slip and tests the strategy against full disclosure.
- [ ] No counterpart claim recorded as verified fact.
