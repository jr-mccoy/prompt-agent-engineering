# Daily Context Switch Protocol

**Objective:** For a person who switches between very different life modes during the day — caregiver to remote worker, student to employee, meeting mode to deep-work mode — produce a brief structured close protocol for the outgoing mode and an open protocol for the incoming one. Prevents the bleed of one mode's mental state into the next.

**When to use:** When you're about to switch from one substantially different role or mode to another and want to arrive in the next mode with the right mental state — not distracted by what you just left, not scrambling to remember where you were. Most useful when modes are separated by less than 30 minutes (no natural recovery gap).

**Audience:** People whose daily life requires playing substantially different roles in close succession — parents who also work from home, students with part-time jobs, caregivers who do administrative work, anyone alternating between meeting-heavy and deep-work-heavy blocks. Not for people transitioning between similar tasks within the same role (that's a focus-block tool). Not designed for the work-to-evening transition — use the End-of-Day Shutdown for that.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **The two modes.** What are you switching from and to? Be specific about the nature of each mode — not just "work" and "parenting" but what each demands of you mentally and emotionally. "Client-facing sales calls mode (high energy, persuasion, reactive)" vs. "deep analysis mode (slow, focused, no interruptions)" is more useful than "work to more work."

2. **What you're leaving (outgoing state).** What's currently in progress in the outgoing mode? Any open decisions, unresolved tensions, things you want to remember when you return? What was the last thing you were doing before this switch?

3. **What the incoming mode needs from you.** What does the next mode require you to arrive with? Examples: "Parent brain needs patience and present attention, not mental task lists." "Code review mode needs fresh eyes, not the frustration from the sales call." "Study mode needs focus, not the social residue of three hours of meetings."

4. **Available transition time.** How many minutes do you have between modes? "5 minutes," "10 minutes," "walking distance between locations" — this determines how long the protocols can be.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Define the Modes

Characterize each mode with three attributes:
- **Primary demand:** What it primarily asks of you (cognitive, emotional, social, physical)
- **Typical posture:** How your body and attention are oriented (reactive vs. focused, talking vs. listening, creating vs. evaluating)
- **Common residue:** What mental state tends to linger after time in this mode (task lists, emotional charge, interpersonal tension, creative absorption, decision fatigue)

This characterization is used to make the protocols relevant — a generic "take a deep breath" instruction does not serve someone transitioning from crisis management to infant care.

### Step 2 — Write the Close Protocol for the Outgoing Mode

The close protocol is 2–5 steps that take no more than 3 minutes. Its purpose: put the outgoing mode's mental state into a container so it doesn't leak into the next mode.

Elements of a close protocol:
- **Capture:** Write down or dictate one sentence about where you left off and what the next action is when you return. This offloads the "don't forget" mental loop.
- **Interrupt the posture:** A small physical shift that signals the mode is closing (stand up, close the laptop lid, leave the room, put the phone face-down). The physical signal matters — the brain uses environment as a context cue.
- **Name the unresolved tension (if any):** If there's an open emotional charge from the outgoing mode (a frustrating call, a difficult decision pending), name it in one sentence and set it aside deliberately: "That call was frustrating. I'll address it at 3pm." Unexpressed, it will persist.

### Step 3 — Write the Open Protocol for the Incoming Mode

The open protocol is 2–4 steps that take no more than 2 minutes. Its purpose: activate the incoming mode's mental state before you're in it, so you arrive ready rather than arriving and then spending 10 minutes ramping up.

Elements of an open protocol:
- **Mode-entry cue:** A consistent signal that marks the entry to this mode. Can be physical (putting on specific headphones, sitting in a specific chair), environmental (moving to a different room), or behavioral (opening the right app, reviewing the last note).
- **State the mode's primary goal:** One sentence about what this mode session is for. "The next two hours are for the proposal — everything else waits." This is not a full plan — it is an orientation.
- **Drop the previous mode explicitly:** One sentence acknowledging that the previous mode is closed. "The calls are done. That's not my job right now." This sounds trivial but interrupts residual attention.

### Step 4 — Calibrate to Available Transition Time

If transition time is under 5 minutes: trim close and open protocols to the two most essential steps each. Prioritize the capture step (close) and the mode-entry cue (open) as the minimum viable pair.

If transition time is 5–10 minutes: use the full protocols.

If transition time is over 10 minutes: the protocols can include a brief physical reset (short walk, water, face wash) as a natural separator between close and open steps.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Produce a separate close protocol and open protocol for each transition, not a single combined list
- Make each step specific to the two modes described — no generic mindfulness instructions
- Include a physical or environmental cue in both protocols (not just mental steps)
- Calibrate length to available transition time
- Include a "capture" step in the close protocol so open loops from the outgoing mode are externalized

### Must Not
- Produce a generic "transition checklist" that could apply to any mode switch
- Recommend meditation, breathing exercises, or journaling as the primary mechanism (these are slow and require a separate practice to be useful)
- Treat both modes as equivalent — the protocols should reflect the specific demands and residue of each mode
- Extend the protocols beyond what fits in the stated transition time
- Leave the open protocol as a mental list — it must include a physical or environmental entry cue

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The generic wellness trap:** "Take three deep breaths and reset your intention" is not a context switch protocol — it is a generic mindfulness instruction. The protocols must be specific to what was happening in the outgoing mode and what is required by the incoming mode.

2. **The symmetry trap:** The close protocol and open protocol are not mirror images. The close is about containment (putting things away); the open is about activation (priming the next state). They have different structures and different purposes.

3. **The overcomplicated protocol trap:** A 10-step close protocol that takes 12 minutes defeats the purpose. Context switch protocols must fit inside the transition window. If they don't, cut steps — the minimum viable pair is capture (close) and cue (open).

4. **The emotional-residue blindspot:** The most common failure of context switching is emotional residue — frustration from a difficult meeting leaking into a parent-child interaction, or interpersonal tension from caregiving leaking into analytical work. The close protocol must include explicit handling of any emotional charge, not just cognitive open loops.

5. **The physical-environment neglect trap:** Mental protocols without physical anchors are weak. The brain uses physical environment as a context cue — the same chair, the same desk, the same device configuration will re-trigger the previous mode's state. Require at least one environmental or physical signal in each protocol.

## Output Format

```
## Context Switch Protocol: [Outgoing Mode] → [Incoming Mode]

**Transition window:** [X minutes]

---

### Close Protocol — [Outgoing Mode] (~[X] minutes)

**1. Capture:** [What to write down or dictate before leaving this mode]
*Where to write it: [specific location — notebook, app, sticky note]*

**2. Interrupt the posture:** [Specific physical action that marks the close — close laptop, leave room, put phone away]

**3. Name the residue (if any):** [If there's an open emotional charge or decision pending, write it here]
*Set-aside statement: "[Specific sentence to say or think — e.g., 'The X situation is on hold until 3pm']"*

---

### Open Protocol — [Incoming Mode] (~[X] minutes)

**1. Mode-entry cue:** [Specific physical or environmental signal that activates this mode — sit in this chair, open this app, put on these headphones, move to this room]

**2. State the goal:** [One sentence: what this mode session is for]
*Example: "[Specific sentence calibrated to the incoming mode and the current session]"*

**3. Drop the previous mode:** [One sentence acknowledging the previous mode is closed]
*Example: "[Specific sentence — not generic, specific to what was just left]"*

---

### Minimum Viable Version (if transition time is under 3 minutes)

1. [Most essential close step]
2. [Most essential open step]
```

## Verification

- [ ] Close protocol and open protocol are separate and distinct
- [ ] Both protocols include at least one physical or environmental step
- [ ] Close protocol includes a "capture" step that externalizes open loops
- [ ] Open protocol includes a mode-entry cue (physical or environmental)
- [ ] Protocols are specific to the two modes described — not generic
- [ ] Total protocol time fits within stated transition window
- [ ] Emotional residue from the outgoing mode is addressed, not ignored
- [ ] A minimum viable version is provided for short transition windows
