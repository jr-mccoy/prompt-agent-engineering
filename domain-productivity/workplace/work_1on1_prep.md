# 1:1 Meeting Prep

**Objective:** Prepare a focused agenda for a one-on-one meeting — from either the manager's side or the direct report's side. The agenda surfaces what each person actually needs from the conversation, not just project status. The result is a 1:1 that feels different from a standup.

**When to use:** Before any scheduled 1:1 where you want the conversation to be more than a status exchange. Especially useful when a 1:1 has been feeling rote, when something has gone unsaid for too long, or when a relationship or work situation is at an inflection point.

**Audience:** Managers preparing to meet with their direct reports, and individual contributors preparing to meet with their manager. Both roles get different but complementary prep. Not for skip-level meetings, performance reviews (those have their own format), or first-time 1:1s between strangers (use an intro conversation format instead).

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Your role in the meeting.** Are you the manager or the direct report? This determines what items you bring and what you are trying to get from the conversation.

2. **Relationship age.** How long have you been having regular 1:1s with this person? New (under 3 months), established (3+ months), or long-standing (over a year). Newer relationships need more explicit context-building; established relationships can move faster into substance.

3. **What has happened since the last 1:1.** Key work events, completed milestones, setbacks, decisions made without the other person, things that surprised you, anything you said you'd do and either did or didn't.

4. **Topics that need to come up.** What can't leave this meeting without being addressed? Include both work topics (blockers, priorities, upcoming decisions) and any interpersonal or relationship topics (feedback to give or receive, a tension, an unspoken concern).

5. **What you need from this person.** What do you want to walk away with? A decision, their advice or input, feedback on your work or approach, visibility into something, emotional support after a hard week, or just a sense of their current state.

6. **Meeting length.** How long is the 1:1? (Typical: 30–60 minutes.) This determines how many items can realistically be covered.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Separate status from substance

A 1:1 is not a standup. Status updates (what's done, what's in progress) belong in asynchronous channels, project tools, or team standups — not in the 1:1. If the person across from you already knows what you're working on, don't repeat it.

Audit your tentative agenda items: for each item, ask "could this be an async message instead?" If yes, send it async and free up 1:1 time for things that require dialogue, trust, or nuance.

Items that belong in a 1:1:
- Blockers that require your manager's help to remove
- Career-related conversations (growth, development, trajectory, concerns)
- Feedback to give or receive
- Interpersonal dynamics that need direct conversation
- Decisions where your manager's input or approval is needed
- Priorities that need re-alignment
- How the person is actually doing (not just the work)

Items that should usually be moved async:
- Routine project status
- Updates that require no response
- Information that could be an email or Slack message

### Step 2 — Manager prep: shift from informer to coach

If you are the manager:

Your job in a 1:1 is not to deliver information to your report — it's to understand what they need and remove obstacles. Build your agenda around questions, not statements.

Specifically, prepare to ask about:
- What's blocking them that you could unblock?
- What's going well that you should amplify or protect?
- What are they unsure about that requires a decision or your input?
- How are they doing, honestly — energy, engagement, stress?
- What do they want to be working on or learning that they're not?
- Any feedback you need to give — and how to give it in a way that lands

Do not fill the 1:1 with your updates. If you have information to share, share it — but then ask what they think, not just whether they understand.

### Step 3 — Direct report prep: bring asks, not just updates

If you are the direct report:

Your job in a 1:1 is not to demonstrate that you are on top of things — it's to use the time your manager is giving you to get what you need. Prepare around three questions:

1. What do I need from my manager that I'm not getting? (Decisions, unblocking, feedback, visibility, support)
2. What am I worried about that my manager should know? (Risk, a relationship problem, a work situation that's getting harder)
3. What do I want to accomplish or grow toward that isn't happening? (Career, scope, skills)

Also: what commitments did you make in the last 1:1 and what happened? Brief your manager on your own follow-through before they ask.

### Step 4 — Handle the hard items

If there is a tension, unresolved conflict, or piece of feedback that has been sitting unsaid: plan specifically for when and how you will raise it. Hard items left until the last 5 minutes get rushed. Hard items raised first get real conversation time.

Decide:
- Which hard item (if any) is on your agenda?
- Where in the meeting will you raise it?
- How will you open that topic? Write the first sentence.

### Step 5 — Build the agenda

Assemble the items in order. The most important or sensitive item goes near the top — not at the end. Allocate rough time to each item without overscheduling. Leave room for the conversation to go where it needs to go.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Identify what you want to walk away with from the conversation (decision, feedback, support, alignment — be specific)
- Include at least one item that is not a status update
- Place the most important item near the top of the agenda, not the end
- If you have feedback to give, write the opening sentence before the meeting

### Must Not
- Fill the agenda with items that could be async messages
- Show up without having reflected on whether commitments from the last 1:1 were kept
- Leave the meeting without a clear next step for any item that raised an open question
- Use the 1:1 as the primary format for delivering project status that the other person doesn't already know

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The status-update 1:1:** Spending most of the time on project updates that belong in a tool or async message. After the meeting, both parties feel vaguely satisfied but nothing changed. Audit each agenda item: "Could this be async?" If yes, make it async.

2. **The manager monologue:** The manager fills the time with their own updates, strategic context, and observations, leaving little time for the direct report to raise what they need. A good 1:1 gives the direct report the majority of the airtime.

3. **The deferred hard topic:** A tension or issue that needs to be addressed gets bumped week after week because there's never "a good time." Name the hard topic, put it on the agenda, and commit to raising it this time.

4. **The vague ask:** Walking into a 1:1 wanting something but not being specific about what. "I need more support" is not an ask. "I need you to make the final call on the vendor decision by Friday so I can proceed" is an ask.

5. **The agenda without closure:** Items are raised but not resolved — no decision, no next step, no named owner. Before leaving each item, confirm: what was decided? Who does what next? When?

- [Key event or work development]
- [Commitment you made — and whether you kept it]

## Verification

- [ ] At least one item is not a status update
- [ ] The most important or sensitive item is near the top of the agenda
- [ ] Every agenda item has a named "want" — what you need from the conversation on that topic
- [ ] Commitments from the last 1:1 are reviewed and accounted for
- [ ] If there is a hard topic, it has a planned opening sentence and a position on the agenda
- [ ] The agenda items fit within the meeting duration with room for the conversation to breathe
- [ ] Manager prep includes at least two open questions to ask the report
- [ ] Direct report prep includes at least two specific asks of the manager
