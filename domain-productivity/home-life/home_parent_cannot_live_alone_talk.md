# When a Parent Can No Longer Live Alone

**Objective:** Get from "something has to change" to a change your parent has been part of
choosing — siblings aligned on the facts first, a conversation (usually several) that
starts from what your parent wants, money discussed honestly but within limits, and a move
or new arrangement sequenced so the first month goes well.

**When to Use:**
- Falls, missed medications, weight loss, unpaid bills, a hospital stay or a near-miss
  suggest living alone is no longer safe enough.
- You need to raise it with your parent and dread how it will go — or tried once and it
  ended badly.
- Siblings see the situation differently, and one of you is carrying it.
- An arrangement has been chosen and now needs to be carried out kindly.
- **Not this prompt if** the family has not yet worked out *which* arrangement fits the
  parent's care level — that decision is
  `domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_caring_for_aging_parent.md` (this
  prompt runs the conversations around it and the transition after it). Comparing specific
  facilities: `domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_care_facility_comparison.md`.
  Making the current home safer instead: `home_dementia_home_safety_plan.md`. Running care
  week to week afterwards: `home_family_caregiving_coordination.md`.

**Audience:** Adult children or other family. Not legal, financial or medical advice. If
there is suspected abuse, neglect, self-neglect or financial exploitation, contact adult
protective services `[VERIFY: the right body locally]`; if there is an emergency, call
emergency services — this plan comes after.

## Inputs / Context

1. **The evidence:** specific incidents with dates — falls, missed doses, food left to spoil,
   unpaid bills, getting lost, hospital visits — and who saw each.
2. **The parent:** what they have said about where and how they want to live; what matters
   most to them (their garden, the dog, church, independence, not being a burden); cognition —
   any diagnosis, and whether anyone has questioned their capacity to make this decision.
3. **Documents in place:** power of attorney, health-care proxy, advance directive — only
   whether they exist and who holds them, not their legal effect.
4. **Siblings and others:** who, where, what each believes, what each can give, old tensions.
5. **Options already shortlisted** (from the decision prompt or a care assessment).
6. **Money in outline:** whether the parent's income and assets broadly cover the options —
   no detailed figures needed here.

## Method

1. **Write the evidence log.** Dated, specific, observed: "fell in bathroom 3 Aug, lay on
   floor ~2 h" — not "she's declining". This log is shared with siblings and later, gently,
   with the parent. It also shows whether the concern is real or one person's anxiety.
2. **Hold the sibling pre-talk first (RP-02).** Agenda: agree the facts in the log; agree
   what you do *not* yet know (care-needs assessment, capacity); agree one or two spokespeople
   for the parent conversation; agree that no one presents a decision as already made.
   Disagreement about the solution is fine at this stage; disagreement about the facts must
   be resolved first.
3. **Settle the decision-maker question (DP-29).** Rank the values the family will use to
   break ties — usually the parent's own wishes, then safety, then family capacity, then cost.
   If the parent has capacity, **they decide**, including choices the family thinks are risky
   ("dignity of risk"). If capacity is genuinely in doubt, the next step is a clinician's
   assessment, and any use of a power of attorney or guardianship is a question for an
   elder-law attorney — not a family vote.
4. **Plan the conversation with your parent.** Setting: their home, a calm time of day, no more
   than two family members, no surprise audience. Open with their goals: "What matters most
   to you about living here?" and "What worries you?" Ask before telling. Then share one or
   two specific incidents from the log. Offer options, starting with the smallest change that
   addresses the risk (more help at home, a pendant alarm, a trial stay), not an ultimatum.
   Expect a series of conversations; the goal of the first is to be heard and to agree the
   problem, not to settle the answer.
5. **Script the hard responses (GT-09).** Write two replies each for: "I'm fine, stop
   fussing"; "You just want my money / my house"; "I'd rather die than go to a home"; anger
   or tears; agreeing and then forgetting (common with dementia — write down what was agreed
   and give them a copy). Raise each serious concern clearly *once* per conversation; then
   listen rather than repeat it louder.
6. **Keep the money conversation to facts.** Share what the options cost in outline and who
   pays now. Do not move, gift, or spend the parent's money without documented authority.
   Questions about benefit or programme eligibility, selling the house, gifting rules,
   look-back periods, and using a power of attorney go to an elder-law attorney and a
   financial adviser (`domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_estate_wishes_attorney_prep.md`,
   `domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_financial_decision_framework.md`;
   insurance enrolment: `home_medicare_insurance_enrollment_prep.md`).
7. **Offer a trial where possible (DP-11).** A respite stay, a month of daily home-care
   visits, or a fortnight living with a sibling is reversible and lets your parent judge it.
   Agree in advance what you will each look at afterwards.
8. **Sequence the transition.** Once agreed: who does what by when; downsizing with your parent
   choosing what comes (familiar furniture, photos, the chair); `home_moving_checklist.md` for
   the move itself; a 30-day settling plan — frequent short visits in week one, then the
   family's normal rhythm; a review at 30 and 90 days.
9. **Check for false agreement (QA-12).** A parent who goes quiet may be overwhelmed, not
   agreeing; siblings who say yes in the meeting may not show up. Confirm in writing afterwards.

## Output Format

```
# Living-alone plan — [parent], [age], [current situation]
## Evidence log | Date | What happened | Seen by |
## Sibling pre-talk: agreed facts · unknowns · spokespeople · tie-break values (ranked)
## Decision-maker: [parent with capacity / capacity in question → assessment + attorney]
## Conversation plan: setting · who · opening questions · incidents to share · options to offer
## Hard responses: | They say | Reply A | Reply B |
## Money: facts to share · questions routed to [attorney / adviser]
## Trial (if any): what, how long, what we'll look at
## Transition timeline · 30-day settling plan · review dates
```

## Verification

- [ ] Evidence log has dates and observers, not impressions.
- [ ] Siblings agree facts before anyone proposes a solution to the parent.
- [ ] The parent's right to decide is stated; capacity questions go to a clinician and attorney.
- [ ] Opening questions ask about the parent's goals before presenting concerns.
- [ ] At least one option is a smaller or reversible step.
- [ ] No money is moved or promised without documented authority; legal and benefit questions routed.
- [ ] Transition has owners, dates, and 30/90-day reviews.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The ambush meeting.** Five relatives arriving with a brochure produces refusal; two people,
   their home, their goals first.
2. **Treating agreement in the room as a decision.** Write it down; revisit it.
3. **Capacity assumed from age or diagnosis.** A diagnosis is not incapacity; ask a clinician.
4. **One sibling's anxiety as evidence.** The dated log settles whether the risk is real.
5. **Money silence.** Avoiding cost breeds suspicion later; share facts, route the rules.
6. **All-or-nothing options.** "Home or a home" ignores the steps in between.
7. **Ending support at move-in.** The first month is when people decline or settle; plan it.

```
# Living-alone plan — Dad (Walt, 86), widowed, lives alone in a two-storey house

## Evidence log
| 3 Aug | fell on stairs, bruised hip, didn't call anyone for 6 h | Lena (found him) |
| 20 Aug | 9 days of tablets still in the organiser | Lena |
| 11 Sep | electricity shut-off notice; 3 bills unopened | Mark |
| 28 Sep | lost 4 kg since spring (GP weighed) | GP letter |

## Sibling pre-talk (video, 30 Sep): facts agreed; unknown — GP thinks no dementia but
  memory clinic referral pending; spokespeople Lena + Mark; tie-break: Dad's wishes > safety
  > Lena's capacity (she does 12 h/wk) > cost. Tom (abroad) takes bills + phone calls.
## Decision-maker: Dad (capacity not in question per GP); Mark holds POA — not used.
## Conversation (Sun 6 Oct, Dad's kitchen, Lena + Mark): "Dad, what matters most about
  this house?" → "What worries you about the winter?" → share 3 Aug fall + bills only.
  Options: A) carer visits 7 mornings + pendant alarm, bedroom moved downstairs;
  B) 2-week respite stay at Willow Court; C) move to Lena's (she has offered).
## Hard responses: "You want the house" → "It stays yours. We want you safe in it if we can."
## Money: Dad's pension covers option A's visits; Willow Court costs routed to elder-law
  attorney (Mark books 15 Oct) — no talk of selling the house until then.
## Trial: option A for 6 weeks; review 20 Nov: falls, meals eaten, how Dad feels.
```

## Techniques Used

- **RP-02 Audience-Specific Framing** — separate framing for the sibling pre-talk and for the parent.
- **DP-29 Value Hierarchy Construction** — ranked tie-break values, with the parent's wishes first.
- **GT-09 Flag-Once-Then-Defer** — raise each concern once, then listen; the capable parent decides.
- **DP-11 Safe Experiment Design** — reversible trials before permanent moves.
- **QA-12 False Positives Identification** — silence or a yes in the room mistaken for agreement.
