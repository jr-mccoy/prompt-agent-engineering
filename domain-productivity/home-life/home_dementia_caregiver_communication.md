# Talking With a Parent Who Has Dementia

**Objective:** Replace arguing, correcting and quizzing with responses that lower distress
— a playbook for the four situations that wear carers down most, a shared phrase card so
every family member responds the same way, and a clear line where a behaviour stops being
a communication problem and becomes a call to the care team.

**When to Use:**
- A parent or relative has a dementia diagnosis (or is under assessment) and conversations
  keep ending in frustration, tears or arguments.
- They ask the same question every few minutes, or ask for a husband, wife or parent who has died.
- They refuse a shower, a tablet, a change of clothes or getting into the car.
- Late afternoons and evenings bring pacing, "I want to go home" (while at home) or agitation.
- Several relatives visit and each responds differently, which confuses the person more.
- **Not this prompt if** you are trying to work out *whether* it is dementia, depression or
  something else — that is a clinical differential
  (`domain-psychology/populations/geriatric/psychology_geriatric_depression_vs_dementia.md`)
  for their doctor. If the home itself is unsafe (wandering, stove, driving, falls) use
  `home_dementia_home_safety_plan.md`. If *you* are running on empty, start with
  `domain-psychology/client-self-use/specialty/clientself_caregiver_burnout_plan.md`.
  Rotas and sibling coordination: `home_family_caregiving_coordination.md`.

**Audience:** An adult child, spouse or other family carer. Communication coaching only —
it does not diagnose, change medication, or replace the care team's instructions.

## Inputs / Context

1. **The person:** relationship, age, diagnosis as given by their clinician (type and stage
   if known), hearing and eyesight, first language, what they did for work, what they love.
2. **The situations that recur:** the exact words they use, time of day, how often, what you
   currently say and what happens next.
3. **Who is involved:** who lives with them, who visits, paid carers.
4. **Family stance on "therapeutic fibs"** — some families are comfortable with gentle
   untruths to avoid distress, some are not. This is a values choice, not a technique choice.
5. **Recent changes:** anything new in the last two weeks (illness, medication change, a fall,
   a move, a hospital stay, constipation, poor sleep). Needed for the red-flag check.

## Method

1. **Run the red-flag check first.** Before coaching any response, ask whether the behaviour
   is *new or suddenly worse over hours or days*. Sudden confusion, drowsiness, hallucinations,
   fever, new incontinence, or a sharp change after a medication change can be **delirium**
   (often from infection, dehydration, constipation, pain or medication) — a medical problem
   that needs the doctor the same day, not a better script. Route these out before Step 2.
2. **Set the ground rules (DS-01).** Apply the shared principles of person-centred dementia
   communication, drawn from Naomi Feil's Validation method and Teepa Snow's Positive
   Approach to Care: do not argue, correct or quiz ("Don't you remember?"); respond to the
   emotion, not the factual error; approach from the front at eye level and say who you are;
   one short sentence and one question at a time; offer two choices, not open questions; wait
   10–15 seconds for an answer before repeating.
3. **Read the feeling behind the words (AG-10).** For each recurring situation, name the
   likely need: "When are we going home?" is often *I feel unsafe or out of place*; "Where is
   Frank?" is often *I feel alone*; refusing a shower is often *I'm cold, embarrassed, or don't
   understand what's happening*. The response targets the need.
4. **Build the situation playbook (IT-22).** For each situation: trigger, likely feeling,
   what to say (two options), what to avoid, what to do if it escalates. Cover at minimum:
   - **Repeated questions** — answer calmly as if for the first time; then move to the
     feeling ("You're keen to know when Sarah's coming — you love her visits"); write the answer
     on a whiteboard or card they can re-read; look for a pattern (hunger, toilet, boredom).
   - **Asking for someone who has died** — avoid re-announcing the death each time, which can
     make them grieve as if it were new. Join them: "You're thinking about Frank. Tell me about
     him." If they ask directly "Is he dead?", answer gently and briefly, and stay with the
     feeling. How far to go along with "he's at work" is the family's stated stance (input 4).
   - **Refusing care** — stop, do not force; leave and come back in 15–20 minutes; try a
     different person; change the wording ("let's freshen up before lunch" not "you need a
     shower"); check pain, cold, fear of falling, modesty (towel over shoulders, warm room).
     Medication refusal is logged and reported to the prescriber — never hidden in food or
     drink without the prescriber's explicit instruction.
   - **Late-day agitation (sundowning)** — shape the afternoon: daylight and activity earlier,
     lights on before dusk, curtains closed against reflections, quieter TV, caffeine before
     midday only, a predictable evening routine; note what precedes episodes.
5. **Write the family phrase card.** One page with the five phrases everyone uses and the
   three things nobody says. Consistency across visitors matters more than any single phrase.
6. **Dual-failure check (QA-20).** Each response must avoid *both* failures: cold correction
   that causes distress, and deception or infantilising that strips dignity. Speak to them as
   an adult; no baby talk; involve them in choices they can still make.
7. **Name the escalation routes.** Sudden change → same-day doctor or urgent care; aggression
   that risks injury, a fall with pain, or not drinking for a day → urgent help; going missing
   → emergency services; your own exhaustion → the burnout plan and respite.

## Output Format

```
# Communication playbook — [person], [diagnosis as given], from [date]
Family stance on gentle untruths: [stated]   Red-flag check: [clear / ROUTED: reason]

## Ground rules (everyone)
## Situation playbook
| Situation (their words) | Likely feeling | Say (option A / B) | Avoid | If it escalates |

## Afternoon/evening plan (if sundowning)
## Family phrase card — 5 phrases to use · 3 never to say
## This is NOT a communication problem — call the care team if:
## Log for the next appointment (date · what happened · what helped)
```

## Verification

- [ ] Red-flag check done first; sudden change routed to a clinician the same day.
- [ ] Every playbook row names a feeling, two phrasings, an "avoid", and an escalation step.
- [ ] No response argues, corrects, quizzes or uses baby talk.
- [ ] The family's stance on gentle untruths is stated and respected, not imposed.
- [ ] Medication refusal is logged and reported; no hiding medicine without prescriber instruction.
- [ ] The phrase card fits one page.
- [ ] Carer strain is routed, not ignored.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Delirium mistaken for "the dementia getting worse".** A change over hours or days is a
   medical red flag; scripts are not the fix.
2. **Correcting as kindness.** "Mum, Dad died in 2023" feels honest but makes many people grieve
   afresh each time. Truth-telling is a family value to weigh, not a rule.
3. **One magic phrase.** No phrase works every time; the playbook gives two options and a
   step-back.
4. **Forcing care "for their own good".** Physical insistence escalates fear and causes injuries;
   step back and re-approach.
5. **Assuming behaviour is deliberate.** "She does it to annoy me" is rarely true; look for unmet
   needs (pain, toilet, hunger, boredom, noise).
6. **Inconsistent visitors.** One sibling correcting and another redirecting confuses the person —
   hence the shared card.
7. **Clinical advice by the back door.** Questions about sedatives, antipsychotics or dose timing
   go to the prescriber.

## Situation playbook
| "Where's Margaret?" (most evenings, ~5 pm) | lonely, unsettled | A: "You're thinking about
  Margaret. What was she like when you met?" B: "You miss her. Shall we look at the wedding

  arm, "I miss her too" — then tea |

| Refuses shower (Mon/Thu) | cold, embarrassed | A: "Let's warm up before your barber

  in 20 min; on 2nd refusal do a sink wash and log it |
| "I want to go home" (4–7 pm, at home) | out of place, tired | "Tell me about home. What do

  from the 1960s, call Jo if it continues |

## Afternoon plan: walk 2 pm; lamps on 4 pm; news off; decaf only after 12; dinner 5:30
## Phrase card: "I'm Sam, your son." · "Tell me about…" · "Let's do it together." ·
  "You're safe; I'm here." · "Shall we try later?" | Never: "Don't you remember?" ·

## Techniques Used

- **AG-10 Emotional Context Spectrum** — each response adapts to the feeling behind the words.
- **IT-22 Workflow Decision Matrix** — situation → response → escalation playbook table.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — Validation and Positive Approach to Care principles applied consistently.
- **QA-20 Dual-Failure Quality Test** — neither cold correction nor dignity-stripping deception.
