**Purpose:** Audit family language for the fixed-vs-growth framing — without falling into the early-Dweck oversimplification — and surface specific patterns to change without making the household feel like a behaviorist lab.

**When to use:** Caregiver wants to shift family language; observed fixed-mindset pattern in kid (refusing to try things, "I'm bad at X" identity); high-achievement-pressure household; first attempt at this work; revising after attempt that didn't land; co-parents disagreeing on praise / language norms.

**When NOT to use:** Acute mental-health concern (handle that first; language work is medium-term).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Mindset is real but the early framing oversimplified.** Dweck's later work (especially since 2017) has corrected: praising effort that didn't produce learning is hollow; the real shift is praising strategies, problem-solving, and the link between effort and progress.
2. **Mindset is about how struggle is interpreted, not about constant cheerleading.** A growth-mindset family doesn't pretend everything is great. They treat hard things as workable, mistakes as data, and identity as in-process.
3. **Family-language pattern matters more than individual statements.** What the household says about ability, struggle, mistakes, and identity over time shapes the kid's framing.
4. **Both caregivers and kids are in the audit.** Caregivers' self-talk, talk about other adults, and talk about the kid's siblings all communicate framing.
5. **Hollow growth-talk is worse than nothing.** Kids detect performative "everyone can do anything!" as fake.
6. **Identity language is specifically high-leverage.** "I am the kind of person who…" lands differently than "I am [trait]."

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — The Updated Frame

The early-Dweck story:
- Praising "smart" → fixed mindset → fragile under failure.
- Praising "effort" → growth mindset → resilient.

The corrections (Dweck's own later work):
- Hollow effort-praise (praising effort that didn't produce learning) becomes a "consolation prize" and backfires.
- The real lever is praising strategies, problem-solving, asking for help, persistence linked to outcome.
- "Yet" matters — "I can't do this" → "I can't do this yet" — but only when the path to the next yet is real.
- Mindset interventions in schools don't always replicate; the family-context version still has support.
- Some kids need different language than others (gifted kids especially struggle with simple effort-praise; autistic kids need concrete language; anxious kids need calibration).

The cleaner frame: **process language, struggle-as-information, identity-in-progress.**

### Stage 2 — Audit Domains

Conduct the audit across:

**Caregiver self-talk:**
- "I'm so bad at math."
- "I never could draw."
- "I'm not a creative person."
- These statements model fixed identity to kids.

**Talk about kid:**
- "He's just not athletic."
- "She's the smart one."
- "He's always been shy."
- These cement traits.

**Talk about siblings (comparison):**
- "Why can't you be more like your sister?"
- "Your brother never had this trouble."
- These cement traits and damage sibling relationship.

**Talk about other adults:**
- "Daddy's not good with feelings."
- "Grandma was never a reader."
- Models fixed-trait framing about adults too.

**Response to kid struggling:**
- "It's okay, you're just not good at this."
- "Let me do it for you."
- "You don't have to."
- All teach: difficulty = stop.

**Response to kid succeeding:**
- "You're so smart!"
- "You're a natural!"
- "I knew you could do it!"
- All teach: success = trait, not process.

### Stage 3 — Caregiver Self-Audit

Track for one week:

- Statements about your own abilities (fixed or growth?).
- Statements about kid's abilities.
- Statements about other adults' abilities.
- Response to kid's struggle.
- Response to kid's success.
- Sibling comparison.

Note patterns. Most caregivers find at least 2–3 fixed-language patterns they didn't know about.

### Stage 4 — Replacement Language

Common fixed → growth replacements:

**About self:**
- "I'm bad at math" → "Math has always been hard for me; I've gotten better at some pieces."
- "I'm not creative" → "I haven't done a lot of creative work; I could try."
- "I'm not a runner" → "I haven't run in a long time."

**About kid (success):**
- "You're so smart!" → "You worked through that. What was the hardest part?"
- "You're a natural!" → "You've been doing this for a while; it's getting smoother."
- "Beautiful drawing!" → "I see you used a lot of yellow. Tell me about it."

**About kid (struggle):**
- "You're not good at this" → "This is hard. What are you going to try next?"
- "Let me do it" → "What's stuck? Want me to show you the next part, or want to figure it out?"
- "You don't have to" → "It's hard; you don't have to be good at it now. What's one piece we could work on?"

**About sibling comparison:**
- "Why can't you be more like your sister?" → [Don't compare. Talk to each kid about their own arc.]
- "Your brother got this at your age" → [Don't compare.]

**Identity statements:**
- "You're shy" → "It can take you a minute to warm up to new people."
- "You're not athletic" → "You haven't found the sport yet that fits."
- "She's the smart one" → "She enjoys school. He enjoys [other domain]."

### Stage 5 — Authentic vs. Hollow Growth Language

**Hollow:**
- "Everyone can do anything!"
- "Just believe in yourself!"
- "Failure is just a stepping stone!"
- Doesn't account for real difficulty, real difference in interest or capacity.

**Authentic:**
- "This is really hard. Let's figure out what to try next."
- "You worked on that for an hour. What did you learn?"
- "You're stuck. Stuck is okay. What's the next small step?"
- Acknowledges difficulty; surfaces process; honors struggle.

The difference: authentic growth language treats reality as the starting point.

### Stage 6 — When the Issue Isn't Mindset

Sometimes a kid's "I can't do this" is:

- A learning disability that hasn't been evaluated.
- Anxiety that's making the situation feel impossible.
- A sensory issue.
- Boredom (material below level).
- Burnout / exhaustion.
- Family stress.
- Trauma response.

Mindset language doesn't fix these. Address the actual driver.

The mindset trap: turning everything into a mindset problem when it's actually a structural one.

### Stage 7 — Process Language Examples

Across activities:

**Academic struggle:**
- "What strategy did you try?"
- "What's another way to approach this?"
- "Where did it get stuck?"
- "What part feels hard, and what part is okay?"

**Sport / physical:**
- "What are you working on right now?"
- "What did your coach say?"
- "What's getting better; what's still hard?"

**Creative work:**
- "Tell me what you're trying."
- "What's working?"
- "What would you do differently?"

**Social struggle:**
- "What were you trying to do?"
- "What worked, what didn't?"
- "What might you try next time?"

**General failure:**
- "What did this teach you?"
- "What would you do differently?"
- "What's the next step?"

### Stage 8 — Mistakes as Data

Family culture around mistakes is a key mindset signal.

- Caregiver names own mistakes openly: "I made a mistake at work today. Here's what I'm going to do differently."
- Mistakes don't trigger shame or blame from caregiver.
- Kid's mistakes are debriefed for learning, not punished.
- Success and mistakes both get specific attention.
- "What did you learn from that?" lands.

### Stage 9 — Identity Language Specifically

Identity language is high-leverage in 9–18.

**Fixed identity:**
- "I'm bad at math."
- "I'm shy."
- "I'm not a reader."
- These crystallize.

**In-progress identity:**
- "Math is hard for me right now."
- "I'm working on speaking up in groups."
- "I've been getting into reading lately."
- Open to change.

**Strengths-identity (when authentic):**
- "I'm someone who keeps going when it's hard."
- "I'm someone who tells the truth."
- "I'm someone who notices when others are left out."
- These are character-trait statements that build identity in a useful direction.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

- **Gifted kids:** acutely vulnerable to fixed-trait praise; very high-leverage to shift to process language; "smart" trap is real.
- **Autistic kids:** literal language; concrete examples; identity-affirming (don't try to growth-mindset away autism itself).
- **ADHD kids:** process language helps with the "I can't focus" identity trap; address EF directly too.
- **Anxious kids:** "yet" language and small-step framing reduce anxiety load.
- **Trauma history:** identity-affirming with care; "you are someone who survived" can be true and strong.
- **Teen with consolidated fixed identity:** slow shift; arguments backfire; model and let it land.
- **Caregiver own fixed-mindset history:** the work is also the caregiver's.
- **Cultural context:** some cultures emphasize humility differently; integrate growth-mindset with cultural framing.
- **Co-parents disagreeing:** less is more (one growth-language caregiver beats none); don't fight about it in front of kids.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Use early-Dweck "always praise effort" | Strategy + process + linked outcome |
| Use hollow growth-talk | Authentic acknowledges real difficulty |
| Treat mindset as the answer to every struggle | Sometimes it's LD, anxiety, sensory, burnout |
| Skip caregiver self-audit | Caregiver patterns model the most |
| Use comparison between siblings | Damages relationship and identity |
| Use trait-fixing language about kid | "You're shy" cements |
| Use trait-fixing language about self | Models fixed-identity |
| Pretend everyone can do anything | Honors reality of difficulty / variance |
| Use "yet" hollowly without real path | "Yet" lands when the path is real |
| Override identity-affirming for ND kids | Don't growth-mindset away autism |
| Lecture about mindset | Model in language |
| Punish kid for fixed statements | Reflect, offer, model |
| Skip the "what did you learn" debrief on mistakes | High-leverage |
| Treat one shift as the project | Pattern over time |
| Fight with co-parent about it in front of kids | Model your own language |
| Use mindset to push kid past genuine no | Honor real limits |
