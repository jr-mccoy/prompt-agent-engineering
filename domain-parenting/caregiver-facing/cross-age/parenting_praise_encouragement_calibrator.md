**Purpose:** Help a caregiver use praise and encouragement that lands well across the developmental arc and across kid profiles — without falling into "praise junkie" patterns, hollow effort-praise, or fixed-trait-praise that backfires under failure.

**When to use:** Designing feedback patterns for a young child; calibrating after noticing a "praise junkie" or "fragile-confidence" pattern; ND kid for whom standard praise lands oddly; teen for whom over-praise has become noise; recurring underperformance after praise; specific high-stakes feedback moment.

**When NOT to use:** Acute mental-health crisis or trauma response (handle that first; praise calibration is medium-term).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Praise and encouragement are different.** Praise is evaluative ("you did great"). Encouragement is descriptive and process-oriented ("you kept going when it got hard"). Both have their place; default to encouragement.
2. **Effort-praise alone is not the answer.** Dweck's later work corrected the early oversimplification: praising effort that didn't produce learning is hollow; praising strategies, problem-solving, and the link between effort and progress is the real lever.
3. **Praise the controllable.** What the kid did, chose, tried, persisted at — controllable. What the kid is — not controllable. Stable-trait praise ("you're so smart") sets up "I'm a smart kid; if I struggle, I'm not smart") fragility.
4. **Authenticity matters more than technique.** Hollow praise — even well-formed — lands as hollow. Specific, observed, sincere is the floor.
5. **Less is often more.** Constant praise becomes noise. Specific, occasional, well-placed praise lands.
6. **Different kids need different praise patterns.** Autistic kids may experience some praise as intrusive or confusing; ADHD kids may become praise-dependent; anxious kids may experience evaluative praise as performance pressure; gifted kids may dismiss generic praise.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Praise vs. Encouragement Distinction

**Praise (evaluative):**
- "Great job."
- "You're so smart."
- "Beautiful drawing."
- Positions caregiver as judge.
- Kid's worth tied to caregiver's evaluation.

**Encouragement (descriptive / process):**
- "You kept going when it was hard."
- "I see you tried three different ways before that worked."
- "You used a lot of yellow in this part."
- Positions kid as the agent.
- Kid's worth tied to their own experience.

Default to encouragement. Reserve praise for moments where evaluative warmth is the right note.

### Stage 2 — The Effort-vs-Trait Frame (Updated)

**Don't:** "You're so smart."
- Sets up fixed-trait identity. When struggle comes, "I'm not smart anymore."

**Don't:** "Great job working hard." (When effort didn't produce learning.)
- Hollow. Kid knows the work didn't go anywhere. Effort-praise without substance becomes noise.

**Do:** "You tried [specific strategy]. Did it work? What about trying [different angle]?"
- Strategy-praise + redirection.

**Do:** "I noticed you stuck with that even after the first try didn't work. That's how the second try got you somewhere."
- Effort + linked outcome.

**Do:** "You figured out a different way to solve it."
- Process-praise.

**Sometimes do:** "You're really kind." (Trait praise on character traits, occasionally, when accurate.)
- Identity-building when authentic.

### Stage 3 — Praise the Controllable, Honor the Uncontrollable

What the kid controls:
- Choice (chose to try, chose to keep going, chose to ask for help).
- Effort (worked at it, came back to it).
- Strategy (tried different approaches).
- Recovery (handled disappointment well).
- Generosity / kindness / honesty (chose those).

What the kid doesn't control:
- Talent / smart / pretty / fast.
- Outcome (especially when luck or unfair contest involved).
- Other people's praise.

When the uncontrollable shows up beautifully, you can honor without trait-fixing:
- "You finished first." (Outcome named.)
- "I love how you look in that." (Appreciation.)
- "You have a good ear for that." (Observation; can be hollow if overdone.)

### Stage 4 — Specific, Observed, Sincere

The floor for praise that lands:

- **Specific:** what specifically.
- **Observed:** something the caregiver actually saw.
- **Sincere:** caregiver actually means it.

Compare:
- "Great job!" (Generic.)
- "I saw you spent twenty minutes figuring out how to make that block balance. That's real persistence." (Specific, observed, sincere.)

Kids — especially older — calibrate caregiver praise quickly. Hollow praise gets discounted. Specific praise lands.

### Stage 5 — Age-Tiered Application

**Ages 2–4:**
- More encouragement than praise.
- Connection-oriented: "Look at that!" without evaluation.
- Avoid heavy "good girl / good boy."

**Ages 4–8:**
- Specific descriptive praise lands well.
- Avoid trait-praise as default.
- Praise effort + strategy + outcome link.
- Honor character (kindness, honesty, generosity) when authentic.

**Ages 9–12:**
- Tweens detect hollow praise.
- Specific, observed, fewer-but-deeper.
- Praise about emerging values and identity matters.
- Avoid public praise that embarrasses.

**Ages 13–18:**
- Teens often dismiss praise; the relationship is more important than the praise itself.
- Process-praise: "You handled that hard moment well."
- Specific recognition of who they're becoming (when relevant).
- Don't oversell; teens calibrate against reality.

### Stage 6 — Praise Dependence (Praise Junkie)

Pattern:
- Kid seeks praise constantly.
- Performance is for praise, not for the activity.
- Activity stops when praise stops.
- Asks "is this good?" repeatedly.

Intervention:
- Reduce evaluative praise; increase descriptive encouragement.
- Reflect the question back: "What do you think?"
- Notice the activity itself, not the kid's quality.
- Time when kid does the activity without praise; build internal reward.
- Don't withdraw warmth — withdraw evaluative pressure.

### Stage 7 — Fragile-Under-Failure (Trait-Praise Backfire)

Pattern:
- Kid was told they were smart / talented / a natural.
- Encounters something hard; refuses to engage.
- Says "I'm not good at this" and disengages.
- Shame-avoidance.

Intervention:
- Stop trait-praise.
- Shift to strategy and process language.
- Normalize struggle as how learning works.
- Talk about the brain growing through challenge (age-appropriate).
- Don't rescue by removing the hard task; do scaffold it.
- Caregiver own talk about struggle / mistakes models the move.

### Stage 8 — Praise Dismissal (Teen / Older Kid Edition)

Pattern:
- Caregiver praises; kid dismisses or rolls eyes.
- Praise has lost meaning.

Intervention:
- Less is more. Stop blanket-praising.
- Specific, infrequent, sincere.
- Trust the kid's own internal evaluation more visibly.
- Notice without commenting evaluatively: "I saw you finished that draft."
- Acknowledgment of effort or character without "great job" language.
- Sometimes: silent presence is the praise.

### Stage 9 — Adaptations Across Profiles

**Autistic kids:**
- Some autistic kids find generic enthusiastic praise ("good job!" with intonation) overwhelming, intrusive, or confusing.
- Lower-affect, descriptive language often lands better: "I see you finished the puzzle."
- Specific, factual.
- Avoid praise mid-task (interrupts focus).
- Honor special interests directly.
- Some autistic kids do enjoy explicit praise; calibrate to the kid.

**ADHD kids:**
- Praise dependency risk if overused (dopamine-loop).
- Frequent specific feedback for skill-building, but variable enough to not become noise.
- Praise the act of getting started (often the hardest part).
- Praise the strategy, not just outcome.

**Anxious kids:**
- Evaluative praise can feel like performance pressure.
- Process and effort safer than outcome.
- Reduce comparison.
- Acknowledge struggle: "That was hard, and you stayed with it."

**Gifted kids:**
- "You're so smart" backfires acutely; setup for fragility.
- Honor process and emerging mastery.
- Acknowledge boredom as a real signal, not "you're so advanced."

**Trauma history:**
- Praise can feel destabilizing (attention is dangerous in their history).
- Calibrate to relationship safety.
- Quiet acknowledgment often safer than loud praise.

**Performer / external-validation profile:**
- Performance teen who lives for praise: gradual reduction of evaluative; increase intrinsic-engagement language.

**Strong-willed:**
- Praise can be received as control; lower-affect, autonomy-respecting.

**Teen post-rupture:**
- Praise after blowup can feel performative; warmth without evaluation often better.

### Stage 10 — Caregiver Self-Audit

Track yourself for a week:

- How often praise vs. encouragement?
- Trait-praise vs. process-praise?
- Specific vs. generic?
- One-kid-vs-other?
- Public vs. private?
- After-failure response: rescue, evaluate, or normalize-and-scaffold?

Most caregivers have patterns they don't know about. The audit is the first lever.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to "always praise effort" | Praise strategy + linked outcome; effort alone is hollow |
| Use generic "good job!" as default | Specific, observed, sincere |
| Praise fixed traits (smart, talented, pretty) habitually | Process and choice |
| Use praise as constant background | Less is often more |
| Use same praise pattern for all kids | Profile-calibrated |
| Use praise to manipulate behavior | Erodes meaning |
| Withdraw praise punitively | Recalibrate, don't weaponize |
| Treat autistic kid's praise-confusion as ungrateful | Lower-affect descriptive often better |
| Skip the trait-praise backfire conversation for gifted kid | High-fragility risk |
| Dismiss kid's discomfort with praise | Real signal |
| Confuse "praise less" with "be cold" | Warmth in many forms |
| Skip caregiver self-audit | Patterns invisible without |
| Praise teen the way you praised them at 5 | Recalibrate by age |
| Public-praise in front of siblings always | Sibling dynamics |
| Treat hollow praise as harmless | Discounted; reduces caregiver credibility |
| Skip after-failure language design | Failure is the highest-leverage moment |
