**Purpose:** Help a caregiver design consequences that are connected, proportional, and actually work — distinguishing natural, logical, and imposed consequences, and avoiding the punishment-spiral pattern.

**When to use:** Recurring behavior the caregiver wants to address; first time setting up consequence for a new behavior; revising a punishment-spiral pattern; teen pushing limits; younger kid not learning from current consequence; pre-loading the consequence before the behavior happens.

**When NOT to use:** Acute safety crisis (act first; consequence design later). Behavior that signals mental-health, learning, or trauma issue (driver work first; consequence is downstream).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Three kinds of consequences exist.** Natural (what happens without intervention), logical (caregiver-imposed but tied to the behavior), and imposed (caregiver-imposed and unrelated to the behavior). Effectiveness varies.
2. **Natural is often the most powerful — when it's safe.** Cold without coat = cold. Tired in school after late bedtime = tired in school. Getting natural consequences shapes future behavior with no caregiver-relationship cost.
3. **When natural is unsafe or asymmetric, logical works.** Caregiver-designed consequence connected to the behavior.
4. **Imposed consequences (unrelated to behavior) erode trust.** "You forgot your homework, no screens for a week" connects nothing.
5. **Proportional matters.** A consequence too large breaks the relationship; too small isn't a signal.
6. **Punishment-spiral is the failure mode.** Behavior → punishment → resentment → more behavior → bigger punishment → deeper relationship damage. The exit is functional analysis, not bigger punishments.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — The Three Kinds

**Natural consequence:**
- What happens without caregiver intervention.
- Examples: not wearing coat = cold; not eating dinner = hungry; not doing homework = poor grade; not setting alarm = late; not putting bike away = bike rained on.
- Lever: caregiver doesn't intervene to prevent it (within safety).
- Power: high; no relationship cost.

**Logical consequence:**
- Caregiver-imposed, but connected to the behavior.
- Examples: drew on wall = help clean wall; broke sister's toy = repay or replace; lied about whereabouts = lose trust on whereabouts (specific to that domain); didn't put dishes away = no dishes available next meal.
- Lever: caregiver designs the link.
- Power: moderate-to-high when truly connected.

**Imposed consequence:**
- Caregiver-imposed, not connected.
- Examples: bad grade = no screens for a month; rude to sibling = grounded; missed curfew = no friend over.
- Lever: caregiver wields authority.
- Power: low; high relationship cost.

Default to natural; use logical where natural fails; reserve imposed for very specific cases (and even then, often not the right intervention).

### Stage 2 — When Natural Works

Conditions:

- Safety is okay (kid won't be seriously harmed).
- Harm is to the kid, not to others.
- Kid has developmental capacity to connect cause and effect.
- The natural consequence is timely enough to register.
- Caregiver can resist rescuing.

Examples:
- Kid refuses lunch → kid is hungry by 3.
- Kid won't wear coat → kid is cold (then puts on coat).
- Kid won't do homework → poor grade.
- Kid loses phone → temporarily without phone.
- Teen sleeps in → late to school (after age 12 or so).

Caregiver role: don't rescue. Empathize after. "That was a hard morning. I bet you don't want to oversleep tomorrow." Not "I told you so."

### Stage 3 — When Natural Doesn't Work

Switch to logical when:

- Safety is real (won't wear seatbelt = no driving).
- Harm is to others (hit sibling = separated, repair).
- Asymmetric stakes (run into street = caregiver intervenes).
- Kid lacks developmental capacity (3-year-old won't learn from "hungry later").
- Natural consequence is too delayed (today's homework affects grade in 6 weeks).
- Caregiver can't actually let it happen.

### Stage 4 — Designing Logical Consequences

A logical consequence:
- Connects to the behavior in a way the kid can see.
- Is proportional.
- Is time-bounded.
- Is enforceable.
- Doesn't shame.
- Doesn't withdraw essential connection / care.

Examples by domain:

**Made a mess:**
- Logical: clean it up.
- Not logical: no screens.

**Hit sibling:**
- Logical: separated for a window; repair when calm.
- Not logical: lose toy.

**Lied about substance use:**
- Logical: tighten curfew, more contact, demonstrated honesty re-earns previous freedom.
- Not logical: ground for a month.

**Broke shared item:**
- Logical: repair or replace from own resources; help fix.
- Not logical: lose unrelated privilege.

**Didn't do agreed chore:**
- Logical: do the chore; lose the discretionary thing the chore was prerequisite to.
- Not logical: extra chores as punishment.

**Late curfew:**
- Logical: earlier curfew next time; demonstrated reliability re-earns later curfew.
- Not logical: lose phone.

### Stage 5 — When Imposed Is the Right Move (Rare)

- Acute safety violation where logical isn't sufficient deterrent.
- Repeated behavior where logical hasn't worked and natural can't.
- Specific household-rule violation that has no natural or logical analog.

Even here:
- Brief.
- Time-bounded.
- Followed by re-earning structure.
- Doesn't withdraw care or connection.

### Stage 6 — The Punishment-Spiral

Pattern:
- Behavior → punishment.
- Same behavior → bigger punishment.
- Same behavior → biggest punishment.
- Relationship damaged; behavior unchanged.

Exit:
- Functional analysis: what is driving this behavior? (Not "why are they bad?" but "what is the behavior doing?")
- Driver-based intervention: anxiety, ADHD, sleep, peer, identity, family stress, sensory.
- Smaller, more connected consequences.
- Repair after.
- Sometimes: stop the consequence approach entirely; approach the behavior differently.

### Stage 7 — When Consequence Is the Wrong Intervention

Sometimes the behavior is signaling something else:

- Anxiety / depression presenting as defiance.
- ADHD presenting as forgetting / "not caring."
- Sensory overwhelm presenting as meltdown.
- Trauma response presenting as withdrawal or aggression.
- Learning disability presenting as homework refusal.
- Family stress presenting as acting-out.
- Mental-health crisis presenting as substance use.

Consequence work doesn't address driver. Address driver first.

### Stage 8 — What Not to Use as Consequence

- **Withdrawal of care or affection.** "I don't love you when you do that." Erodes attachment.
- **Withdrawal of basic needs.** Food, warmth, sleep.
- **Public shaming.**
- **Long isolation that breaks connection.**
- **Physical punishment.** Counterproductive; evidence consistently against.
- **Sibling shaming or comparison.**
- **Removal of an unrelated long-term privilege.** "You'll never play sports again."
- **Permanent removal.** Ends the lever.

### Stage 9 — Repair After

A consequence delivered does not end the relationship.

- Once consequence is delivered, the behavior is closed.
- Caregiver doesn't bring it up repeatedly.
- Connection re-established.
- Brief acknowledgment if useful: "That was hard. We're good."

The kid feels: this was a moment, not a permanent state.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

- **Autistic:** explicit, predictable, written if helpful; sensory-aware; avoid using sensory environment as punishment.
- **ADHD:** time-blind kids may not connect delayed consequences; immediacy and visual cues help.
- **Anxious:** consequences can spike anxiety; calibrate; focus on repair.
- **Trauma history:** punitive consequences re-trigger; focus on collaborative; therapeutic support.
- **Strong-willed:** consequence battles can become identity battles; logical, brief, autonomy-respecting.
- **Teen with substance / mental-health:** integrated treatment; consequences alongside, not instead of, treatment.
- **PDA profile:** demand-avoidance is acute; collaborative framing; specialist support.
- **Cultural context:** cultural norms around discipline vary; integrate without losing principle.
- **Co-parent disagreement:** consistency matters; alignment conversation separately; don't undercut in front of kid.
- **Foster / kinship / adopted:** trauma-aware; loss-of-care risk specifically activated; predictable, non-rejecting consequences.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to imposed-unrelated consequences | Natural / logical first |
| Use removal of care or affection | Connection is not negotiable |
| Use removal of basic needs | Food, warmth, sleep, parent presence not consequences |
| Use shame as consequence | Damages identity |
| Use physical punishment | Counterproductive; evidence-based against |
| Escalate consequences when behavior persists | Functional analysis; bigger isn't more effective |
| Skip driver check | Sometimes consequence isn't the right intervention |
| Use unrelated privilege removal | Not connected = not learning |
| Make consequence permanent | Re-earning structure |
| Bring up the consequence repeatedly | Closed when delivered |
| Skip repair after | Connection restored |
| Treat one consequence as fixing a pattern | Pattern needs structural look |
| Use comparison ("your sibling never") | Damages relationship |
| Apply same consequence across kids regardless of profile | Profile-aware |
| Conflate consequence with revenge | Caregiver-regulation prerequisite |
| Use consequences to feel powerful | Watch caregiver-side motivation |
