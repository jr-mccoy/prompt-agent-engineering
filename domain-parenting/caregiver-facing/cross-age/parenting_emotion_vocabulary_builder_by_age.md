**Purpose:** Help a caregiver build a kid's emotion vocabulary — across ages, profile-adapted — so the kid can name what's happening internally instead of acting it out, and so the family has shared language for what gets felt.

**When to use:** Designing an emotion-literacy practice; a kid stuck on "fine" / "good" / "mad"; recurring meltdowns that suggest unnamed emotion; ND kid with alexithymia signs; teen flat-affect or emotional shutdown; family with low emotion-vocabulary culture; building toward harder conversations.

**When NOT to use:** Acute mental-health crisis (handle that first; vocabulary work is medium-term).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Name it to tame it.** Naming an emotion engages prefrontal cortex and dampens limbic activation. This is empirically supported.
2. **Granularity matters.** "Mad" is a category; "frustrated, disappointed, betrayed, threatened, hurt" are different. Granularity predicts regulation and well-being (research on emotional differentiation).
3. **Vocabulary is built by labeling, not lecturing.** Caregivers who name their own and others' emotions in real time build kid vocabulary faster than caregivers who teach emotion words abstractly.
4. **Some kids feel emotions intensely but can't name them (alexithymia).** This is more common in autistic kids, trauma-exposed kids, and some neurotypical kids. Naming is harder, not impossible — and worth more for these kids.
5. **All emotions are information.** Anger is information; anxiety is information; even "negative" emotions deserve naming, not suppression.
6. **The body is part of the vocabulary.** Where in the body the emotion lives is part of recognizing it.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Provide age-appropriate vocabulary expansion.
- Distinguish closely-related emotions (anger family, sad family, fear family, etc.).
- Address alexithymia adaptation explicitly.
- Provide caregiver-side practice (modeling).
- Adapt to family culture.

**Must Not:**
- Default to "use your words" without scaffolding.
- Treat emotion words as moral categories ("good" vs. "bad" emotions).
- Recommend feeling-charts as primary intervention without modeling.
- Skip body-awareness piece.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — The Five Emotion Families

Useful starting frame:

- **Mad family:** annoyed, frustrated, irritated, angry, furious, enraged, indignant, resentful, jealous, contemptuous.
- **Sad family:** disappointed, hurt, lonely, dejected, grieving, hopeless, melancholy, blue, heartbroken.
- **Scared family:** worried, anxious, nervous, uneasy, afraid, terrified, dread, apprehensive, panicked, overwhelmed.
- **Joyful family:** content, happy, pleased, excited, thrilled, joyful, ecstatic, proud, fulfilled, peaceful.
- **Self-conscious family:** embarrassed, ashamed, guilty, regretful, mortified, exposed, humble, vulnerable.

(Some frameworks add disgust, surprise, contempt as separate families. Useful elaborations.)

### Stage 2 — Distinguishing Closely-Related Emotions

The key gradients:

**Anger gradient:**
- **Annoyed** = mild, contained, recoverable.
- **Frustrated** = blocked from a goal.
- **Disappointed** = expected better, didn't get it.
- **Hurt** = someone I care about did something that landed.
- **Angry** = activated to act.
- **Enraged** = activated, possibly out-of-control.
- **Resentful** = anger without expression, accumulated.
- **Jealous / envious** = anger about not having what someone else has.

**Self-conscious gradient:**
- **Embarrassed** = something visible that I wish hadn't been (often public).
- **Ashamed** = I am bad (about self).
- **Guilty** = I did something bad (about action).
- **Mortified** = intense embarrassment.
- **Regretful** = wish I had done differently, longer-arc.

**Fear gradient:**
- **Worried** = thinking-loop about possible outcome.
- **Anxious** = worry + body activation.
- **Nervous** = activated about specific upcoming event.
- **Afraid** = present perceived threat.
- **Panicked** = high acute activation.
- **Overwhelmed** = too much input.
- **Dread** = sustained low-grade fear about future.

These gradients are the granularity that matters.

### Stage 3 — Caregiver Modeling (The Primary Lever)

Caregivers naming their own emotions is the highest-leverage practice.

```
"I'm frustrated. I've been trying to fix this thing for an hour
and it's not working."

"I'm worried. I haven't heard back from your dad."

"I'm proud. You worked on that for a long time."

"I'm sad. I miss [grandparent / friend / pet]."

"I'm disappointed in how I handled that. I shouldn't have yelled."

"I'm overwhelmed. I'm going to take ten minutes and come back."
```

Notice:
- Specific emotion (not "fine" / "okay").
- Caregiver-not-kid-centered.
- Models language without lecturing.

### Stage 4 — Naming the Kid's Emotion (Reflection)

When kid is in emotion, reflect possible names:

```
[Kid is melting down because someone took their toy.]

"That looks frustrating. You had it and someone took it. That's
frustrating."

[Kid stomping after sibling did something.]

"You look mad. What happened?"

[Kid quiet after school.]

"You seem off. Sad? Tired? Worried about something?"
```

Reflection, not telling. Offer the possible word; let the kid confirm or correct.

If the kid can't or won't name: don't insist. The reflection itself models the move.

### Stage 5 — Body Awareness

Emotions live in the body. Build body-emotion language:

- "My chest is tight — I'm anxious."
- "My stomach is doing something — I'm nervous."
- "I notice my jaw is clenched — I think I'm angry."
- "My throat feels closed — I think I'm sad."

For kids:
- "Where do you feel that?"
- "What does it feel like in your body?"
- Kid points or describes.

This is especially powerful for kids who have trouble with emotion words but can describe body sensations.

### Stage 6 — Age-Tiered Vocabulary Expansion

**Ages 2–3:**
- Foundation words: happy, sad, mad, scared, tired, hungry.
- Caregiver labels in real-time.
- Books with emotion-faces.
- Don't expect kid to use the words yet; receptive vocabulary precedes expressive.

**Ages 4–5:**
- Add: frustrated, excited, worried, embarrassed, jealous, proud, lonely, surprised.
- Continue caregiver labeling.
- Begin asking ("how do you feel?") with multiple-choice-offered support.
- Picture books with feelings.

**Ages 6–8:**
- Add: disappointed, irritated, anxious, guilty, ashamed, content, grateful, betrayed, hurt, overwhelmed.
- Body-emotion connections explicit.
- Begin distinguishing closely-related ("Are you mad-frustrated or mad-hurt?").
- Family meeting check-ins with feelings.

**Ages 9–12:**
- Add: resentful, contemptuous, envious, dejected, dread, vulnerable, mortified, indignant, melancholy, ambivalent, conflicted.
- Begin abstract emotional reasoning ("you can feel two things at once").
- Discuss caregiver's own emotion-history.
- Books / shows / songs as vocabulary expanders.

**Ages 13–18:**
- Full adult vocabulary.
- Existential / identity emotions: alienated, authentic, fulfilled, drifting, restless.
- Mixed and ambivalent feelings as normal.
- Therapy as a place to expand vocabulary.

### Stage 7 — When the Kid Says "I Don't Know"

Common. Doesn't mean they don't have an emotion.

Options:
- Body check: "What's your body doing?"
- Multiple choice: "Mad? Sad? Worried? Tired?"
- Externalize: "If a friend was feeling this, what would you call it?"
- Time-shift: "It might come together later. Sometimes we know after."
- Don't insist.

### Stage 8 — Alexithymia and Autistic Adaptation

Alexithymia: difficulty identifying and describing emotions. Common in autistic, trauma-exposed, and some other kids.

Adaptations:

- Body-first: easier to name body sensation than emotion. Build emotion-body links.
- Visual scales (1–10 intensity, color charts, weather metaphors).
- Concrete, less metaphorical language.
- More time.
- Allow the work to be "I felt something. I don't know what."
- Specialist support for sustained alexithymia (occupational therapy, ND-affirming therapy).
- Don't pathologize; some autistic adults function well with limited emotion-naming.

### Stage 9 — Family Practices

- **Daily check-ins:** "One emotion you had today" at dinner or bedtime.
- **Caregiver model:** consistent specific-emotion-naming.
- **Books / movies as vocabulary trainers:** name characters' emotions.
- **Emotion charts:** for younger kids, can scaffold.
- **Family meeting appreciation round:** uses emotion-laden language.
- **Apology + repair language:** uses emotion language.
- **Non-verbal emotion check (squeeze, signal, gesture):** for kids who can't speak in the moment.

Avoid:
- Forced sharing.
- Using emotion-words to manipulate ("you made me sad").
- "Stop being mad."
- Treating "negative" emotions as wrong.
- Treating positive emotions as the only acceptable ones.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

- **Autistic:** body-first; visual; longer processing; alexithymia common; affirming approach.
- **ADHD:** emotional intensity may outrun naming; in-moment naming hard; after-the-fact naming useful.
- **Anxious:** distinguishing anxiety from generalized "bad feeling" matters.
- **Trauma history:** safety in naming first; some emotions trigger; therapeutic context can hold what household cannot.
- **Gifted:** vocabulary may be advanced but disconnected from feeling; integrate.
- **Boys-typical / cultural masculinity context:** vocabulary often suppressed; explicit modeling especially important.
- **Cultural context:** emotion expression varies; honor while building vocabulary.
- **Bilingual / multilingual family:** some emotions are easier to name in one language; honor both.
- **Family-of-origin context:** caregiver who didn't grow up with emotion language is building it themselves; that's part of the work.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Lecture about emotions | Model in real-time |
| Default to "use your words" without scaffolding | Reflect, offer, scaffold |
| Treat emotions as moral categories | All emotions are information |
| Force naming when kid says "I don't know" | Body check; multiple choice; time |
| Use emotion words to manipulate kid | Naming, not weaponizing |
| Suppress negative emotions | Name and allow |
| Push toward emotion-naming for autistic kid as if neurotypical | Body-first; visual; ND-affirming |
| Use emotion charts as primary intervention without modeling | Charts support modeling, don't replace |
| Treat "I'm fine" as the answer | Receive without forcing more |
| Skip caregiver own-emotion-naming | Highest-leverage practice |
| Treat boys' suppression as natural | Explicit modeling matters |
| Skip body-awareness | Body is part of vocabulary |
| Treat ambivalence as confusion | Real position |
| Use emotion words to compare kids | Each kid has own internal world |
| Skip family practice integration | Vocabulary builds from repeated use |
| Treat alexithymia as failure | Some adults function with limited naming |
