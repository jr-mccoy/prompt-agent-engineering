**Purpose:** Design a household-contributions system that fits the family — by age, by neurodivergent profile, by family structure — and that avoids the common failures (allowance as bribery, chore-tracking-becomes-the-job, kids getting the message that home is unpaid labor).

**When to use:** Designing a chore system for the first time; revising one that's failing; adding a kid to an existing system; major family change; teen joining the workforce.

**When NOT to use:** Acute family crisis. Single-issue conflict (use the boundary-script library instead).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Membership chores are different from earned-income chores.** Membership chores are part of being in the family; not paid. Earned-income chores are work for money beyond membership.
2. **Two separate systems work better than blended.** Conflating them teaches kids that everything kids do at home is a transaction. Or that there's nothing money-able. Both are limiting.
3. **Capacity, not perfection.** A 4-year-old's bed is not made well; the practice of making it is the point.
4. **Stop tracking what you can't sustain tracking.** Charts that work for a week and collapse don't help. Build something durable.
5. **Chore systems are calibrated to family size and adult capacity.** Single-parent + three kids + full-time work has different demands than two-parent + one kid + part-time work.
6. **Failure is part of the design.** A kid forgetting their chore is not the system failing. It's the system catching the gap.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — The Two-System Frame

**Membership chores:**
- "Things we do because we live here."
- Not paid.
- Examples: making bed, taking dishes to sink, putting clothes in hamper, age-appropriate tidying, family meal contribution.
- Caregiver does many membership chores too. Kids see this.

**Earned-income chores:**
- "Work that earns money."
- Paid (per-task, hourly, weekly, or project).
- Examples: lawn mowing, deep cleaning, washing car, organizing garage, walking dog beyond family walks, pet care for neighbors, baby-sitting siblings on demand.
- Optional: kid can decline; no money if declined.

**Allowance (if used):**
- Separate from chores. Either a fixed amount for being in the family + having things to learn money-management with, or tied to specific savings / spending split, or none.
- See domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-9-12/parenting_money_allowance_chores_system_designer.md for tween-and-up money system design.

### Stage 2 — Age-Appropriate Membership Chores

**Ages 2–3:**
- Put toys in basket.
- Carry small items to hamper.
- Help wipe up small spills.
- Put own dish in sink with help.
- Caregiver-led, kid-assist.

**Ages 4–5:**
- Make bed (not perfectly).
- Put clothes in hamper.
- Set table partially.
- Put away own laundry (folded by caregiver).
- Feed pet with supervision.
- Put dishes in dishwasher.
- Tidy own toys after play.

**Ages 6–8:**
- Make bed daily.
- Set / clear table.
- Empty / load dishwasher partially.
- Sweep small areas.
- Take out trash to bin.
- Feed pet independently.
- Put away own laundry.
- Help cook simple things.
- Pack own school bag.

**Ages 9–12:**
- Make bed.
- Wash dishes / load dishwasher fully.
- Cook simple meals with supervision.
- Clean own room weekly.
- Vacuum.
- Wipe down bathroom surfaces.
- Take out trash and recycling.
- Pet care.
- Pack lunch.
- Manage own laundry partially (sorting, transferring).

**Ages 13–15:**
- Make bed.
- Manage own laundry start to finish.
- Cook a meal for the family weekly.
- Clean bathroom.
- Mow lawn / shovel snow.
- Grocery list contribution.
- Pet care.
- Specific household maintenance task as assigned.

**Ages 16–18:**
- Same as 13–15 plus:
- Plan and cook some family meals.
- Grocery shopping with list.
- Manage some household errands.
- Specific area of household responsibility (e.g., bathroom, kitchen).
- Scaling toward independent-adult capacity.

### Stage 3 — Sustainable Tracking

The most common failure: a system that the caregiver can't keep up with.

**Low-maintenance options:**

- **Daily-routine baked into morning / evening:** make bed before breakfast; put dishes in sink after meals; pajamas in hamper. No chart needed once habit lands.
- **One weekly task per kid:** "Saturday is your room day." Done.
- **Family deep-clean session weekly:** 30 minutes, everyone participates.
- **Whiteboard with rotating weekly assignments.**

**Higher-maintenance options (only if caregiver can sustain):**

- **Per-day chart with stickers.** Works for 4–8 if caregiver maintains; collapses fast otherwise.
- **App-based tracking.** Same caveat.
- **Token economy.** Significant tracking demand.

**Test:** can the caregiver maintain this in a typical busy week? If no, simplify.

### Stage 4 — Earned-Income Chore System

If using earned-income chores:

- Post a list of available paid chores with rates.
- Kid signs up or claims.
- Quality standard explicit (lawn = mowed AND edged AND trimmings bagged).
- Payment on completion or weekly.
- Kid can decline; no income.
- Don't use earned-income chores to do work you'd otherwise pay an outside person — be honest about value.

Example list:
- Wash car: $10
- Mow lawn: $20
- Clean garage / basement: $15
- Wash all windows: $12
- Reorganize pantry: $10
- Walk dog 7+ times in a week: $5

### Stage 5 — When Membership Chores Don't Get Done

Not a punishment problem; a system problem first.

- Was the chore explicit?
- Did the kid know when it was due?
- Was there a reasonable cue?
- Did the kid have capacity (time, energy, ND-adjusted)?

If yes-yes-yes-yes and chore not done: that's accountability time.

Accountability options:
- Re-do the chore now, then do whatever was next.
- Lose access to the next discretionary thing until done.
- Repair: do the chore + extra-help-with-something.
- Family meeting agenda: "Why isn't this working?"

Avoid:
- Lecture.
- Shame.
- Permanent removal of access.
- Endless reminding (this becomes the caregiver's chore).

### Stage 6 — Caregiver-Side Discipline

The biggest lever:

- Caregivers do membership chores too, visibly.
- Kids observe what membership work looks like.
- Caregivers don't use chores as punishment for unrelated behavior.
- Caregivers don't redo kids' chores in front of them.
- Caregivers separate "this isn't to my standard" from "this isn't done."
- Caregivers calibrate standards to age.

### Stage 7 — Adapting to Family Structure

**Single-parent:**
- Higher kid-contribution often necessary; honest about the family-unit dynamic.
- Risk of parentification — kid becomes co-adult, loses childhood. Watch.

**Blended family:**
- New rules need joint articulation.
- Stepparent-asking-for-chores can feel different than biological-parent; bio parent often holds.

**Co-parenting across households:**
- Chore systems vary; explain to kid: "We do it this way here. They may do it differently."

**Multigenerational:**
- More adults means more capacity but also more potential for kids to escape contribution.

**Foster / kinship:**
- Capacity-and-trauma-aware; chores can be grounding (predictability) or triggering (parentification history).
- Start small; build with relationship.

**Adoptive:**
- Same fundamentals.

**Big-family:**
- Older kids sometimes carry too much; protect childhood.
- Younger kids' membership contribution honored even when not strictly necessary.

**Small-family / only child:**
- Kid sometimes does very little because the household runs without them; explicit chores correct this.

### Stage 8 — Adapting to ND Profiles

**ADHD:**
- Visual cues (lists, alarms, location-based reminders).
- Body-double approach (caregiver doing parallel chore alongside).
- Smaller chunks.
- Immediate completion-checking.
- Avoid time-sensitive chores requiring sustained focus.

**Autistic:**
- Predictable schedule.
- Explicit, written instructions.
- Sensory-sensitivity to specific chores (some hate dishwasher noise, wet hands, specific textures).
- Routine-disruption is hard; build chores into existing routines.

**Anxious:**
- Clarity reduces anxiety.
- Avoid moving target standards.
- Don't add chores during high-anxiety windows.

**Sensory-processing differences:**
- Match chores to sensory tolerance.

**Trauma history:**
- Predictability is regulating.
- Chores tied to identity ("I am someone who can keep my room") rather than compliance.

**Chronically ill / disabled:**
- Capacity-aware; chores adapted.
- Don't extract performance equal to siblings.

### Stage 9 — When the System Breaks

If the chore system has collapsed:

- Don't reset by adding more chores.
- Reset by simplifying.
- Audit: what did the caregiver actually maintain?
- Audit: what did the kids actually retain?
- Rebuild the smaller version.
- Acknowledge the reset to kids: "We tried that and it didn't work. Here's the simpler version."

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

Already covered in Stage 8. Additional notes:

- **Big age range:** elder kids' chores may compensate for younger kids' inability; adjust as younger kids age in.
- **Kid in extracurricular sport / activity:** chore load adjusts during peak season; resumes off-season; kid still has membership.
- **Working teen:** earned-income chores may shift to outside work; membership still applies.
- **Teen-with-mental-health-issue:** capacity may temporarily reduce; honest renegotiation.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Pay for membership chores | Membership ≠ transaction |
| Use chores as punishment for unrelated behavior | Conflates work with shame |
| Build a tracking system caregiver can't sustain | Simpler beats elegant |
| Redo kids' chores in front of them | Communicates futility |
| Treat 4-year-old's chore as adult-quality | Capacity, not perfection |
| Add more chores when system collapses | Simplify and rebuild |
| Skip the caregiver-doing-chores-too piece | Modeling matters |
| Treat all chores as paid | Allowance separation matters |
| Treat all chores as unpaid | Earned-income teaches money-and-work link |
| Skip ND adaptation | One-size doesn't fit |
| Allow parentification in single-parent / big-family | Protect childhood |
| Use endless reminding | Reminding becomes caregiver's job |
| Drop chores entirely during sport season | Reduce volume; don't drop membership |
| Skip kid voice in design | Buy-in matters |
| Treat the chore chart as the job | The chart serves the chores, not the other way |
| Conflate "not to my standard" with "not done" | Calibrate standards to age |
