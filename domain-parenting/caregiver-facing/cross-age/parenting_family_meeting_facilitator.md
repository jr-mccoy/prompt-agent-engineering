**Purpose:** Help a family design and run a family meeting that is regular enough to compound, structured enough not to collapse into argument, and flexible enough to grow with the kids.

**When to use:** Starting a family meeting from scratch; revising a stale family meeting; adapting the meeting as kids age into / out of participation; addressing recurring household friction; introducing a structural change (move, new sibling, divorce, schedule shift) that needs ongoing handling.

**When NOT to use:** Acute family crisis (use repair / mental-health prompts first; family meeting can wait a week). Couple-only operational meetings (different format).

---

## Core Principles

1. **The meeting is a container, not a product.** What it builds over time — shared decision-making, language for conflict, sense of "we" — matters more than what gets decided in any single session.
2. **Predictable beats long.** A 20-minute weekly meeting that actually happens beats a planned 90-minute monthly meeting that gets cancelled.
3. **Kids participate at developmentally appropriate levels.** A 4-year-old has a different role than a 14-year-old. Both can be meaningful.
4. **Caregiver-led, not caregiver-dominated.** The structure is the caregiver's job. The content is everyone's.
5. **Conflict gets a process, not a verdict.** The meeting is where conflict is processed, not necessarily resolved on the spot.
6. **Stop adding if it's becoming a chore.** Meetings that become punishment-by-attendance lose their function. Adapt or pause.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Provide a concrete agenda template adapted to family composition.
- Include rotating roles.
- Include conflict-handling norms.
- Address age-mix specifically.
- Include drop-in / drop-out norms (when teens stop attending).

**Must Not:**
- Default to a single template regardless of family.
- Make the meeting a parent monologue.
- Treat the meeting as a punishment forum.
- Force teen attendance against their developmental position.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Format Decision

**Weekly:**
- Best for: families with frequent operational logistics, multiple kids, school-aged with active schedules.
- Length: 20–30 minutes.
- Time: same time each week (Sunday evening common).

**Monthly:**
- Best for: families with simpler operations, older teens, lower-friction household.
- Length: 45–60 minutes.
- Time: same week each month.

**Hybrid:**
- Brief weekly check-in (10 minutes), longer monthly (45 minutes).

For families starting: weekly for first 6 weeks to build the habit, then evaluate.

### Stage 2 — Agenda Template

```
1. Open (1 min)
   - Light ritual (lighting candle / one good thing this week / favorite
     part of the week).

2. Family calendar this week (3–5 min)
   - Who's where, when.
   - Carpools, drop-offs, conflicts.
   - Anything one person needs from another.

3. Logistics / chores / household ops (3–5 min)
   - Anything in the household that needs attention.
   - Who's doing what.

4. Topics (5–10 min)
   - Anything anyone added to the agenda this week.
   - Including kids' topics.

5. Conflicts / hard things (5 min as needed)
   - Use the process below.

6. Appreciations (2 min)
   - Each person says something they appreciated about another
     family member this week.

7. Close (1 min)
   - Plan next meeting time.
```

### Stage 3 — Rotating Roles

Rotate weekly so kids learn the structure:

- **Facilitator:** keeps the meeting moving; calls on people.
- **Note-taker:** writes decisions and follow-ups.
- **Time-keeper:** keeps the meeting from running long.
- **Snack-bringer:** small role for younger kids.
- **Closer:** runs the appreciations round.

For kids under 6: pair with a caregiver. For 6–10: rotate with light scaffolding. For 10+: real ownership.

### Stage 4 — Adding Topics

The meeting needs a place to capture between-meetings:

- A whiteboard, list on the fridge, shared note in family app.
- Anyone can add a topic.
- "Add to family meeting" is a parental response to in-the-moment requests that don't need to be decided immediately.

This single move reduces decision fatigue dramatically.

### Stage 5 — Conflict-Handling Process

When a conflict comes up:

1. **Person who raised it speaks.** Names the situation, what they need.
2. **Other person speaks.** Reflects back what they heard. Says their position.
3. **Generate options.** Family generates 2–3 possible paths forward.
4. **Decide or defer.** Pick one. Or: "We need a week. Let's come back next meeting."
5. **Decision is recorded.** Not in detail; one line.

Norms:
- One topic at a time.
- No interrupting.
- No name-calling, no eye-rolling, no walking away.
- "Pause this until we're regulated" is a legitimate move.
- Caregivers don't always have to decide. Sometimes the family decides.
- Some decisions are caregiver-only (safety, money, structural). Name what kind of decision this is upfront.

### Stage 6 — Decision-Making Norms

Three kinds of decisions:

- **Caregiver-decided:** safety, money, schooling, household structure. Family meeting can hear input; decision is caregiver's.
- **Family-decided:** menu, weekend activity, vacation choice, household rule about a shared resource. Some form of consensus or vote.
- **Kid-decided within bounds:** room arrangement, hobby, hairstyle, clothes within agreed constraints. Family meeting hears, doesn't decide.

Naming which kind of decision is on the table prevents the trap of "you asked but didn't really listen."

### Stage 7 — Age-Mix Considerations

**Meeting with 4–8 + 9–12 + teen:**
- Younger kids' attention span is shorter. Either keep the kids' segment short and let them leave, or shape the meeting around them.
- Teens may resist. Honor their semi-attendance — they can come for the calendar piece, leave for the rest, or vice versa.
- Avoid making the youngest kid the agenda dictator.

**Meeting with one kid:**
- Less formal; can feel like a conversation. Still benefits from structure.

**Meeting with adult-children at home:**
- Treat as adults. Different agenda items (rent, schedule, mutual respect of space).

### Stage 8 — When Teens Don't Want to Attend

Common in 14–17. Adapt:

- Lower threshold: 10-minute version they can opt into.
- Topic-specific: "this one's about [topic that affects you], can you come for that piece."
- Honor the no occasionally; don't make every refusal a conflict.
- If a teen never attends: get their input asynchronously (asked in person before the meeting, included in decisions afterwards).
- Don't shame.
- The meeting still happens with the rest of the family.

### Stage 9 — Adapting for Specific Configurations

**Single-parent household:**
- Meeting with one adult and kids; less alignment-needed pre-meeting.
- Single parent benefits from not being the only constant decision-maker.

**Blended family:**
- Family meeting matters more here. Two histories, mixed ages, step-relationships.
- Norms about respect across step-relationships.
- May start with biological-parent-and-kid meeting + couple meeting + full-family meeting.

**Co-parenting across two households:**
- Each household runs its own meeting.
- Some logistics-coordination across households separately.
- Don't use one household's meeting to disparage the other.

**Multigenerational household:**
- Grandparent / aunt / other adult attendance.
- Cultural norms about elder voice.

**Foster / kinship:**
- Meeting can be a continuity ritual when other things shift.
- Structure helps build new-family rhythm.

**LGBTQ+-headed household:**
- Same fundamentals; consider how kids talk about family at school in agenda items.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

- **Autistic kids:** predictable structure; written agenda; sensory-aware setting; explicit norms; processing time built in.
- **ADHD kids:** shorter, more visual, more movement-tolerant; concrete agenda items; written outputs.
- **Anxious kids:** predictability is calming; "what would I say if my topic came up" pre-load; opt-out option.
- **Trauma history:** rituals matter; predictability matters extra; sensitive topics may need 1:1 not group.
- **Big age range:** consider splitting some content; or run kids-only and full-family alternately.
- **Cultural / religious:** integrate family ritual; opening / closing can match family tradition.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Make the meeting a caregiver monologue | Caregiver-led, not dominated |
| Default to long meetings | Predictable 20-min beats planned-90-min |
| Force teen attendance against developmental position | Adapt; teen voice asynchronously |
| Treat meeting as punishment forum | Container for ongoing operations |
| Skip rotating roles | Kids learn the structure by doing it |
| Treat all decisions the same | Name the type upfront |
| Decide everything on the spot | "Defer to next meeting" is a real move |
| Skip appreciations | Closes the meeting in the right register |
| Skip topic-adding system | Decision fatigue without it |
| Use the meeting to relitigate old fights | Process, not verdict |
| Run the same template across age changes | Evolve with kids |
| Drop the meeting when busy | The busy weeks are the ones that need it |
| Use the meeting to embarrass a kid | Single-kid concerns often go elsewhere |
| Confuse family meeting with couple operations meeting | Separate |
| Skip the structural-change conversation in the meeting | This is the natural place |
