**Purpose:** Calibrate a parent's "is this normal?" question with an age-tiered, research-aligned view of development from 4 to 8. The tiers intentionally span the huge cognitive-emotional shift between preschool and early elementary. Output labels the behavior **Typical / Intense but Typical / Worth Raising with Pediatrician** — never "abnormal," never "disordered."

**When to use:** "Should I be worried about...?" "Is this an ADHD thing or a 5-year-old thing?" "Is my 7-year-old too old to...?" Before deploying a behavior plan, to confirm you're not pathologizing typical development.

**When NOT to use:** As a diagnostic tool. For that, you need a pediatrician, developmental pediatrician, psychologist, occupational therapist, or speech-language pathologist.

---

## Safety Block

Some signals warrant same-day or same-week attention regardless of age. If the parent describes any of these, surface them at the top of the output:

- Loss of previously acquired skills (language, toileting, self-care) → pediatrician within 1–2 weeks.
- Suicidal statements, self-injury, or talk of wanting to disappear — at any age → pediatrician same-day; 988 (US) if acute.
- Significant sleep disruption + mood changes + behavior change lasting more than 4 weeks → pediatrician.
- Any concern about abuse or neglect → pediatrician / child protection. Mandatory reporter obligations apply for professionals.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Use the three tiers: **Typical / Intense but Typical / Worth Raising with Pediatrician.**
- Break recommendations out by age band: 4–5, 6–7, 8.
- Cover behavior, emotion/regulation, attention, social, cognitive/language, motor, self-care, and sleep when the question touches them — not all of these in every output.
- Cite the type of professional who evaluates each flag (pediatrician, developmental pediatrician, psychologist, OT, SLP).
- Distinguish "common but intense" from "outside typical range" — many 5-year-olds are difficult; that's not pathology.
- Acknowledge variability: individual kids, family culture, sleep debt, and life stress all shift where a given behavior falls.

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose. "Your child sounds autistic" is off-limits. "This pattern is sometimes seen in autistic kids; a developmental pediatrician can evaluate" is fine.
- Use "normal" as a moral word. The framing is about range, not worth.
- Tell a parent to wait and see if a red-flag signal is present. Early evaluation opens services; waiting doesn't.
- Collapse the three ages. A 4-year-old and an 8-year-old are different animals.
- Assume middle-class US defaults. Sleep norms, meal norms, and supervision norms vary across cultures.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Frame the Range

Open with: "At [age], the range of typical behavior in [domain] is wide. Here's how to calibrate what you're seeing."

### Step 2 — Age-Tiered Baselines

Use the table below as a reference when building the output. Pick the age band and the relevant domain(s). Include only what the parent asked about — don't dump the whole table.

#### Ages 4–5 (Preschool / Pre-K / Kindergarten)

| Domain | Typical | Intense but Typical | Worth Raising |
|--------|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Behavior & limits** | Tests limits daily; "no" as a full sentence; physical outbursts recover in minutes | 20–40 min tantrums a few times/week; defiance around transitions and bedtime | Daily meltdowns >45 min sustained, aggression that injures others, can't recover with support after 2+ hours |
| **Emotion / regulation** | Big feelings; struggles to name emotions without coaching; needs adult co-regulation | Cries easily and often; occasional rage when frustrated | Frequent freezing, extreme withdrawal, stated hopelessness or self-harm talk |
| **Attention** | Sustained attention on preferred activity: 10–20 min; non-preferred: 3–10 min | Struggles to sit through family dinner; distracted by every stimulus | Cannot follow a two-step instruction in any setting; cross-setting (school AND home AND grandma's) inattention |
| **Social** | Parallel play shifting to cooperative; rigid rules ("I go first!"); jealousy over parental attention | One best friend or prefers adults; struggles with unstructured groups | No interest in peers after a full year of exposure; lacks shared enjoyment (pointing, showing); social confusion with familiar people |
| **Language** | 2000+ word vocabulary; multi-word sentences; grammatical errors still present; can retell a simple story | Mispronunciations (r, l, th, s); sometimes hard for strangers to understand | Strangers cannot understand most of what they say by age 5; no multi-word sentences by 4; recent loss of words |
| **Motor** | Can hop on one foot; pedal a tricycle/bike with training wheels; draw a person with 3–6 parts | Clumsy; trips a lot; messy with utensils | Can't climb stairs alternating feet by 5; can't hold a crayon with stable grip by 5 |
| **Self-care** | Can mostly dress self; toilet-trained in day; still needs help with complex fasteners | Accidents when excited or tired; refuses clothing textures; rigid food preferences | Not daytime toilet-trained by 5 (medical + OT/psych worth considering); not dressing self at all by 5; extreme food restriction causing weight loss |
| **Sleep** | 10–13 hours/day (some nap, some don't); night waking for water/bathroom; occasional nightmares | Prolonged bedtime routine (45–90 min); climbing into parents' bed | Chronic sleep <8 hours; night terrors weekly; persistent insomnia; extreme daytime sleepiness |

#### Ages 6–7 (Kindergarten / 1st / 2nd)

| Domain | Typical | Intense but Typical | Worth Raising |
|--------|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Behavior & limits** | More negotiation, fewer tantrums; "that's not fair!"; sibling rivalry peaks | Occasional meltdowns (often tired/hungry/overstimulated); push-back on homework and chores | Daily aggression, destruction of property, cruelty to animals, stealing from family |
| **Emotion / regulation** | Can name basic emotions; starting to hide feelings from peers; perfectionism emerges | Intense reactions to losing games, being wrong, embarrassment | Pervasive sadness >2 weeks, extreme anxiety preventing school, self-harm behaviors, suicidal statements |
| **Attention** | Can sit for 15–25 min on non-preferred task with support; reads for pleasure starts 6–7 | Needs frequent redirection; homework is a 45-min battle | Teacher reports inattention across the day; cross-setting + persistent + interfering = worth an ADHD evaluation |
| **Social** | Best friends; same-gender preference common; cliques start; empathy emerging; rules become important | Hurt feelings from exclusion; over-invested in fairness; one-friend-or-none | Isolation after 2+ months in a stable peer group; aggressive rejection of all peers; social cluelessness with repeated misreads of familiar people |
| **Language** | Speech is clear; reads simple chapter books (end of 7); follows multi-step directions | Confused with idioms; literal interpretations; verbose or very quiet | Not reading at grade level by end of 1st/2nd (refer to school for evaluation); persistent stutter; loss of words |
| **Motor** | Can tie shoes (sometime 6–8); rides bike without training wheels; handwriting legible-ish | Fatigues on fine motor; messy handwriting; poor scissor skills | Handwriting unreadable by end of 2nd grade; can't use utensils appropriately; significant clumsiness affecting daily life |
| **Self-care** | Showers/baths with supervision; brushes teeth with reminders; manages clothing | Needs many reminders; forgets steps; overwhelmed by sequences | Cannot independently complete a 3-step routine after repeated teaching by end of 7 |
| **Sleep** | 9–11 hours; sleeps through the night mostly; occasional nightmares | Resistance to bedtime; needs parent to fall asleep; sneaks out | Persistent sleep <8 hours, night terrors weekly, sleepwalking with risk, extreme daytime sleepiness |

#### Age 8 (2nd/3rd Grade)

| Domain | Typical | Intense but Typical | Worth Raising |
|--------|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Behavior & limits** | Self-regulation improving; aware of rules and social standing; "it's not fair" still a feature | Occasional sulking, slamming doors, sibling fights | Persistent defiance across settings for 6+ months, rule-breaking involving theft/deceit/aggression |
| **Emotion / regulation** | Naming feelings more precisely; hiding vulnerability from peers; perfectionism or anxiety can spike | Tears over homework; friendship drama; worried about looking dumb | Functional impairment from anxiety/depression (school refusal, social withdrawal, sleep/eating changes >2 weeks) |
| **Attention** | 20–30 min sustained on non-preferred; independent work stretches; planning simple tasks | Forgets assignments; needs homework scaffolding; distracted by peers | Cross-setting inattention impairing academics and social life — evaluation worth it |
| **Social** | Close friendships; peer opinion matters; reciprocal understanding; lying to avoid consequences | Cliques, gossip, exclusion; fallings-out and makings-up | Persistent peer rejection, zero friendships by end of year, bullying behavior (either direction) |
| **Cognitive / language** | Reading fluently; writing paragraphs; math to 3-digit +/-; explaining reasoning | Struggles with specific subjects; avoids reading | Grade-level gap of 1+ years; letter reversals persisting; word-finding problems; loss of skills |
| **Motor / self-care** | Independent in hygiene with reminders; packs backpack; rides bike | Disorganized; forgets gear; slow in mornings | Persistent inability to complete routines independently; significant fine/gross motor delays |
| **Sleep** | 9–11 hours; more routine around bedtime; may resist | Hard to fall asleep after screen use or busy day | Chronic insomnia, night terrors, excessive daytime sleepiness |

### Step 3 — Apply to the Parent's Specific Question

For the parent's observation, answer:

1. **Tier:** Typical / Intense but Typical / Worth Raising with Pediatrician
2. **Confidence:** High / Medium / Low — and why (how much was in the input, how many domains are involved).
3. **Contributing factors to check before worrying:** sleep debt, hunger patterns, recent life change, new stressor, illness, changes in caregiver dynamics.
4. **If Worth Raising:** Which professional? Pediatrician is always the first door. From there: developmental pediatrician (broad), child psychologist (behavior / mood / attention / anxiety), SLP (language, social communication), OT (sensory, motor, self-care), audiologist (hearing), optometrist (vision).

### Step 4 — Neurodivergence Notes

If the parent mentioned ADHD, autism (ASD Level 1), strong-willed, or sensory concerns, add a short adaptation:

- **ADHD** is distinguished from typical 4–8-year-old wiggliness by **frequency (daily), intensity (more than most peers), impairment (getting in the way of life), and cross-setting consistency (home AND school AND elsewhere).** One 5-year-old being squirmy is not ADHD. Many 5-year-olds are squirmy.
- **Autism / ASD Level 1** often shows as social-rule confusion with familiar people, intense interests, difficulty with unexpected changes, and sensory patterns — not simply shyness or introversion. Kids who excel academically can still benefit from evaluation.
- **Strong-willed temperament** is not a disorder. The differentiator from conduct concerns: is this child oppositional primarily with authority figures they have relationships with, or does it include deceit, theft, cruelty, or rule-breaking across settings? The former is temperament; the latter is worth a pediatrician check-in.
- **Sensory sensitivity** (seeking or avoiding) can exist without autism or ADHD and may benefit from OT evaluation.

### Step 5 — What to Do This Week

Short, concrete next steps based on the tier. Don't leave the parent with only a diagnosis map.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Diagnose autism or ADHD based on a parent's 3-sentence description | Name patterns; defer diagnosis to a qualified professional |
| Dismiss a red-flag signal because "lots of kids do that" | Flag the signal; recommend evaluation; early is better than late |
| Treat a 4-year-old's inattention the same as an 8-year-old's | Age bands exist because brains change fast in this window |
| Assume middle-class US norms for sleep, supervision, or meals | Acknowledge variability; don't pathologize cultural difference |
| Call a single tantrum a week a problem in a 5-year-old | Common; the question is intensity, duration, and functional impact |
| Pathologize a strong-willed temperament | Differentiate from conduct concerns; temperament is not disorder |
| Recommend "wait and see" when a prior skill was lost | Regression is a medical red flag — pediatrician within 1–2 weeks |
| Use "normal" as a moral word | Use "within the typical range" or "intense but typical" |
| Overweight intense-but-typical behavior in a sleep-deprived household | Ask about sleep first. A lot of "behavior problems" are sleep debt |
| Tell a parent to "just relax, he'll grow out of it" | Trust the parent's instinct; many parents know before professionals do |
