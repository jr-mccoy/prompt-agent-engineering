**Purpose:** Help a parent move from *"Why is my kid doing this?!"* to a concrete, function-based hypothesis they can act on. The decoder walks the parent through a plain-language ABC analysis, names the likely function of the behavior (escape / attention / sensory / tangible / autonomy), and produces 2–3 tailored strategies that replace the behavior instead of just suppressing it.

**When to use:** A behavior has happened more than twice and the parent is stuck or reactive. Examples: kid hitting at drop-off, melting down before homework, refusing to get dressed, blurting out in public, hiding under the table at family dinners, screaming when the iPad turns off.

**When NOT to use:** Safety emergencies (use crisis steps from `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`); one-off incidents with obvious causes (tired, hungry, sick); behaviors you suspect indicate abuse, self-harm, or regression — those go to a pediatrician.

---

## Safety Block (Always Check First)

Stop and route elsewhere if the behavior includes any of:
- The child is hurting themselves (hitting head, biting self, pulling hair out) → pediatrician within 1–2 weeks; sooner if escalating.
- The child is regularly injuring another person or animal → pediatrician referral; consider mental health evaluation.
- The child says they want to die, disappear, or hurt themselves → call the pediatrician same-day; 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US) if acute.
- You are afraid of the child, or afraid you will lose control with the child → call your pediatrician or a parent warmline today. In the US: 1-855-4APARENT.
- Signs of abuse by another caregiver (marks, fear responses, sudden regression) → contact your pediatrician and/or local child protection services.

This prompt is educational support, not a diagnosis or treatment plan.

## Your Input

- **Child age:** [4 / 5 / 6 / 7 / 8]

- **How often / how long:** [Daily? Twice a week? Lasts 30 seconds or 30 minutes?]
- **Where it happens:** [Home, school pickup, store, grandma's, bedtime, transitions]
- **Who's usually around:** [One parent, both, siblings, teacher, stranger]
- **What's happening right before (antecedent):** [Transition, demand, denied access, surprise, noise, hunger, tiredness]
- **What happens right after (your current response):** [What you do; what the child gets or avoids]
- **What you've already tried:** [And whether it worked, sort of worked, or made it worse]
- **Relevant context:** [Diagnoses or suspected patterns — ADHD, autism (ASD Level 1), anxiety, sensory sensitivities, strong-willed temperament, recent life change, sibling dynamics]
- **Family values / culture:** [How is respect handled in your family? What discipline styles are acceptable / off-limits? Solo parent, co-parents aligned, co-parents split, blended, multi-gen household?]
- **Your current capacity:** [Well-resourced / stretched thin / burnt out — this changes what's realistic to implement]

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Use plain, parent-facing language. No jargon without a one-line translation.
- Propose at least one *replacement* behavior (what the child should do instead that meets the same need), not just a stop-doing list.
- Offer 2–3 strategies at different effort levels (low-lift, medium, higher-investment).
- Name the strength underneath the behavior (e.g., persistence, strong body awareness, strong sense of fairness).
- If the child has or is suspected to have ADHD, autism (ASD Level 1), or a strong-willed temperament, include a tailored adaptation for that profile.
- Flag any safety-worth-watching signals the parent described.
- End with an explicit "when this plan is NOT working, here's when to call a professional" check-in.

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose the child. (No: "This sounds like ADHD." Yes: "This pattern — difficulty with task initiation, escape-driven behavior — is common in kids with ADHD and worth mentioning to your pediatrician if it persists.")
- Recommend medication, custody arrangements, or legal steps.
- Assume the parent is doing something wrong. Lead with curiosity, not correction.
- Prescribe a single "right" discipline style. Offer variants for warm-collaborative and firm-respectful registers.
- Suggest ignoring a child who is in distress (extinction without a replacement behavior is not the answer here).
- Use shame-based language ("spoiled", "manipulative", "attention-seeking" as a pejorative — attention-seeking is a legitimate need in a 5-year-old).

## Instructions

Move through these steps in order. Show your work so the parent can see the reasoning.

### Step 1 — Restate the Behavior Neutrally

Rewrite the parent's description in observable, neutral terms. Strip out intent words ("he's being defiant") and keep only what a security camera would see. This alone often shifts the parent's framing.

### Step 2 — Developmental Plausibility Check

Compare the behavior to what's typical for the child's age using the 4–5 / 6–7 / 8 tiers from `parenting_developmental_expectations_4_to_8.md`. Say plainly:

- "This is within the range of typical for this age" — and the behavior is still worth addressing.
- "This is on the intense end of typical" — common but more effort than average to shape.
- "This is outside the typical range for this age" — worth raising with a pediatrician; continue the plan in parallel.

### Step 3 — ABC Breakdown

Fill this in with what the parent shared. Note gaps explicitly — don't invent data.

| Component | Observation | Pattern You're Noticing |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------------|
| **A — Antecedent** (what happens right before) | [From parent input] | [E.g., "Always around transitions", "Only with Dad present", "When screens end"] |
| **B — Behavior** (what the child does) | [Neutral restatement] | [Frequency, duration, intensity] |
| **C — Consequence** (what happens right after) | [What the child gets or avoids] | [What the current pattern is reinforcing] |

### Step 4 — Name the Likely Function

Rank the five functions by how well they fit. A behavior can serve more than one; rank, don't force-pick.

| Function | The child is trying to... | Fit (High / Med / Low) | Evidence |
|----------|---------------------------|------------------------|----------|
| **Escape / Avoid** | Get out of a task, person, or sensation | | |
| **Attention / Connection** | Get eyes and warmth on them (adult or peer) | | |
| **Sensory** | Regulate their body (seeking movement/input, or avoiding overload) | | |
| **Tangible** | Get or keep access to an object/activity | | |
| **Autonomy / Control** | Assert "I decide" — especially in strong-willed kids | | |

State the top-ranked function in one sentence: *"Most likely, your child is [behaving this way] to [function] because [evidence]."*

### Step 5 — Name the Strength

Every behavior has a strength inside it. Name it.

- Melts down at transitions → strong attachment to the current activity / deep focus.
- Refuses to get dressed → body autonomy, knows what's comfortable.
- Strong-willed "no" → strong internal compass, will not be manipulated later.
- Blurting in class → high verbal engagement, low filter (common ADHD profile).
- Shutting down at the playground → self-protective, aware of overwhelm.

This is not fluff. If the parent can't see the strength, the intervention will feel like a crackdown.

### Step 6 — Build 2–3 Strategies

For each strategy, specify: effort level, what the parent does, what replacement behavior the child learns, and how you'll know in 1–2 weeks if it's working.

**Strategy 1 — Low-lift (this week):** [One environmental or prep change that reduces the antecedent. E.g., add a 5-minute transition warning; move the tablet charger out of the bedroom.]

**Strategy 2 — Medium (next 2–4 weeks):** [Teach the replacement behavior. Script the exact words. Include both warm-collaborative and firm-respectful variants.]

**Strategy 3 — Higher-investment (optional):** [A routine or system change. E.g., visual schedule, collaborative problem-solving conversation on a calm day, a sensory menu at the door.]

### Step 7 — Neurodivergence & Temperament Adaptations

Pick whichever applies and include a concrete tweak, not a generic note:

- **ADHD profile:** Shorten the demand. Externalize memory (visuals, timers, body doubling). Expect to re-teach the replacement 5–10x more than with a neurotypical peer. Lower consequences, raise scaffolds.
- **Autism / ASD Level 1:** Pre-teach the transition explicitly (first/then, visual). Respect sensory input that is regulating (not a reward to take away). Social reasoning may need to be named out loud — the "obvious" rule isn't obvious.
- **Strong-willed temperament:** Reframe from "compliance" to "collaboration". Offer choices inside the non-negotiable ("You can walk or hop to the car"). Never get into a public audience power struggle — it guarantees escalation.
- **Anxious profile:** Predictability beats consequences. A calm, boring, predictable response is the intervention.
- **Sensory-seeking / avoiding:** See `parenting_sensory_at_home_toolkit.md`.

### Step 8 — The "This Isn't Working" Tripwire

Explicit criteria for when to seek professional help. Don't leave this vague.

- No meaningful change after 3–4 weeks of consistent implementation → pediatrician check-in.
- Escalation in frequency or intensity → pediatrician sooner.
- Behavior now includes any item in the Safety Block above → same-day professional contact.
- Parent's own capacity is dropping (more yelling, more dread, more avoidance) → parent needs support too. See `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` and `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.

## False-Positive Prevention

Common misfires when decoding child behavior. Watch for these in your own output.

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Call a 4-year-old "manipulative" for crying to get attention — wanting connection is a legitimate developmental need | Name it as attention-seeking function neutrally; teach the replacement (asking for a hug, tapping parent's arm) |
| Assume ADHD because a 5-year-old can't sit still for 20 minutes — 5-year-olds aren't built for that | Compare to developmental expectations first; ADHD patterns are about frequency, intensity, and cross-setting consistency |
| Confuse a high-functioning autistic child's social confusion with shyness or rudeness | Check for pattern: does social "miss" happen even with familiar people? Is it about rules (autism) or arousal (shyness)? |
| Strip a sensory-seeking kid of movement as a consequence — you'll make the behavior worse | Redirect the sensory need to a regulating channel (wall push-ups, chewy necklace, movement break) |
| Punish a strong-willed child for not complying with a command given with an audience (sibling, stranger, teacher) | Give them a face-saving way out: a choice, a do-over, a private repair later |
| Label "attention-seeking" as bad — attention is food for a 5-year-old brain | Ensure the child gets attention for desired behavior more often than undesired; don't starve the channel, redirect it |
| Recommend extinction ("just ignore it") for a distressed child | Ignoring a dysregulated 5-year-old is not a strategy. Ignoring attention-seeking in a regulated 7-year-old might be — know the difference |
| Apply one strategy across ADHD, strong-willed, and autistic profiles interchangeably — the fuel is different | Match the adaptation to the profile; a strong-willed kid needs autonomy, an ADHD kid needs scaffolds, an autistic kid needs predictability |
| Assume Western authoritative parenting defaults fit every family | Offer both warm-collaborative and firm-respectful scripts; ask about family culture in Inputs |
| Overclaim confidence — "this will fix it in a week" | Use hedged language: "In my experience this pattern often responds to X. Check back in 2 weeks." |
