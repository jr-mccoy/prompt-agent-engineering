# Sensory Profile and At-Home Regulation Toolkit

## Objective

Help a caregiver of a 4–8 year old (a) build a working map of the child's sensory profile across the eight sensory systems, (b) distinguish seeking from avoiding patterns for each, (c) design an at-home regulation menu using ordinary household items, and (d) know when to consult an occupational therapist. Outputs are practical, parent-runnable, and do not assume a diagnosis.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver describes:

- A child who seems to melt down predictably at loud events, crowded spaces, haircuts, nail clipping, tag-itchy clothing, food textures, or bright lights.
- A child who constantly moves, crashes into things, chews sleeves/pencils, spins, climbs high, or seeks rough-and-tumble.
- A child who is picky about clothing, food, or bath water temperature to an unusual degree.
- A child whose "behavior" problems are actually tied to specific environments (birthday parties, mall, grocery store, school cafeteria).
- Post-school "after-school restraint collapse" where the child falls apart at pickup.
- A recently-diagnosed autistic or ADHD child where sensory is now visible as a factor.
- A generally cooperative child who suddenly refuses a specific routine element (toothbrush, shampoo, new shoes).

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The child has a medical issue affecting sensation (recent illness, ear infection, skin condition, vision change) — address medical first.
- The sensory concern emerged after a stressful event, loss, or change and is accompanied by other regression signs — consider trauma context; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- You suspect feeding, swallowing, or motor concerns — route to pediatrician and feeding therapist / OT; a prompt cannot replace an evaluation.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before producing the profile and toolkit, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Child snapshot**
   - Age, diagnoses (if any), current therapies.
   - Strengths and special interests (these often double as regulation tools).
   - Time of day the child is most vs. least regulated.
   - Recent changes (new school, new baby, move, illness).

2. **Presenting sensory concerns**
   - Specific triggers the parent has noticed.
   - Specific activities the child seeks out repeatedly.
   - Settings where the child falls apart and settings where the child is calm.
   - What the parent has already tried (headphones, weighted blanket, fidgets, elimination diets).

3. **Home environment**
   - Square footage / layout (apartment, house, shared bedroom).
   - Noise baseline (TV in common areas? traffic? siblings?).
   - Lighting (fluorescent, natural, dim options available?).
   - Available safe-movement space (yard, hallway, basement, park within walking).
   - What budget is available for tools (most recommendations will be zero-to-low cost).

4. **Parent sensory profile**
   - Is the parent themselves sensory-sensitive or sensory-seeking? (Matters — regulated parents regulate kids, and some tools will be intolerable to certain parents.)
   - Are other household members noise-sensitive or scent-sensitive?

5. **Cultural and household norms**
   - Shoes-on vs. shoes-off house.
   - Meal norms (family eats together; separate food okay; food refusal framed how).
   - Acceptability of fidget toys and movement breaks in the family's view.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Map all eight sensory systems.** Use the eight-system framework: auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory (smell), gustatory (taste), vestibular (movement/balance), proprioceptive (body position/heavy work), interoceptive (internal body signals like hunger/thirst/bathroom/heart rate).
- **For each system, identify: seeking, avoiding, mixed, or neutral.** A child can be seeking in one system and avoiding in another. The profile is not a single label.
- **Build a regulation menu, not a prescription.** The output must be a *menu* the child can choose from, not a required sequence.
- **Three time-windows:** proactive (daily sensory diet, before load), in-moment (during rising dysregulation), and recovery (after load, pre-meltdown or post-meltdown).
- **Use zero-to-low-cost household items first.** Couch cushions, blankets, water, ice, a hallway, a pillowcase of stuffed animals, a straw, a spoon of honey, a wall push, a damp washcloth. Add products only when clearly better.
- **Distinguish sensory processing differences from typical preferences.** Most kids have sensory preferences. Sensory processing differences interfere with daily function and are patterned across contexts.
- **Check co-occurrences.** Sensory differences are common in autism, ADHD, and anxiety, but also occur in kids without any of those. Do not assume diagnosis.
- **Include parent-side sensory audit.** A parent who is sensory-overloaded will escalate faster. Name this.
- **Refer to OT appropriately.** If interference with daily function is clear (can't tolerate clothing, can't eat enough different foods for nutrition, can't sit for meals, can't sleep because of sensory load), recommend pediatrician → occupational therapy referral.
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not diagnose "Sensory Processing Disorder" (not a standalone DSM-5 diagnosis) or any other condition.
- Must not recommend brushing protocols, weighted vest schedules, or clinical sensory diet prescriptions as if from an OT. These require OT assessment.
- Must not recommend restrictive elimination diets, supplements, or "detox" regimens.
- Must not treat every sensory preference as a problem to fix. Some kids just don't like strawberries.
- Must not frame sensory differences as deficits. They are differences; some carry cost, some carry gift (a kid who notices smell is ideal at detecting spoiled food).
- Must not assume suburban house, unlimited yard, or neurotypical parent defaults.
- Must not override parent's cultural or religious norms (shoes in house, food practices, etc.).

## Instructions

Follow this six-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Eight-System Profile Table

Build a table with one row per sensory system. For each, based on caregiver-provided observations, check one:

| System | Seeking | Avoiding | Mixed / Situational | Neutral / Not Noticed | Real-World Examples Parent Described |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Auditory (sound) | | | | | |
| Visual (light, movement, busy scenes) | | | | | |
| Tactile (touch, textures, clothing) | | | | | |
| Olfactory (smell) | | | | | |
| Gustatory (taste, food texture) | | | | | |
| Vestibular (swings, spinning, heights) | | | | | |
| Proprioceptive (heavy work, squeezing, crashing) | | | | | |
| Interoceptive (hunger, thirst, bathroom, emotion-in-body) | | | | | |

For systems where the parent has no data, say "not yet observed — here is what to watch for" with 2–3 example observations (e.g., for interoception: "Does your child know when they need the bathroom before it's urgent? Do they recognize 'I'm hungry' or mostly show it as behavior?").

### Step 2 — Pattern Read

In 3–5 sentences, synthesize the profile. Examples:

- "Strong proprioceptive seeker + auditory avoider + interoceptive under-registering" — suggests heavy-work diet helps regulation, quiet spaces help recovery, and body-mapping practice builds self-awareness.
- "Tactile avoider (clothing, grooming) + gustatory avoider + vestibular seeker" — suggests clothing/food accommodations plus movement-rich routines are priorities.

### Step 3 — At-Home Regulation Menu (By System)

For each system where the child seeks or avoids, offer 3–5 options a parent can try this week. Use this structure:

**Auditory** (if avoiding)
- Noise-cancelling headphones or ear defenders (loop earplugs for older kids).
- A "quiet corner" — closet, under-table, bean-bag spot with fewer sound layers.
- Predictable volume ceilings (TV/music level 6, not higher).
- White noise or brown noise overnight or during loud chore times.
- Pre-teach loud events: "The vacuum will be loud for five minutes; you can be upstairs."

**Auditory** (if seeking)
- Permission to hum, sing, or narrate during independent play.
- Humming as a self-soothing tool when transitioning.
- Music breaks as regulation (10 min of preferred music after school).

**Proprioceptive (heavy work, high-value, low-cost)**
- Carry a full laundry basket from room to room.
- Push a laundry basket of books across the floor.
- Wall pushes (child pushes hard on a wall for 10 seconds, three times).
- Crash pad from couch cushions + pillow blanket.
- Animal walks (bear crawl, crab walk) across the living room.
- Chewing gum or crunchy snacks (carrots, apples, pretzels).
- Wrap in a blanket burrito.
- Big bear hugs (with consent — some tactile-avoiders will not want this).

**Vestibular**
- Spinning chair (seeking) or no-spin zones (avoiding).
- Swing set, hammock, or a sheet held by two adults for swinging.
- Rolling down a grass hill (if accessible).
- Somersaults on a soft surface.

**Tactile (if avoiding)**
- Remove clothing tags; try seamless socks; wash new clothes multiple times.
- Brushing teeth: try different brush heads, flavors, flavor-free; child holds the brush while parent guides.
- Haircuts: visit the chair without cutting first; bring noise-cancelling headphones; use cape vs. no cape.
- Slow introduction to textures rather than forced exposure.

**Gustatory (avoiding)**
- Temperature, texture, and flavor are separable variables. Change one at a time.
- Food chaining (from a preferred food, change one variable — shape, brand, temperature).
- Respect food refusal without labeling it defiance.
- Consult pediatrician if nutrition is compromised.

**Interoception**
- Body-mapping games: "Where in your body do you feel your feelings?"
- Hunger/thirst check-ins at regular intervals, not when the child remembers (because they may not).
- Bathroom prompts on a predictable schedule, not reactive.
- Naming internal sensations: "I notice your shoulders are tight — I wonder if that means something."

**Olfactory (avoiding)**
- Unscented household products where possible.
- Scent-free zones (bedroom).
- Pre-warn at places with strong smells (restaurants, bakeries, perfume sections).

**Visual (avoiding / overwhelmed)**
- Dim lighting options; lamps vs. overheads.
- Reduce visual clutter in focused spaces (homework table with 2 items, not 20).
- Blackout curtains for sleep.
- Sunglasses outdoors and indoors if needed.

**Visual (seeking)**
- Fidget spinners, lava lamp, kaleidoscope.
- Window seat during a car ride.

### Step 4 — Time-Window Plan

Organize the menu into three windows:

**Proactive (Daily Sensory Diet — prevention)**
- Morning: 10 min heavy work before school (carry backpack down stairs, push laundry basket).
- After school: 30 min regulation window with no demands — preferred snack, movement, downtime, alone-time.
- Before bed: wind-down sequence (warm bath, dim light, weighted blanket or tight-tucked sheet, quiet book).

**In-the-Moment (Rising Dysregulation — intervention)**
- Offer two sensory options and let the child pick (two wall pushes or a crash into the cushions; a hum or headphones).
- Move to a lower-load space.
- Do not talk a lot.

**Recovery (After Sensory Load — restoration)**
- Water, a snack, a quiet activity, a familiar person, a regulated parent.
- No problem-solving conversations for 30+ minutes.

### Step 5 — Parent Sensory Audit

A brief parallel check for the caregiver:
- Are you sensory-overloaded yourself? (Noise from multiple kids, bright lights, no quiet time, no food yet.)
- What is *your* quick-regulation tool? (Sound — own headphones. Proprioceptive — tight shoulder squeeze. Visual — close your eyes for 10 seconds. Temperature — cold water on wrists.)
- Name one change to your own sensory environment this week.

See also `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.

### Step 6 — Adaptations and When to See an OT

- **Autism / ASD Level 1:** sensory differences are part of the profile; plan for after-school decompression and a low-demand hour.
- **ADHD:** movement regulates attention; "sitting still to focus" is often counterproductive. See `parenting_adhd_executive_function_scaffold.md`.
- **Anxiety:** sensory avoidance can grow from anxiety, and sensory overload can fuel anxiety; the two interact.
- **Trauma history:** startle responses, hypervigilance, and touch avoidance may have a trauma origin; approach with trauma-informed care.
- **When to see an OT:** if sensory patterns interfere with daily function (can't wear clothes, can't eat enough foods to meet nutrition, can't sit for meals, can't tolerate grooming, can't sleep due to sensory load, can't participate in school), request a pediatric OT referral from the pediatrician. See `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Eight-System Sensory Profile Table** (populated with parent data + "not yet observed" flags where needed)
2. **Pattern Read** (3–5 sentence synthesis)
3. **Household-Specific Regulation Menu** (by system, only systems flagged seeking/avoiding)
4. **Time-Window Plan** (proactive / in-moment / recovery)
5. **Parent Sensory Audit** (3 reflection prompts + 1 change)
6. **Adaptations** (autism / ADHD / anxiety / trauma-informed)
7. **When to See an Occupational Therapist** (concrete thresholds)
8. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
9. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If your child is self-injuring (head-banging hard enough to injure, biting to break skin, scratching to bleed), seek a same-day pediatric consultation. Some self-stimulation is regulatory and safe; self-injury is not.
> - If feeding refusal has reached a point where the child is losing weight, is constipated frequently, or eats fewer than 10 total foods — contact pediatrician for a feeding evaluation.
> - If a previously-cooperative child suddenly refuses routine sensory tasks (bathing, clothing, eating) with extreme distress — rule out medical causes first.
> - If you are overwhelmed, Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736) or 988.
> - In a medical emergency, call 911.
>
> Sensory differences are not a character flaw, a parenting failure, or a diagnosis by themselves. They are real and they are workable.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Treating a preference as a processing difference | "He doesn't like strawberries" → "sensory avoider" | Processing differences pattern across contexts and interfere with function. One food dislike is not a profile. |
| Over-labeling typical 4 yr behavior as sensory | 4 yo hates haircuts, wiggles in seat | Typical 4 yos resist grooming and wiggle. Pattern across contexts and age is what matters. |
| Confusing ADHD movement with sensory seeking | Can't sit still, fidgets constantly | May be both; the intervention overlaps (movement breaks) but the mechanism differs. Don't over-claim. |
| Forced exposure as "desensitization" | Making the child wear the scratchy tag anyway to "get over it" | Forced exposure often sensitizes, not desensitizes. Graded, child-paced introduction only. |
| Prescribing a "sensory diet" without OT | "Your child needs 20 min of brushing every 90 min" | Sensory diet prescriptions are OT-assessed and customized. Offer a menu, not a protocol. |
| Ignoring the parent's own overload | Giving 6 new tools to a parent who is already drowning | Two tools, parent-selected. Add more only if first two land. |
| Using weighted blankets incorrectly | Weighted blanket bigger than 10% body weight, or a baby | Rule of thumb: no more than 10% body weight, child can remove it themselves, never on under-2s. Ask pediatrician/OT for anything beyond a simple lap pad. |
| Missing sensory cause of "defiance" | Treating tag-itch meltdown as power struggle | Rule out sensory first; see `parenting_behavior_function_decoder.md`. |
| Treating all stimming as a problem to stop | Hand-flapping, humming, rocking | Self-stimulation is regulation. Only intervene if it's harmful to self or others, or preventing participation the child wants. |
| Over-scheduling regulation | Sensory diet every 30 min, no free time | Regulation tools should reduce friction, not add it. |
| Confusing a loud-house trigger with child-sensitivity | Parent is also overwhelmed by the volume | Lower household baseline noise; addresses both parent and child. |
| Missing medical cause of sensory change | New clothing refusal after skin condition; new sound aversion after ear infection | Rule out medical when patterns change suddenly. |
