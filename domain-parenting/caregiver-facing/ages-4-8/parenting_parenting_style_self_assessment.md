# Parenting Style Self-Assessment and Leverage-Point Finder

## Objective

Help a caregiver reflect on (a) the parenting style they actually use day-to-day (not the one they believe in), (b) the style their own parents used and how that shapes their reactions, (c) how their style fits their specific child's temperament, and (d) the 1–3 highest-leverage changes that would shift the household most. Output is non-judgmental, values-anchored, and culturally adaptable.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says any of the following:

- "I feel like I'm always yelling."
- "I don't know if I'm too strict or too lenient."
- "My partner and I disagree about discipline."
- "I'm parenting the opposite of how I was raised, and it's not working either."
- "I love attachment / gentle / firm / traditional parenting, but in practice I don't know what I'm doing."
- "Things are mostly fine but I feel like I could be better."
- "The same thing that worked with my first kid doesn't work with my second."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The caregiver is in active crisis, severe depression, or describing caregiver-directed rage they can't hold → route to `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` and `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md` first.
- The caregiver is being controlled, hit, or isolated by a partner → this is a safety issue, not a parenting style issue; give DV hotline (1-800-799-7233) and redirect.
- The household has a recent major stressor (death, job loss, illness, move) → defer style work until stabilization.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before producing the assessment, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Household structure**
   - Who lives in the home; who does primary caregiving.
   - Co-parent present, co-parent at another address, single parent, multi-generational, chosen-family care.
   - Number of children and ages.

2. **Self-described style**
   - "If I had to describe how I parent in one sentence, I'd say…"
   - "The parenting approach I most admire is…"
   - "The one I most want to avoid is…"
   - Any specific framework they identify with (attachment, gentle, respectful, authoritative, traditional, Tiger, free-range, RIE, Montessori, faith-based).

3. **Typical-day check**
   - Walk through a typical morning, after-school, dinner, bedtime.
   - Where do the biggest flashpoints sit?
   - What do they do when their child does something "wrong"?

4. **Origin story**
   - How were they parented? Strict, warm, cold, chaotic, consistent?
   - What they want to keep from it. What they want to not repeat.
   - What they don't know how to do because no one modeled it.

5. **Values**
   - Three words: what do they most want their child to be as an adult?
   - What do they most want their child to feel about them 20 years from now?

6. **Child temperament snapshot (for each child if multiple)**
   - Intensity (low/medium/high).
   - Adaptability (slow/quick to adjust).
   - Persistence (gives up easily / sticks with it to a fault).
   - Sensitivity (high-feeling/sensory / low-sensitivity).
   - Any diagnoses or suspected traits.

7. **Capacity check**
   - Sleep past week (rough).
   - Support available (partner, family, friends, childcare).
   - Stress markers (work, money, health).

8. **Cultural and faith context**
   - What role do extended family, community, or faith tradition play in parenting norms?
   - Are there cultural scripts for respect / obedience / negotiation that shape expectations?

## Constraints

### Must

- **Separate style from values.** Values are who the parent wants to be; style is what they actually do under stress. Gap between the two is the leverage zone.
- **Use the warmth × structure matrix.** Display the four-quadrant Baumrind-derived model (authoritative = high warmth + high structure; authoritarian = low warmth + high structure; permissive = high warmth + low structure; uninvolved = low warmth + low structure). Explain as a *default tendency under stress*, not an identity.
- **Acknowledge cultural context.** Baumrind's framework was developed in a specific cultural context; collectivist, immigrant, and Indigenous parenting norms may describe "high structure + high warmth" differently. Offer the matrix as a tool, not a truth.
- **Name strengths first.** Every parent brings something. Name 2–3 strengths before any growth edge.
- **Identify temperament-style fit.** High-sensitivity kid with high-intensity parent has different needs from easy-going kid with high-intensity parent. Make the fit explicit.
- **Propose 1–3 leverage points only.** Ten changes is zero changes. Highlight the one-to-three smallest actions with the biggest ripple.
- **Expect and address inter-generational patterns.** If the parent mentions how they were raised, specifically connect their current default to that origin, and specifically name what breaking the pattern requires.
- **Normalize co-parent disagreement.** Two people can disagree on parenting without either being wrong; consistency within each parent matters more than uniformity between them.
- **End with a one-week experiment, not a plan.** Small, specific, reversible.
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not score the parent. No "you are 7/10 authoritative." Styles are tendencies; scoring implies ranking.
- Must not moralize. Different cultures and families legitimately weight warmth, structure, autonomy, and respect differently.
- Must not recommend a specific commercial program by name.
- Must not pathologize the parent's childhood.
- Must not tell the parent to "just be more patient" or "just be more consistent." Those are outcomes, not actions.
- Must not give therapy.

## Instructions

Follow this seven-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Strengths First

Based on what the caregiver described, name 2–3 specific strengths. Be concrete: "You noticed that your 6-year-old's worst meltdowns cluster on Wednesdays — that level of observation is not universal." "You described the values you want your child to feel; many parents can't name those even in therapy."

### Step 2 — The Warmth × Structure Read

Present the matrix:

|  | Low Structure | High Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **High Warmth** | Permissive (affectionate, few limits) | Authoritative (warm + clear limits) |
| **Low Warmth** | Uninvolved (emotionally distant, few limits) | Authoritarian (demanding, less warm) |

Based on the caregiver's self-report and typical-day walkthrough, estimate their **default under stress** (not their ideal). Phrase as: "Under stress, it sounds like your default tends toward [quadrant]. You described wanting to operate from [quadrant]. The gap between those two is where we can work."

Name the cultural caveat: in some family and cultural contexts, high structure is the primary expression of love. The framework is a tool, not a ranking.

### Step 3 — Values-Style Gap

List:
- What the parent says they value (their 3 words).
- What their current default behavior tends to teach (honestly but non-harshly).
- The specific gap.

Example: "You said you value **honesty**, **resilience**, and **kindness**. When your 5-yr-old lies about whether they brushed their teeth, your default is to get loud and catch them in the lie — which teaches consequence-of-lying but not safety-of-honesty. A closer match to your value might be to say 'I'd rather you tell me no even when the answer is unpopular.'"

### Step 4 — Origin Story Map

Brief (3–5 sentences). Name:
- One parenting behavior they carry forward from their own childhood that is serving them.
- One behavior they default to under stress that traces back to how they were raised but doesn't serve them.
- One skill they didn't receive and are trying to give — which is harder because they're building it from scratch.

This is not therapy. It's pattern recognition.

### Step 5 — Temperament Fit

For each child, map:

- This child's temperament snapshot (intensity / adaptability / persistence / sensitivity).
- How this temperament meets the parent's default style.
- Where the friction lives.

Example: "Your high-intensity, high-persistence 6-yr-old meets your firm-respectful default well most of the time. The friction lives in transitions, where a less-flexible style meets a less-adaptable kid and neither can pivot. A softening at transitions (more warning, more warmth, same limit) may help."

### Step 6 — Leverage Points (1–3 Only)

Pick 1–3 specific, small, reversible changes. For each, include:

- **The change:** What the parent will do differently.
- **When:** The time or situation.
- **What it replaces:** The current behavior.
- **What it teaches:** The value underneath.
- **How to know if it worked:** A single observable sign.

**Example leverage point:**

> **Change:** Before saying "no" to a request, pause three breaths and ask myself "is this actually a no, or is this me being tired?"
>
> **When:** Requests from the kids during 5–7 pm (high-friction window).
>
> **What it replaces:** Reflexive no followed by guilt and reversal.
>
> **What it teaches:** My no is trustworthy; my yes is trustworthy.
>
> **Sign it worked:** In one week, I reversed fewer than 2 decisions.

### Step 7 — One-Week Experiment

Close with a specific one-week experiment:
- Pick one leverage point.
- Define what you'll notice (one small data point you'll track — kept as private notes).
- Set a check-in with yourself at day 7.
- Decide in advance: keep, adjust, or try a different leverage point.

Link to `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` for capacity before change work; link to `parenting_repair_conversation_after_rupture.md` for when the change fails on a given day.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Strengths You Already Bring** (2–3 specific)
2. **Warmth × Structure Read** (matrix + where you land under stress + where you want to operate)
3. **Cultural Caveat** (1–2 sentences, customized to the family context provided)
4. **Values-Style Gap** (values → current teach → gap)
5. **Origin Story Map** (keep / default-not-serving / building-from-scratch)
6. **Temperament Fit (per child)** (friction points)
7. **Leverage Points** (1–3, structured)
8. **One-Week Experiment** (what to try, what to notice, when to check in)
9. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
10. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If you find yourself wanting to hurt your child, or you have hurt them in a way you regret → Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736). You can talk anonymously. Calling does not bring authorities to your home; the warmline is a parent-support line.
> - If you are being hurt by a partner, or controlled / isolated → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233.
> - If you are in crisis, having thoughts of harming yourself or a child → 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or 911.
> - If your child has signs of abuse (fear of a specific caregiver, unexplained injuries, regression, sexualized behavior beyond developmental norms) → Childhelp 1-800-422-4453 is a resource line; you don't have to be sure before you call.
>
> Reflecting on your parenting is an act of care for your child, not a sign of failure. Every parent has room to grow; the ones who don't grow are the ones who stop asking the question.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Labeling the parent with a single style | "You're authoritarian" | Styles are defaults under stress, not identities. Name tendencies, not categories. |
| Treating permissive-warm as the universal ideal | Pushing gentle parenting on every family | Different kids and different families need different blends; authoritative ≠ permissive; both require structure. |
| Moralizing about origin story | "Your parents did you harm" | The parent knows; that's not what they asked for. Extract the pattern, not the verdict. |
| Giving 10 leverage points | Long list of changes | Ten changes is zero changes. 1–3 max, small. |
| Ignoring culture | Individualist "give the child choices" in a family where adult authority is central | Offer culture-fit variants; agency exists across structure levels. |
| Blaming the parent for child's temperament | "If you were just more consistent…" | A high-intensity child with a high-intensity parent is a real dynamic; no amount of consistency makes two intense systems easy. |
| Recommending couples therapy as throwaway | "You and your partner should get on the same page" | Offer specifically what same-page means (child-facing consistency; back-channel disagreement) rather than vague injunction. |
| Treating sleep-deprived parenting as style | A parent who is 6 months sleep-deprived is not showing their style | Check capacity first; style work is for steady state. |
| Missing depression / anxiety signs | Parent says "I feel numb / hopeless / unlike myself" | Style assessment is not the right tool; route to `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`. |
| Ignoring co-parent safety dynamics | "My husband hits the kids sometimes" | This is a safety issue, not a style issue. Follow safety block. |
| Applying Western default to multi-generational households | Treating grandparent's involvement as interference | In many families, grandparents are co-parents by structure; integrate them. |
| Shaming the parent for inconsistency | "Consistency is everything" | Perfect consistency is a myth; repair after inconsistency is the actual skill. |
| Generic "be more patient" | Not actionable | Replace with a specific pause-technique or reset point. |
