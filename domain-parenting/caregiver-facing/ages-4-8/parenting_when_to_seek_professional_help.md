**Purpose:** Convert "should we see someone?" into a concrete recommendation: **who, how soon, how to frame it, and what to bring.** Most parents wait too long because they can't tell which door to knock on. This guide maps common concerns to the right first professional, clarifies urgency, and gives a script for the appointment.

**When to use:** Any parenting concern where the parent is wondering whether to call a professional. Before and after a pediatrician visit. When school has raised a concern. When the parent's gut says something is off.

**When NOT to use:** This replaces no professional's judgment. If you're unsure whether something is urgent, err toward calling. Same-day pediatric calls are what pediatric nurse lines exist for.

---

## Safety Block (Call Today / Now)

**Call 911 or go to the ER if:**
- The child is actively harming themselves or someone else in a way that could cause injury.
- The child has ingested something dangerous, has a high fever with lethargy, is having trouble breathing, or any medical emergency.
- The child is expressing suicidal intent with a plan or access to means.

**Call today (same-day appointment, nurse line, or crisis line) if:**
- The child says they want to die, kill themselves, disappear, or not exist — in any words, at any age. Call the pediatrician same-day; 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US: call/text 988).
- The child is self-injuring (repetitive head-banging on hard surfaces, biting self, cutting).
- The child is talking about hurting another child or an animal in a specific, planned way.
- You suspect abuse (by you, by another caregiver, or by anyone else). Contact your pediatrician and/or Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline: 1-800-422-4453. Pediatricians, teachers, and mental health professionals are mandated reporters.
- A previously acquired skill has been lost (speech, toileting, motor, self-care). Regression is a pediatric red flag.
- You are afraid of your child, or afraid of what you might do to your child. Parent Warmline (US): 1-855-4APARENT.
- Sudden, dramatic change in the child's personality, sleep, appetite, or behavior lasting more than a few days with no clear cause (infection, sleep loss, life event).

**This is not the time for a self-help prompt. It is the time for a phone call.**

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Assign urgency: **Emergency / Today / This Week / This Month / Within 3 Months / Watchful Waiting with Check-In.**
- Name the first professional to call (usually pediatrician, not directly a specialist).
- Give an appointment script in two registers (warm-direct, firm-direct).
- Recommend what to document and bring.
- Note insurance, school, and Early Intervention / special education routes in parallel when relevant — they're not mutually exclusive.
- Flag when waiting is not okay (regression, suicidal statements, abuse, safety).
- Address the common parent fears that cause delay: "making a big deal out of nothing," "labeling the child," "what if they judge us."

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose.
- Recommend bypassing the pediatrician for first evaluations when the pediatrician is the correct gateway.
- Tell a parent their worry isn't valid — the parent's pattern-detection is a signal.
- Treat "wait and see" as a default. Waiting has costs; early evaluation opens services.
- Assume middle-class US access defaults — many families need county, school-based, or community routes.
- Shame a parent who waited. That's already behind them.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Urgency Sort

Slot the concern into one of six urgency tiers. Be explicit.

| Tier | Examples | Action |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Emergency (now)** | Active safety risk, suicidal plan + means, serious injury | 911 / ER |
| **Same-Day** | Suicidal statements without plan, self-injury, regression, abuse suspicion, acute mental-status change | Pediatrician same-day, nurse line, 988, 1-800-422-4453 |
| **This Week** | New sudden anxiety preventing school, acute family crisis with the child in distress, concerning behavior escalation over days | Pediatrician appointment this week |
| **This Month** | Persistent attention / behavior / mood patterns that cross settings, school concern raised, social concerns persisting through the school year | Pediatrician appointment this month; start documentation now |
| **Within 3 Months** | Suspected ADHD / autism / learning concerns with stable presentation, long-standing sensory or motor concerns, speech clarity issues | Pediatrician this month; evaluations often have waitlists — start now |
| **Watchful Waiting + Check-In** | Single incidents with clear cause, intense-but-typical behavior in context | Track for 4–6 weeks; pediatrician visit if pattern persists |

### Step 2 — Who to Call First

In almost every case: **the pediatrician is the gateway.** They do the first screen and referrals, they know local specialists and waitlists, and insurance often requires a referral anyway. Skipping them is usually slower, not faster.

After the pediatrician, the specialist map for 4–8-year-olds:

| Concern Pattern | First Specialist |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Attention, focus, hyperactivity, impulsivity across settings | Pediatrician → developmental pediatrician, OR psychologist for ADHD evaluation; some pediatricians manage ADHD directly |
| Social communication, restricted interests, sensory + social pattern | Pediatrician → developmental pediatrician and/or psychologist for autism evaluation; SLP for social communication |
| Anxiety, depression, big mood shifts, school refusal | Pediatrician → child psychologist or child psychiatrist if medication is on the table |
| Speech clarity, language delays, stuttering | Pediatrician → speech-language pathologist (SLP); often accessible through school |
| Sensory sensitivities, motor clumsiness, self-care delays, handwriting | Pediatrician → occupational therapist (OT); often accessible through school |
| Reading, writing, math struggles | School → evaluation for specific learning disability; private psychoeducational evaluation is an option |
| Hearing concerns | Pediatrician → audiologist |
| Vision concerns | Pediatric optometrist / ophthalmologist |
| Sleep concerns (night terrors, sleepwalking with risk, suspected sleep apnea) | Pediatrician → pediatric sleep specialist |
| Eating — extreme restriction, weight loss, purging, obsession with food | Pediatrician → pediatric eating disorder specialist (rarer but real at 7–8) |
| Trauma exposure (loss, violence, medical, accident) | Pediatrician → trauma-informed child therapist |
| Family therapy / co-parenting support | Licensed marriage and family therapist (LMFT) or child and family psychologist |

### Step 3 — Parallel Tracks (Don't Wait for One to Move Before Starting Another)

Because waitlists for developmental evaluations can be 3–12+ months, start these in parallel:

- **School-based evaluations:** Request in writing. Federal law (US, IDEA) requires schools to evaluate children suspected of having a disability, often at no cost. 504 plans and IEPs are school-side tools that work alongside clinical diagnosis.
- **Early Intervention (under 3) / Child Find (3–5) / Special Education (5+):** State-level programs, free to the family, often faster than private clinical routes.
- **Private clinical evaluation:** Often more thorough; often insurance-covered with a referral; often with a waitlist.
- **Insurance navigation:** Call the insurance BH (behavioral health) line to request in-network pediatric specialists. In the US, under the Mental Health Parity Act, BH must be covered comparably to medical.
- **Community options:** Federally Qualified Health Centers, county mental health, training-clinic sliding scales at graduate programs, telehealth for specialty care.

### Step 4 — What to Document Before the Appointment

The more concrete your documentation, the more useful the visit.

- **Plain-language description of the behavior/concern** (what a camera would see).
- **Frequency and duration** over 2 weeks if you can.
- **Settings** — home, school, grandma's, other.
- **Triggers and patterns** — time of day, before/after certain events.
- **What you've tried and how it went.**
- **Teacher's perspective in writing** — even a short email from a teacher is gold.
- **Developmental timeline** — any delayed or advanced milestones, any regressions.
- **Family history** — ADHD, autism, anxiety, depression, learning differences run in families and are relevant.
- **Current sleep, eating, and activity baseline.**

### Step 5 — Appointment Script

The first sentence in the appointment sets the tone. Don't bury it.

**Warm-direct:** *"I'm worried about [specific pattern]. I've been tracking it for [duration], and it's showing up in [settings]. It's interfering with [life impact]. I'd like to understand whether this is within the typical range or whether an evaluation is worth doing."*

**Firm-direct:** *"I'd like to request an evaluation for [concern]. Here's what I've documented. If you think this is within typical range, I'd still like to know what we'd watch for. If evaluation is warranted, who do you refer to, and what should we start in parallel?"*

If the pediatrician is dismissive, you can say: *"I hear you that this may be typical. I'd still like a formal evaluation because [reasoning]. Can you refer us, or should we go through the school?"*

You are allowed to persist. Parents who persist get their kids seen faster.

### Step 6 — Address Common Delay Traps

- **"I don't want to label my child."** Evaluation is not labeling. Assessment opens services. Services help the child function. The label lives in a file; the support lives in daily life.
- **"What if they think I'm overreacting?"** A pediatrician's job is to evaluate. Overreacting to a false alarm costs less than under-reacting to a real signal.
- **"My mother-in-law says I'm making a big deal out of nothing."** Your mother-in-law is not the evaluator.
- **"We can't afford it."** Start with the pediatrician and the school. Both routes have free or low-cost paths. Ask about sliding scales at teaching clinics.
- **"My co-parent disagrees."** Start the evaluation. Co-parents don't have to agree on the interpretation to agree on gathering data.
- **"We tried once and it was a bad experience."** One provider doesn't represent the whole field. Try a different one.

### Step 7 — What to Expect After the Referral

Briefly, so the parent isn't caught off guard:
- Waitlists are long. 2–12 months for autism / developmental evaluations in many US metros.
- Evaluations often take multiple sessions, parent and teacher rating scales, and a written report.
- The report is yours. You can share parts of it with the school and keep others private.
- A diagnosis is information, not destiny. Many kids excel with supports; some don't need supports long-term.
- Medication is one tool. It is not a parenting failure. It is also not required.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Default to "wait and see" on a regression, suicidal statement, or abuse suspicion | Regression, self-harm talk, and abuse concerns are same-day calls |
| Recommend bypassing the pediatrician for most first evaluations | The pediatrician is the right first call in nearly all cases — faster, not slower, than direct-to-specialist |
| Treat a school-only concern as nothing | Cross-setting is more informative than single-setting, but single-setting persistent concerns matter too |
| Dismiss the parent's instinct | Parent instinct is data. Pair it with structured observation |
| Suggest only private routes when family access is limited | Name school evaluations, Medicaid, sliding scales, FQHCs, 988 |
| Pathologize "intense but typical" development | See `parenting_developmental_expectations_4_to_8.md`. Tier the concern first |
| Promise an evaluation will give clean answers | Evaluations often surface patterns, not always a single label — that's okay |
| Imply medication is a last resort or a moral failure | Medication is a tool families may consider with a clinician; framing here is neutral |
| Treat the referral as the end of parenting | Referrals are one input; parenting at home still matters alongside professional care |
| Assume the parent knows what each specialist does | Briefly name the specialist's role, not just the acronym |
