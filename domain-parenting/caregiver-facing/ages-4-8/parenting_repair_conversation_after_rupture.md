# Parent-Initiated Repair Conversation After Rupture

## Objective

Produce a short, age-appropriate, non-shaming repair script for a caregiver of a 4–8 year old after a rupture they caused — a yelling episode, a harsh tone, a broken promise, an unfair consequence, a cold withdrawal, or a moment they regret. The output (a) names what happened without spiraling, (b) takes accountability without dumping adult burden on the child, (c) rebuilds the specific connection that was damaged, and (d) avoids over-apologizing patterns that would teach the child to caretake the parent.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver describes:

- Yelling at or threatening the child.
- Saying something they regret ("you're being terrible," "I can't stand this," "go away").
- Breaking a promise (not reading the book, not going to the park, canceling the playdate last-minute).
- Delivering a consequence that was out of scale with what happened.
- Grabbing, pulling, or physically handling the child in a way that scared them.
- Withdrawing emotionally (silent treatment, going cold, locking the door).
- Reacting harshly in front of the child to a spouse, sibling, or event.
- A pattern of rupture without repair that they want to interrupt.
- A child who recently said "you don't love me," "you're mean," or "why are you always yelling."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The rupture involved hitting, shaking, or any physical harm → route to `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`, `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`, and Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT. Repair is still possible and necessary, but the parent also needs support beyond a script.
- The rupture is part of a pattern the parent cannot seem to interrupt (multiple times per week, escalating intensity) → capacity and support work precede script work.
- The parent is still flooded as they're writing or reading this → pause; do the reset first.
- The rupture was caused by the child and parent is not at fault → this is not a repair prompt; it's a limit-holding or discipline prompt (see `parenting_strong_willed_power_struggle_defuser.md`, `parenting_behavior_function_decoder.md`).

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before producing the script, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Child snapshot**
   - Age.
   - Verbal / communication style; any neurodivergence.
   - How this child tends to respond to apology (melts, stonewalls, gets clingy, gets angry, takes a long time to warm back up, goes "I'm fine" when not fine).

2. **The rupture**
   - What happened, brief. What was said or done.
   - What the child did or said in response.
   - How long ago (minutes, hours, a day, a week).
   - Is the parent still activated, or fully regulated?

3. **Parent state now**
   - Regulated enough to repair? (If not, pause and reset first.)
   - Feeling shame (which tends to make apologies long and self-focused), or feeling clear and grounded (which tends to make apologies short and child-focused)?
   - Tendency to over-apologize (turning apologies into child-caretaking moments) or under-apologize (defending, explaining, minimizing)?

4. **Context that may need acknowledgment**
   - Was the parent sleep-deprived, sick, grieving, in a fight with a partner, overworked? The child may have noticed. Adult-context naming at their level can help; details are not needed.

5. **Register**
   - Warm-tactile (hug-on-lap language) or firm-respectful (calm, eye-level, brief)? Offer both.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Keep it brief.** A 4-year-old can hold a 2-sentence repair. A 7-year-old can hold 4–6 sentences. An 8-year-old can hold a 2–3 minute conversation. More than that is adult-processing that doesn't belong on the child.
- **Accountability without adult-burden transfer.** The parent owns their behavior without explaining their internal life in detail. "I got too loud" is enough; "I was stressed because the boss yelled at me and I haven't slept and now the laundry is…" is not.
- **Name what happened concretely.** "When I yelled at you during breakfast" — specific, not "when I was a bad mom."
- **Acknowledge impact.** "That probably felt scary" or "I saw your face change."
- **State what you'll try** (not "I promise I'll never yell again"). Realistic, small, observable.
- **Return to connection, not to the argument.** Repair is not the time to re-prosecute what the child did wrong; that conversation happens separately if needed.
- **Three time windows:** immediate (within minutes, brief re-entry), same-day (fuller repair after child is regulated), delayed (hours or days later, especially for older kids or neurodivergent kids who need processing time).
- **Distinguish parent apology from child apology.** The parent is modeling accountability, not extracting forgiveness. The child is not required to say anything back.
- **Flag over-apologizing pattern.** If the parent over-apologizes, the repair script must be shorter, not longer.
- **Register-adaptive.** Provide warm-collaborative and firm-respectful variants.
- **Anchor developmentally.** 4yo needs body-level reconnection more than words. 8yo can hold some context.
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not tell the child "I'm sorry BUT you made me…" (false repair).
- Must not require the child to hug, kiss, say it's okay, or otherwise caretake the parent's feelings.
- Must not promise never to yell / never to get frustrated / to be perfect. That's dishonest and teaches unrealistic standards.
- Must not frame the rupture as the child's fault disguised as an apology.
- Must not recommend bribing or gift-based repair.
- Must not give therapy or deeply explore the parent's childhood trauma inside the repair script (may be relevant elsewhere; see `parenting_parenting_style_self_assessment.md`).
- Must not shame the parent. Every parent ruptures; the variable is repair.

## Instructions

Follow this six-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Regulation Check

Ask: is the parent regulated right now? Indicators: breath is steady, voice isn't tight, body feels their feet on the floor, they can hear the child's side without rebutting it, shame isn't running the show.

If not, pause. Link to `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` (90-second reset, 2-min reset, 5-min reset variants). Repair when flooded will repeat the rupture.

### Step 2 — Time-Window Classification

Classify which repair window applies:

- **Immediate (within minutes):** Brief re-entry; body proximity; a short "I yelled; I'm sorry about that; I'm going to take a minute." Not a full conversation.
- **Same-day (after child is calm):** The standard repair. 2–6 sentences depending on age.
- **Delayed (hours or days later):** For older kids, neurodivergent kids needing processing time, or situations where the parent was too flooded initially. Equally valid; repair is never too late.

### Step 3 — Script Elements (Four Parts, in Order)

1. **Name it concretely (what I did).** "When I yelled at you after you spilled the milk." One sentence.
2. **Own it (no excuse chained to the 'but').** "That wasn't about you. I was frustrated and I took it out on you." One sentence.
3. **Acknowledge impact (what they felt).** "That probably felt scary" or "I saw your face and I know it hurt." One sentence.
4. **State what you'll try.** "Next time when I feel that big feeling I'm going to step into the hallway for a minute first." One sentence.

Optional fifth element if the child is 6+ and receptive:

5. **Invitation (not requirement) to share.** "If there's anything you want me to know, I'm listening." Child is not required to respond.

Close with body-level reconnection for 4–5 yr olds (offered, not imposed): "Do you want a hug, or do you want me to sit next to you?"

### Step 4 — Register Variants

Provide both:

**Warm-collaborative (example, 5yo after yelling at breakfast):**
> "Hey, I want to talk to you about breakfast. I got really loud when you spilled the milk. That wasn't fair — I was frustrated and I yelled at you. I saw you get quiet and I'm sorry that happened. I'm working on stepping away when I feel that big mad feeling, so I don't yell at you. Do you want a hug, or do you want to just sit here a minute?"

**Firm-respectful (example, 7yo after a harsh consequence):**
> "Come here a second. I made your consequence bigger than it should have been last night. No TV for a week was too much. We'll talk about what actually fits tomorrow. I'm sorry I lost my patience. I love you."

### Step 5 — What Not to Do

Before returning the script, flag for the parent:

- **Don't pile on.** One repair, not five apologies over the course of the day.
- **Don't explain your internal life in detail.** Adult stress belongs with adults.
- **Don't require forgiveness.** The child owes you nothing.
- **Don't combine repair with a lecture.** If the child also did something that needs addressing, address it later, separately.
- **Don't break promise cycle.** If you promise to try something different, actually try it; broken repair-promises erode trust faster than the original rupture.
- **Don't rescript history.** "That didn't really happen the way you remember" is not repair; it's gaslighting.

### Step 6 — Adaptations

Append adaptation notes:

- **Strong-willed child:** may reject the apology or say "I'm still mad." That's okay. The apology was offered; the child's pace is their own. Do not press.
- **Autistic / ASD Level 1 child:** may need a few hours of processing time; may need the apology in writing (a note on their pillow); may want facts not feelings ("I yelled. That was wrong. Here's what I'll try"). Adjust accordingly.
- **ADHD child:** may not remember the rupture clearly or may be already moved on; repair is still valuable, keep it short.
- **Child who shuts down under any conflict:** keep it very brief, offer reconnection through an activity (sit next to them while they draw), don't require eye contact.
- **Child who regularly caretakes parent emotions ("parentified" child):** this is exactly the dynamic to interrupt. Keep apology brief and explicitly reassure: "You don't have to take care of my feelings. That's my job."
- **Recent trauma (divorce, death, move):** repair is especially important; keep consistent repair without over-indexing — every parental lapse is not a catastrophe.
- **Co-parent presence:** if the co-parent witnessed the rupture, consider whether the repair is one-on-one (usually better) or witnessed. Don't perform repair for the co-parent; do it for the child.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Regulation Check** (are you ready to repair now, or do you need a reset first?)
2. **Time Window** (immediate / same-day / delayed)
3. **Repair Script — Warm-Collaborative Register** (4–5 sentences, specific to this rupture)
4. **Repair Script — Firm-Respectful Register** (4–5 sentences, specific to this rupture)
5. **Body-Level Reconnection Offer** (age-matched)
6. **What NOT to Do** (5 explicit items)
7. **Adaptations** (for this specific child's profile)
8. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
9. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If the rupture involved hitting, shaking, grabbing hard, or any physical harm → step away, take care of the child's immediate safety, and call Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736). They will not report you for calling; this is a support line.
> - If you are finding yourself repeatedly losing control in ways that scare you → this is a capacity issue that a script alone can't fix. See `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
> - If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your child → 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or 911 for immediate safety.
> - If your child has talked about wanting to die, being worthless, or not wanting to be in the family → this requires same-week pediatric or mental-health attention, not just repair.
>
> Repair is not erasing. It's showing your child that people who love each other can hurt each other and come back — which is one of the most important things they'll ever learn.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Over-apologizing | Parent apologizes 6 times over 2 days; child starts reassuring parent | One repair, then move on. If urge to re-apologize, redirect into a regulation practice for self. |
| Under-apologizing / minimizing | "I just got a little frustrated" for an actual yelling episode | Name what actually happened; minimization teaches the child their reality is unreliable. |
| Conditional apology ("sorry but you…") | Shifts blame | Pure accountability; the child's behavior, if relevant, is a separate conversation later. |
| Long self-flagellation | "I'm the worst mom, I ruin everything…" | Centers the parent, not the child. Short accountability. |
| Gift or bribe repair | "Here's ice cream; we're good now" | Material repair without verbal accountability teaches that love is bought. |
| Demanding forgiveness | "Say it's okay" | Not the child's job. Offer, don't require. |
| Promising never to do it again | "I'll never yell again" | Sets up next-rupture as catastrophic betrayal. Promise smaller, realer action. |
| Rescripting history | "You're remembering it wrong" | Gaslighting, not repair. |
| Same rupture + repair cycle on repeat | Daily yell-repair-yell-repair | Capacity issue beyond scripts; see `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` and `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`. |
| Combining repair with lecture | Apology + "and now let me tell you what you did wrong" | Split into two conversations on different timelines. |
| Repair that shames a sibling | "I shouldn't have yelled at you, your brother was being the bad one" | Sibling triangulation; don't. |
| Performing repair for the co-parent | Apology as display | Face the child, not the witness. |
| Skipping repair because "they don't even remember" | Especially with young kids or ADHD | They may not remember the words but they remember the body state. Repair still matters. |
| Expecting immediate acceptance | Child says "still mad"; parent pushes | Child's pace is their own. Offer, leave the door open. |
| Treating repair as optional for parents who were "raised to never apologize" | "My parents never apologized to me, I'm fine" | The research on rupture-and-repair is well-established; the skill is learnable. |
