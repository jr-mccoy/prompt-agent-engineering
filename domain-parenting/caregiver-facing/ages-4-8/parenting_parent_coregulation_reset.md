**Purpose:** Help a parent of a 4–8-year-old (a) recognize the physical signs that their own nervous system is in fight/flight/freeze (flooded), (b) reset it in the 60–300 seconds available in real family life, and (c) notice when the bucket is chronically empty — not just this afternoon. A dysregulated parent cannot co-regulate a dysregulated child. Parent capacity is a strategy, not a luxury.

**When to use:** Before a predictable flashpoint. Mid-day after a rough morning. During a meltdown, between phases. At 5pm when bedtime is coming and dinner isn't made. When you notice yourself saying things you don't mean. When you've been running on fumes for weeks.

**When NOT to use:** This is not a substitute for therapy, psychiatric care, or safety planning. If you're having intrusive thoughts about harming yourself or your child, or you are afraid you will lose control with your child, skip straight to the Safety Block.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and reach out today if:
- You are having thoughts of harming yourself or your child.
- You are afraid of what you might do next.
- You are yelling daily, hitting, or scaring your child — and can't stop on your own.
- You're using alcohol or substances to get through the day.
- Intrusive scary thoughts about your child are frequent or feel real.

Call a professional today:
- **Postpartum Support International (all caregivers, not just postpartum):** 1-800-944-4773.
- **Parent Warmline (US):** 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736).
- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline:** 988.
- **Your primary care doctor or OBGYN (if postpartum within the last 12 months).**

You needing help is not a parenting failure. Getting help is parenting.

---

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Match the reset length to the time the parent actually has (30 seconds to 15 minutes).
- Normalize flooding as a nervous-system event, not a parenting character flaw.
- Include a "recognize it" step before a "fix it" step — parents often skip straight to fixing.
- Distinguish acute flooding (right now) from chronic depletion (chronic bucket empty).
- Name the 90-second rule (emotion chemistry clears in ~90 seconds if you don't keep feeding it with thought).
- Offer a co-regulation script for AFTER the reset: how to re-enter the room with the child.
- If the input indicates chronic depletion, name it plainly and recommend structural change, not just more breathing exercises.

**Must Not:**
- Tell the parent to "just take a bath" or "try yoga" — patronizing, and mostly not available mid-tantrum.
- Suggest techniques requiring 30+ minutes as the primary reset.
- Assume a partner, money, or childcare. Many parents don't have any of these.
- Shame rage, tears, collapse, or dissociation — they're all nervous-system responses.
- Recommend venting at the child as "being honest about feelings."
- Promise a single technique will work every time.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Recognize It

Flooding has a physical signature. Name the signals the parent is experiencing in their body. A brief taxonomy:

- **Fight signature:** Heat, tight jaw, tense hands, loud thoughts, urge to yell or slam, wanting to be right.
- **Flight signature:** Racing heart, shallow breath, urge to leave the room, scan for exits, "I can't do this."
- **Freeze signature:** Numbness, foggy, stuck, can't make a decision, watching yourself from outside.
- **Fawn signature:** Over-accommodating, saying yes when you mean no, smoothing, over-apologizing to a 5-year-old.

Say it out loud internally: "My nervous system is in [fight / flight / freeze / fawn]. This is my body. It is not who I am."

### Step 2 — The 30-Second Reset

For the moment when the child is watching and you have no exit.

1. **Drop your shoulders.** Physically lower them. Your body leads.
2. **Exhale longer than you inhale.** Inhale 4 counts, exhale 6–8. Do 3 cycles. This tells the vagus nerve to downshift.
3. **Feel your feet on the floor.** Press them down. Literally. This is a grounding move that takes 5 seconds.
4. **One anchor phrase:** "I am the adult. This is a hard moment. It will pass." Or: "I'm here. I'm okay. We're okay."

This is not a miracle. It buys 30 seconds of prefrontal-cortex access. Often, that's enough.

### Step 3 — The 2-Minute Reset

When you can step 6 feet away without the child becoming unsafe.

1. **Narrate your exit:** "I'm going to take a breath in the bathroom. I'll be back in 2 minutes. You're safe. I love you." Age-appropriate: a 4-year-old may cry at your leaving; a 7-year-old usually gets it.
2. **Water on the wrists or splash the face.** Cold water on the face engages the mammalian dive reflex and drops heart rate fast.
3. **Unclench jaw, tongue off roof of mouth, soften forehead.** Most flooded parents are locked in their face.
4. **4-7-8 breath:** Inhale 4, hold 7, exhale 8. Two rounds.
5. **Anchor before you re-enter:** "I'm going back in as the regulated one."

### Step 4 — The 5-Minute Reset

When another adult can hold the room, or the child is occupied/safe.

1. Step outside if possible — literally. Outdoor air resets faster than indoor air.
2. Move your body: walk, wall push-ups, shake out hands, jog in place. The mammal needs to discharge.
3. Name the emotion to yourself, specifically: "I am furious that he won't put on his shoes for the fourth time in a row, and underneath I'm afraid I'm a bad parent." Naming moves activity from amygdala to prefrontal cortex.
4. Drink water. Eat something small if you haven't. Dysregulated parents are often hungry parents.
5. Pick ONE next move, not five. The flood wants to redesign the whole day. Pick one thing for the next 20 minutes.

### Step 5 — Re-Entry Script

Walking back into the room is its own step. Don't skip it.

- **If you stayed regulated:** "I took a breath. I'm back. What were we doing?" Matter-of-fact.
- **If you left in a bad moment:** "I needed a minute. I'm back. I love you." Don't over-explain to a 5-year-old.
- **If you lost it before stepping out:** "I yelled earlier. That was my mistake. I'm sorry. Let's start fresh." Brief. Not groveling. See `parenting_repair_conversation_after_rupture.md` for the longer version.

### Step 6 — The 90-Second Rule

Biochemically, the chemicals released by an acute emotion flush from the body in about 90 seconds — IF you don't keep refueling them with thought ("He always does this, I can't believe he did that again, I'm so tired, this is so unfair..."). If you can stop feeding the story for 90 seconds, the chemistry drops and your prefrontal cortex comes back online.

Practical move: during the 90 seconds, redirect attention to a physical anchor (feet, breath, cold water, something in your hand). You're not suppressing — you're starving the rumination.

### Step 7 — Chronic Bucket Check

If acute resets aren't landing — if the bucket is empty — the problem isn't this afternoon. It's the capacity underneath. Signs:

- More than 3 flooding events per day for a week.
- Dread going into normal parent moments (pickup, bedtime, mealtime).
- You feel you're performing parenting, not present for it.
- Resentment of your child or partner has moved from occasional to steady.
- You've stopped things you used to love. No hobbies in 6+ months.
- You haven't been alone — genuinely alone — in weeks.
- Sleep <6 hours for weeks. Or sleep 9 hours and still exhausted.

Structural fixes beat technique fixes when the bucket is chronically empty:

- **Sleep:** Not optional. If a child's sleep is the problem, the child's sleep IS your mental health plan.
- **Food:** Low blood sugar looks like rage. Keep snacks where you parent.
- **Breaks:** Put 20 minutes of alone-time on the calendar, not "when I have time." "When I have time" never arrives.
- **Another adult:** Human. Regular. Not a text thread. Even a 15-minute call 2x per week.
- **Professional support:** Therapist, PCP, parent coach, support group. Often covered by insurance. Often not the bar you think it is.
- **Medication review:** If you're postpartum, premenstrual, perimenopausal, or under sustained stress — your biology is a variable. A PCP can check.

### Step 8 — Special Considerations for Neurodivergent-Kid Parents

Parenting an ADHD, autistic, or strong-willed kid is genuinely harder in predictable ways. Naming that is not complaining:

- The **repetition load is higher** (re-teaching the same thing 50 times). That is not failure; that is the job description.
- The **sensory load is higher** (more noise, more touch, more interruption).
- The **social load is higher** (explaining your child to other adults, navigating school, handling judgment).
- The **grief is real and recurring** — grief over what was imagined vs. what is. It doesn't mean you love your child less; it means you're human.
- **Your own neurodivergence** (ADHD, autism often run in families) can flood you faster. Worth knowing about yourself.
- Parent-specific community (ADHD parents, autism parents, etc.) is often more useful than generic parenting content.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Prescribe "self-care" as spa day when parent has 90 seconds and no childcare | Match the reset to the parent's real window (30s/2min/5min) |
| Treat chronic depletion with acute techniques | Name the bucket problem; recommend structural change (sleep, food, adult contact, professional support) |
| Suggest "communicating feelings" to the child by venting | Communicating ≠ venting. The child is not your co-regulator |
| Shame rage as a moral failing | Normalize rage as a nervous-system signature; coach the exit strategy |
| Assume a partner, money, childcare, or flexible work | Offer strategies solo parents can use without outside hands |
| Recommend meditation apps as a universal fix | Some parents find them helpful; others are re-traumatized by stillness — offer, don't prescribe |
| Skip the safety block because "it's just a reset prompt" | Parents in crisis sometimes land here; always surface the escalation path |
| Tell parents of neurodivergent kids they "just need to find joy in it" | The load is objectively higher; acknowledging that is not negativity |
| Guilt the parent into "being more present" | Presence comes from regulation, not effort. Regulate first |
| Recommend venting on social media as coping | Public venting often has costs (child reads it later, judgment, co-parent conflict) |
