# School Accommodation Conversation Prep — For Kids Who Excel Academically but Struggle Behaviorally or Socially

## Objective

Produce a concise, written parent brief (1–2 pages) that a caregiver can carry into a meeting (504, student support team, teacher conference, counselor check-in) for a 4–8 year old who is academically on-level or advanced but whose ADHD, autism, anxiety, sensory, or executive-function profile is producing real impact the school may be minimizing. The brief includes: concern summary, documented pattern evidence, asks tiered by formality (informal → teacher agreement → 504 plan), anticipated objections and responses, and fallback positions so the parent leaves with *something*.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says:

- "The school says she's fine because her grades are fine, but she's crying every morning."
- "They told me he doesn't qualify for a 504 because he's reading above grade level."
- "He holds it together all day and melts down as soon as he gets in the car."
- "She has a diagnosis but the school won't do anything without 'academic impact.'"
- "I have a meeting next week and I don't want to walk in and forget my main points."
- "They keep saying 'he's just immature' or 'she's a perfectionist' — I think something else is going on."
- "He's bright but he can't get started on anything and nobody at school sees it."
- "I want to ask for small changes without it turning into an IEP battle."
- "I've raised this three times in conversation and nothing changes — I need to put it in writing."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The child is in acute distress (refusing school daily, somatic symptoms, suicidal statements) → this needs clinical support in parallel; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- There is suspected abuse or a significant safety issue → different pathway entirely.
- The situation is already in a formal IEP dispute, formal complaint, or due-process stage → this prompt is for first and second-tier advocacy; formal stages need legal/advocate support.
- The parent wants to replace the teacher / enroll in a different school as the primary ask → reframe; that's a different decision.
- The parent is flooded → draft when regulated; see `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before drafting the brief, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Child profile**
   - Age, grade, school type (public / private / charter / homeschool co-op).
   - Diagnoses (or diagnostic process in progress), medication if relevant, therapies (OT, SLP, play therapy, ABA — note: family choice).
   - Academic level (on grade, below, above) and evidence (assessment scores, report card, teacher comments).

2. **The gap the school is missing**
   - What the child shows at school (composite the parent has from teacher, observation, pickup).
   - What the child shows at home (de-masking cascade, meltdowns, school refusal, somatic symptoms, dread, exhaustion).
   - Concrete frequency and duration (e.g., "3–4 meltdowns per week lasting 20–40 min, starting within 15 min of being picked up, for 6 weeks").

3. **Documented pattern evidence the parent has or can get**
   - Emails with the teacher.
   - Therapy / pediatrician notes or letters.
   - Home-log data (1–2 weeks of specific observations).
   - Diagnostic evaluations or screenings.
   - Report card comments / behavior checklists.
   - Samples of child work that show the pattern (unfinished, erased, stalled, excessive time).

4. **What's been tried**
   - Previous conversations, emails, requests.
   - Informal accommodations that worked or didn't.
   - Home-side supports the parent is already running.

5. **The specific friction points at school** (ranked by parent's priority)
   - Transitions, unstructured time, mornings, cafeteria, fire drills, read-aloud time, group projects, independent work starts, test-taking, specials, recess social dynamics.

6. **What the parent wants**
   - Informal agreement (teacher-level strategies).
   - Formal 504 plan.
   - Evaluation for IEP.
   - Just to be heard and put concerns on the record.
   - A specific accommodation (seating, check-ins, break pass, sensory tool, extra time, communication system).

7. **Who will be at the meeting**
   - Teacher, counselor, principal, school psychologist, special educator, district admin, advocate, co-parent, interpreter.

8. **Constraints / red lines**
   - "I don't want him labeled."
   - "I don't want her pulled out of the mainstream classroom."
   - "I don't want to medicate."
   - "I'm willing to go to evaluation but not to a separate classroom."

9. **Cultural, linguistic, family-structure context** that affects how the parent will be received and how they prefer to show up.

10. **Parent's own capacity**
    - Baseline — solo parent? partner supportive? work schedule?
    - Anxiety going in — what would help them stay grounded mid-meeting?

## Constraints

### Must

- **Center impact, not just diagnosis.** A diagnosis alone doesn't entitle a child to accommodations under Section 504 — the parent must be able to articulate a substantial limitation in a major life activity (concentrating, learning, thinking, communicating, interacting with others, sleeping, eating). Help the parent name the impact.
- **Reframe "he's fine because grades are fine."** Academic performance is one major life activity; concentrating, learning, interacting with others, sleeping, and caring for oneself are also major life activities per Section 504. Masking-then-crashing at home is evidence of impact even when grades are preserved.
- **Quantify where possible.** "Crying 20+ minutes at drop-off 4 days last week" beats "she seems unhappy."
- **Tier the asks.** Informal → teacher agreement → 504 plan → evaluation referral. Do not lead with the heaviest ask if a lighter one would work.
- **Anticipate objections.** Common ones: "he's coping fine," "she's just shy," "he's immature," "she's a perfectionist," "we don't accommodate for anxiety," "without academic impact he doesn't qualify." Prepare specific responses.
- **Prepare fallback positions.** If the full ask is denied, what's the partial? What documentation does the parent want in writing regardless of outcome?
- **Put it in writing — give the team something.** The most useful brief is one the parent emails 48 hours before the meeting so everyone reads it.
- **Keep the tone collaborative, not adversarial.** Schools remember parents who are advocates *with* them more than parents who brought a hammer.
- **Respect the parent's red lines without lecturing.** If they said "no medication conversation," don't offer medication framing.
- **Provide a concise "what to say if the meeting goes sideways" phrase bank.**
- **Include the universal safety block.**
- **Note that 504, IDEA, and state-specific procedures vary.** Provide general framing; when decisions get formal, direct to the district's procedural safeguards and/or a parent advocacy resource.

### Must Not

- Must not provide legal advice or guarantee outcomes; this is preparation, not representation.
- Must not draft threats ("we'll go to due process if…") as a first move.
- Must not fabricate evidence, quotes, or clinical credentials. Only include what the parent has confirmed.
- Must not disclose more private medical information than the parent has authorized or than is necessary.
- Must not recommend the parent withhold information that's relevant to safety.
- Must not pathologize the teacher or school; name systemic gaps without blaming individuals.
- Must not encourage the parent to go in alone if they're clearly going to be outnumbered and undersupported — suggest bringing a co-parent, friend, or advocate.
- Must not assume a U.S. context if the parent has not stated one; note when procedures are jurisdiction-specific.

## Instructions

Follow this eight-step structure.

### Step 1 — Map the Gap

Complete, with the parent, a four-quadrant grid:

| | At School (visible) | At Home (visible) |
|---|---|---|
| **Academic** | What teacher sees | What homework/reading shows |
| **Behavioral / Regulatory** | What teacher sees | What parent sees (meltdowns, shutdowns, refusal, somatic) |

The "at home, behavioral" box is the evidence the school often hasn't seen. This is the brief's center of gravity.

### Step 2 — Translate to Impact Language

For each observed pattern, translate to one of the Section 504 "major life activities":

- **Learning** — can access instruction but time-cost / recovery-cost is high.
- **Concentrating** — executive function breakdown despite cognitive ability.
- **Reading / Writing / Communicating** — may be grade-level but with hidden cost or inconsistent access.
- **Interacting with Others** — social exhaustion, isolation, rigid patterns, masking.
- **Sleeping** — school-related anxiety interrupting sleep.
- **Eating** — anxiety-related refusal, school lunch sensory overload.
- **Caring for Oneself** — dysregulation at home preventing self-care tasks.
- **Thinking** — rumination, freeze states.

A child whose grades are fine can still have substantial limitations in several of these. Help the parent articulate that.

### Step 3 — Tier the Asks

Produce three tiers:

1. **Informal asks** (no paperwork; teacher agreement) — specific, small, time-bounded.
   - Examples: "Allow her to eat in the classroom with one friend on overstim days." "Give him a 3-minute pre-transition warning." "Pre-teach unstructured activities." "Check-in at the door each morning." "Quiet corner pass, no-questions." "Stim tools allowed."

2. **Documented agreements** (written informal plan, not formal 504) — parent-teacher-counselor letter of understanding.
   - Examples: "The team agrees to the attached strategies; we'll revisit in 8 weeks."

3. **Formal 504 plan** — procedurally protected accommodations (in U.S. contexts).
   - Examples: preferential seating, extended time on timed tasks, break pass, movement breaks, sensory tools, communication system (home-school notebook), advance warning for schedule changes, fire-drill protocol.

If a full evaluation is needed, tier 3 includes a **request for evaluation in writing** (this starts the formal timeline in many U.S. districts).

### Step 4 — Evidence Packet

For each concern, attach or reference:

- Dated observations from parent (1–2 weeks of log data).
- Teacher-sent communications (emails, notes).
- Outside reports (therapist, pediatrician, evaluator).
- Child work samples showing the pattern.
- Home-side impact indicators (missed sleep, somatic symptoms, social withdrawal).

Bundle as an appendix; keep the main brief short.

### Step 5 — Objection → Response Prep

Anticipate the top 3–5 objections the parent expects. For each, draft a 2–3 sentence response that is specific, non-defensive, and redirects to the evidence.

Common objections and sample responses:

- **"His grades are fine."** → "I appreciate that — his cognitive ability has always been strong, and that's part of why the struggle is hidden. I'm here because of the impact on [specific major life activities: sleeping, interacting with others, concentrating outside of direct instruction]. The academic performance reflects his effort, not the absence of a barrier."
- **"She's just shy."** → "Shyness and social anxiety look similar from the outside, but the pattern at home — [specific: hours of decompression, somatic symptoms on Sunday nights, avoidance behaviors] — is past what shyness typically looks like. Her evaluator saw the same thing."
- **"He's immature, he'll grow out of it."** → "It's possible he will, and I hope so. In the meantime, we have 6 hours of impact every day. Can we put some supports in place so the next 6 months are workable, and revisit the 'grow out of it' question in the spring?"
- **"Without academic impact, he doesn't qualify for 504."** → "My understanding is that 504 covers substantial limitation in any major life activity, not only learning. I've attached documentation of the impact on [concentrating / interacting with others / sleeping]. I'd like the team to evaluate under those criteria."
- **"We don't have the resources for that accommodation."** → "I understand resources are real. Let's start with what is possible — a smaller version, or a different approach to the same problem. What do you have capacity for?"

### Step 6 — Fallback Positions

If the full ask is denied, what's acceptable?

- **Partial accommodation:** "If not a 504, I'd like a documented teacher-level plan."
- **Timeline:** "If not now, revisit in 6 weeks with new data."
- **Evaluation:** "If not accommodations, a written evaluation referral."
- **Put-it-in-writing:** "Whatever the outcome, I'd like the team's decision and the reasoning in writing."

Give the parent explicit language for each fallback so they don't have to invent it under pressure.

### Step 7 — Meeting Tools

Produce:

- **Opening statement** (60 seconds, memorizable) — purpose, one sentence of gratitude for the team's time, the brief's structure, and what the parent wants the meeting to accomplish.
- **Phrase bank for hard moments:**
  - "Can we pause? I want to make sure I understand." (when overwhelmed)
  - "Help me understand the reasoning behind that." (when a decision feels arbitrary)
  - "I want to note that I disagree, and I'd like it in the record." (when the parent is being overruled)
  - "Can we circle back to that? I don't want to lose the thread on [X]." (redirect)
  - "I'd like to take that back and respond in writing." (buy time)
  - "What would have to be true for [the ask] to be possible?" (explore)
- **Grounding plan** — where to sit, what to bring (brief + evidence packet + water + pen), what to do if flooded mid-meeting.
- **Note-taker** — ask a co-parent or friend to take notes so the parent can focus on talking.

### Step 8 — After the Meeting

Produce:

- **Follow-up email template** — sent within 24 hours, summarizing what was agreed, what was declined, and what the parent understands will happen next. This is often the single most important document of the process.
- **Watch-for list** — signs that agreed-to supports are being implemented or not.
- **Revisit rhythm** — when to check in (2 weeks, 6 weeks, end of term).
- **Escalation path, if needed** — counselor → principal → district → formal complaint / state agency / advocacy organization.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Restatement** — 2–3 sentences confirming the situation and what the parent wants from the meeting.
2. **Concern Summary** (1 paragraph) — the gap between school-visible and home-visible, translated into impact language.
3. **Evidence Packet Index** — a short list of what the parent has or needs to gather.
4. **Tiered Asks** — Tier 1 informal / Tier 2 documented / Tier 3 formal, with specific example language.
5. **Anticipated Objections + Responses** — 3–5 pairs.
6. **Fallback Positions** — 3–4 specific fallbacks with language.
7. **Opening Statement** (60 seconds, memorizable).
8. **Phrase Bank for Hard Moments**.
9. **Grounding + Logistics Plan**.
10. **Follow-Up Email Template** (24-hour after-meeting).
11. **Watch-For List** (for after the meeting).
12. **Escalation Path** (if Tier 1–3 fail).
13. **Safety Block** (verbatim).
14. **Cross-References**.

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **What this prompt does and doesn't do:**
> - This is preparation, not legal advice or representation. Special-education and disability-rights procedures are jurisdiction-specific. In the U.S., your district must provide procedural safeguards on request; read them. For formal disputes, contact a Parent Training and Information Center (parentcenterhub.org) or a special-education attorney/advocate.
> - If your child is in emotional crisis, physically unsafe at school, or talking about not wanting to be alive, stop the accommodation process and use clinical and emergency pathways. 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline). Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736). 911 in emergencies.
> - If you suspect abuse or retaliation by school staff, contact your district's child-protection liaison and, as appropriate, Childhelp 1-800-422-4453 or law enforcement.
> - If you are flooded, postpone the meeting by a few days if at all possible. A well-prepared parent from a regulated state is worth ten times an under-prepared parent from a flooded state. See `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.
>
> This prompt is for first and second-tier collaborative advocacy. It is not a substitute for an evaluation, an IEP team, an attorney, or a trained advocate when the situation calls for one.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Leading with diagnosis instead of impact | "She has ADHD, so she needs X" | Lead with impact: "Here is what I'm seeing at home; here is the pattern at school; here is the gap." Diagnosis is context, not the ask. |
| Ignoring the grades-are-fine trap | Parent stuck trying to prove academic impact that isn't there | Translate to non-academic major life activities under Section 504. |
| Starting with the heaviest ask | First meeting asks for a formal 504 with 12 accommodations | Tier; lead with Tier 1 informal unless Tier 3 is clearly needed. |
| Adversarial tone | "I'm prepared to escalate this to the state." | Collaborative first; escalation is a fallback, not a greeting. |
| No evidence | Strong feelings, no data | Ask the parent to collect 1–2 weeks of home-side log data before the meeting. |
| Over-sharing clinical detail | Full evaluation report handed over at meeting | Summarize; attach the report as an appendix if and only if the team asks. |
| Ignoring red lines | Recommending medication conversation the parent already opted out of | Respect red lines. |
| Generic objection responses | "Tell them they're wrong" | Specific, non-defensive responses grounded in the child's data. |
| No fallback | All-or-nothing ask | Always provide a partial; leave with something. |
| Forgetting the follow-up email | Agreements made verbally, not documented | 24-hour follow-up email is often the single most important artifact. |
| Parent going in alone and outnumbered | Parent vs. 5 staff members | Recommend bringing a co-parent, friend, or advocate. |
| Expecting legal knowledge | Assuming parent knows 504 vs. IDEA | Explain briefly; direct to procedural safeguards and advocacy organizations. |
| Treating teachers as adversaries | Brief written like a complaint | Frame as partnership: "I'm bringing you my best read; help me build the right picture." |
| Overwhelming the team | 10-page brief | Keep brief to 1–2 pages; appendix has the rest. |
| Promising outcomes | "This will definitely work" | Name it as preparation; outcomes depend on people and context. |
| Missing the parent's flooding | Drafting while parent is activated | Regulate first; draft next. |
| Mistaking 504 for IEP or vice versa | Asking for the wrong thing | Clarify: 504 = accommodations for disability without specialized instruction; IEP = specialized instruction under IDEA. For a child excelling academically, 504 is often the relevant door. |
| Jurisdiction-blind advice | Advising a U.S. process to a family outside the U.S. | Ask; note limits; point to local equivalents if unknown. |

## Adaptations — Neurodivergent-Specific Framing

### ADHD profile

- Lead with the **executive-function visibility gap**: "He completes work but the cost of starting, sustaining, and transitioning is invisible in his output."
- Tier-1 asks: task-initiation cue, written task checklist, chunked instructions, movement breaks, preferential seating away from high-traffic areas.
- Tier-3 asks (if needed): extended time on timed tasks, break pass, written version of verbal instructions, home-school communication notebook.

### Autism / ASD Level 1 profile

- Lead with the **masking-cascade pattern**: "She holds it together all day by suppressing her needs and regulating behaviors; the cost shows up at pickup." Name this explicitly — many teams don't know what masking looks like at this age.
- Tier-1 asks: advance notice of schedule changes, fire-drill preview, sensory-friendly quiet spot, pass to access a regulation tool, stim-accepting classroom.
- Tier-3 asks: communication system for unstructured times (recess, lunch), social pre-teaching for new situations, consistent staff, written confirmation of verbal changes.
- Neurodiversity-affirming framing: the goal is *access and regulation*, not making the child "look more typical." Flag any accommodation that would require suppressing stims, forcing eye contact, or increasing masking.

### Anxiety profile

- Lead with the **hidden-distress pattern**: "She appears compliant but the internal cost shows up in [somatic symptoms / sleep disruption / avoidance / perfectionism]."
- Tier-1 asks: check-in at arrival, predictable routine, advance warning for performance moments (reading aloud, timed tests), safe-adult designation.
- Tier-3 asks: 504 coverage for anxiety disorder with documented impact on concentrating / interacting / sleeping / eating.

### Twice-exceptional (2e) profile

- This is the central case this prompt addresses: academic strengths masking disability.
- Lead with the **asynchronous development pattern**: "Her reading is two grades ahead and her self-regulation is a year behind. The gap is the disability; the strength is not evidence that the disability doesn't exist."
- Expect the "gifted, not disabled" framing as a core objection and prepare specifically for it.
- Tier-1 asks: enrichment + scaffolding co-existing, accommodation isn't remediation.

### Sensory profile

- Lead with **sensory-load as a major-life-activity impact**: "Fluorescent lights + cafeteria noise + unpredictable touch produces regulation breakdowns by 10am; academic access is compromised even though output looks fine."
- Tier-1 asks: sensory tools allowed, seat away from high-traffic, access to a quieter corner, headphones for assemblies, advance notice of fire drills.
