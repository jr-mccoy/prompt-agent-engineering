**Purpose:** Translate "my ADHD kid won't [start / finish / transition / remember / stop]" into externalized, visual, low-friction scaffolds that work with an ADHD brain instead of against it. The guiding principle: **move executive function out of the child's head and into the environment.** Expect to re-teach more than with a neurotypical peer. That is not failure; that is the job.

**When to use:** ADHD diagnosed, suspected, or neurodivergent pattern present. Any of: task initiation stuck, homework a 45-minute battle, bedtime a 90-minute saga, morning routine impossible, child forgets instructions mid-sentence, emotional reactions bigger than the situation.

**When NOT to use:** In place of a clinical evaluation. Medication is a tool some families pursue with a clinician — out of scope here. This is the home-scaffold layer, not the medication layer.

## Safety Block

Route elsewhere if the input includes:
- Self-injury, suicidal statements, or regression → pediatrician same-day; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- Escalating aggression toward others → pediatrician; mental health evaluation.
- Parent at capacity limit (daily rage, scary thoughts) → see `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`. Parent capacity is part of the ADHD support plan.

## Core Principles (Read Before the Scaffolds)

1. **Externalize memory.** ADHD brains under-store; scaffolds over-display. Visuals, lists, timers, labels on the outside world do the work the child's working memory can't yet.
2. **Time is invisible to an ADHD kid.** "5 more minutes" means nothing. Timers with visible countdowns (sand timers, Time Timer, visual phone timer) make time concrete.
3. **Interest fuels function.** An ADHD child can hyperfocus on Minecraft for 2 hours and genuinely not be able to put on socks. Both are real. Use interest as fuel when you can; shorten the demand when you can't.
4. **Consequences are a weak lever; scaffolds are a strong lever.** Stacking bigger punishments on an ADHD kid rarely changes behavior and often tanks the relationship. Changing the environment does.
5. **Shame is the enemy.** ADHD kids hear "try harder" all day. The scaffold removes the need to try harder — it makes the right thing the easy thing.
6. **Re-teaching is the norm.** Expect 5–10x the repetitions of a neurotypical peer. Not doing "this again?" energy; doing "here's the visual we use" energy.
7. **Your regulation is the foundation.** A flooded parent cannot scaffold. See `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Target one specific executive function at a time (task initiation, working memory, time awareness, transitions, emotional regulation).
- Every scaffold is externalized: visual, physical, or tactile — not verbal-only.
- Use the child's interests as currency where possible.
- Build in re-teaching assumptions (pre-mortem the forgetting).
- Provide both warm-collaborative and firm-respectful register scripts.
- Specify low-lift, medium, and optional-higher-investment layers.
- Acknowledge the parent load — ADHD parenting is genuinely heavier.

**Must Not:**
- Prescribe "just tell them to focus" or any cognitive-will-based solution.
- Over-index on consequences as the main strategy.
- Shame the child or the parent for forgetfulness, distraction, or big emotions.
- Assume a partner, stay-at-home parent, or dedicated space.
- Promise a scaffold will work first try. Many need iteration.
- Treat ADHD as an attention deficit only — emotional dysregulation and time blindness are equally core.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Map the Executive Function Gap

Pick the one that's most interfering right now. Don't try to solve five at once.

| Function | What It Looks Like at 4-8 | Typical Gap in ADHD |
|----------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| **Task initiation** | Starting a non-preferred task without reminders | 10–30 minutes of inability to start, even wanting to |
| **Working memory** | Holding a 2–3-step instruction in mind | Loses step 2 while doing step 1 |
| **Time awareness** | "We leave in 10 minutes" means something | "10 minutes" is the same as "5" or "30" |
| **Transitions** | Shifting from preferred to non-preferred activity | Big emotional reactions; feels like an interruption of something critical |
| **Emotional regulation** | Recovering from disappointment without derailing | Bigger, faster, longer emotional reactions; recovery tail is long |
| **Planning / sequencing** | Knowing that shoes go on before going outside | Gets stuck mid-sequence; forgets order |
| **Inhibition** | Not grabbing, interrupting, running into the street | Impulse-to-action is fast; the filter is underpowered |

### Step 2 — Build the Scaffold Stack

For the chosen gap, stack three layers. All three together, not one at a time.

#### A. Environment (the physical setup)

- **Task initiation:** Put the first step physically in the way. Shoes at the door, toothbrush pre-pasted on the counter, homework folder open on the table, seat pulled out.
- **Working memory:** 3–5-picture checklist at the height of the child's eye. Photos work better than words for 4–6; words + pictures for 6–8.
- **Time awareness:** Visible countdown (Time Timer disc, sand timer, phone timer on display). Countdown at transitions, not demands ("10 min until we leave" ≠ "come now").
- **Transitions:** Visual schedule in the same spot every day. First/then boards ("First homework, then Minecraft"). Physical transition objects (a specific hat that means "we're leaving soon").
- **Emotional regulation:** Designated calm-down spot that is NOT a punishment zone. Fidget / chewy / weighted stuffy available. Visual emotion chart at kid-height.
- **Planning / sequencing:** Sequence photo strips of morning / bedtime / homework routines.
- **Inhibition:** Reduce available impulsivity targets. Fewer things to grab. Locks on dangerous cabinets. Screens out of the bedroom.

#### B. Body & Sensory (the physical input the brain needs)

ADHD brains are often under-stimulated for non-preferred tasks. Adding the right sensory input helps the brain engage.

- Movement before demand: 2-minute dance party, wall push-ups, run around the yard, jump 20x.
- Chewy input for focus: crunchy snack, chewy necklace, sugar-free gum (for 6+).
- Proprioceptive input: weighted lap pad, bear hug before homework, couch jumping before bedtime.
- Fidgets that don't distract: putty, a specific-task fidget, doodle-on-margin paper.
- Consider whether your kid is a *mover* or a *stiller* under demand. Neither is wrong. Fit the scaffold.

#### C. Verbal (the language you use, short)

- **Shorten the demand.** "Shoes, please." Not: "It's time to get ready to go so we need to put our shoes on and grab your backpack and make sure you have your lunch."
- **One instruction at a time.** Working memory holds ~1 in this age band under load.
- **State the thing, not the stop.** "Feet on the floor" beats "stop jumping."
- **Pair with a visual.** Point at the checklist instead of listing the steps.
- **Slow down, drop volume.** Faster-louder parent = faster-louder kid.
- **Use interest as the hook.** "Minecraft brain on: we need to build-mode the morning. Step 1."
- **Name the scaffold, not the child's "failure":** "Let's check the chart" > "You forgot again."

### Step 3 — Pattern Scaffolds by Common Pain Point

#### Task Initiation ("He won't start")

- **Low-lift:** Move the first step into the environment (shoes at the door, page already on the screen).
- **Medium:** Body-double. Sit nearby, do your own version of the task, don't nag. Your presence is regulating.
- **Medium:** Start-together cue: "I'll do one, you do one." Many ADHD kids can't start alone but can start alongside.
- **Optional:** "Body doubling partner" — a specific sibling or stuffed animal assigned to start with.
- **Script (warm-collaborative):** "Starting is the hard part. Let's do one together. I'll sit right here."
- **Script (firm-respectful):** "Start with step one. I'm right here. Go."

#### Working Memory ("I told her three times!")

- **Low-lift:** Give instructions at the visual support, not from across the room.
- **Medium:** Chunk to one step. "Teeth." Wait. Check. "Shoes." Wait. Check.
- **Medium:** Re-teach expectation: you're going to re-give instructions many times. That's the scaffold, not a failure.
- **Optional:** Photo schedule card the child carries / clips onto themselves in the morning.
- **Script:** "Point to step two on the chart. What's next?" (They find it; you confirm.)

#### Time Awareness ("5 more minutes" doesn't land)

- **Low-lift:** Use a Time Timer or sand timer, visible from where the child is.
- **Medium:** Pair time with activity: "Timer + one more Minecraft round, then dinner."
- **Medium:** Multiple warnings: 10 min, 5 min, 1 min, now. Not because they're not listening — because their time sense doesn't work.
- **Optional:** Make time competitive. "Can you beat the timer?" (works for interest-wired kids).
- **Script:** "The red disc is almost gone. When it's gone, we're on to dinner."

#### Transitions ("She loses it every time")

- See `parenting_transitions_and_warnings_protocol.md` for a full version.
- **Low-lift:** Preview. "In 10 minutes we're leaving the park. I'll tell you again at 5 and at 1."
- **Medium:** First/then board, physical if the kid is 4–6: "First shoes. Then car. Then snack."
- **Optional:** A transition object that signals "moving modes" — a hat, a specific song, a handshake.
- **Script:** "It's hard to stop. I see you. One more [thing]. Then it's time."

#### Emotional Regulation ("She goes 0 to 100")

- See `parenting_meltdown_response_script.md` for the in-moment version.
- **Low-lift:** Name the feeling neutrally. "Mad body. Got it." Don't argue the feeling.
- **Medium:** Designated reset spot with sensory tools (chewy, weighted stuffy, fidget). Not a punishment zone.
- **Medium:** Pre-identify the pattern: "At 4:30, brains get tired. Let's have snack and movement before homework."
- **Optional:** Teach the "traffic light" or "volcano" language for their own feelings, when they're calm — not mid-meltdown.

### Step 4 — Design the One-Week Experiment

Don't roll out five scaffolds. Pick one, run it for a week, check in.

- **Target behavior:** [the specific thing]
- **Scaffold:** [environment + body + verbal — named]
- **How you'll measure:** [frequency, parent stress, child tears — pick something]
- **Re-teaching plan:** [expect to repeat the intro 5–10 times the first week]
- **1-week check-in:** [what "it's working" looks like vs. "needs a tweak"]

### Step 5 — Parent Capacity Check

Running scaffolds IS the strategy. Scaffolds require a regulated parent who has bandwidth. If you don't, the scaffold won't hold. Honest check:

- Who else can help run the scaffold (co-parent, grandparent, babysitter)?
- What scaffold can you *drop* to make room for this one?
- Is your sleep/food/alone-time baseline workable? If not, see `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.

### Step 6 — Common Pitfalls

- **Scaffold overload.** Too many systems. Pick one.
- **Scaffold abandoned at 3 days.** Most scaffolds need 2+ weeks.
- **Scaffold turned into punishment.** "You didn't follow the chart, so no iPad." The chart is a tool, not a rule to break.
- **Scaffold for show.** A visible chart no one uses is clutter. Point to it, use it, refer to it.
- **Removing scaffolds too fast when it's working.** ADHD scaffolds are not temporary training wheels to phase out at age 8. Many are lifelong aids.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Tell an ADHD kid to "just focus" / "try harder" / "pay attention" | Change the environment so the right thing is the easy thing |
| Stack bigger consequences when scaffolds don't land | Scaffolds beat consequences for EF gaps. Tweak the scaffold, don't escalate the consequence |
| Install 5 scaffolds at once | One at a time. Let one land before adding the next |
| Treat a visible chart as evidence of success | Chart isn't the scaffold; using the chart is |
| Expect scaffolds to work first try | 2+ weeks of consistent use is the minimum; re-teaching is the norm |
| Phase out scaffolds as soon as they're working | Working = using. Removing tools removes the scaffold itself |
| Lecture mid-dysregulation | Scripts are short; lessons happen when calm (see parenting_meltdown_response_script.md) |
| Use the child's hyperfocus interests as a bribe and then yank them | Interests as fuel, not hostage |
| Frame ADHD as willpower failure | Frame it as a brain with different EF defaults — not better or worse, different |
| Blame the parent for the child's EF gap | EF gaps are neurological; parenting shapes outcomes but did not cause ADHD |
| Ignore the parent's own probable ADHD | ADHD runs in families; the parent's own EF may need scaffolds too |
| Treat medication as a failure OR a requirement | Neutral stance; that's a clinician conversation |
