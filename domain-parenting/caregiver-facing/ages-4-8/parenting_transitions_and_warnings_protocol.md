# Transitions and Warnings Protocol

## Objective

Produce a specific, repeatable transition protocol for a 4–8 year old's recurring transition friction — screen-off, leaving the park, stopping play for meals, leaving the house, coming in from outside, ending a playdate, waking up, starting homework — using (a) timed advance warnings, (b) previews, (c) first/then framing, (d) sensory/body bridges, and (e) state-matched scripts, with adaptations by child profile.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says:

- "Every time we leave [place], it's a meltdown."
- "Screen-off is a fight every single day."
- "He can't stop playing even when we've told him three times."
- "She's so upset when the playdate ends."
- "Mornings are fine until it's time to put on shoes."
- "She gets stuck on something and can't pivot."
- "Dinner is fine but coming to the table is impossible."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The transition refusal is a specific safety situation (child refuses car seat, running into street) → also route to `parenting_behavior_function_decoder.md` and consider immediate safety supports.
- The transition meltdown pattern emerged suddenly and is part of larger regression → consider medical / stress cause; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- The problem is actually a sensory problem at the new location (loud, bright, crowded) → route to `parenting_sensory_at_home_toolkit.md`.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before producing the protocol, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Child profile**
   - Age.
   - Neurodivergence (ADHD, autism Level 1, anxiety, sensory sensitivity).
   - Hyperfocus tendency: does the child "lock in" to activities and genuinely not hear?
   - Communication style: verbal, scripted, needs visual.

2. **The specific transition**
   - What are they leaving, and going to what?
   - Time of day; hunger/sleep state.
   - Parent emotional state at the transition (often rushing, often regulated, often flooded).

3. **Current approach**
   - How does the parent signal the transition now?
   - What words?
   - What's the typical escalation path?

4. **Environment**
   - Can parent use a timer? Visual?
   - Who else is present (siblings, other parents, peers)?
   - Is the transition public (playground departures have audience pressure)?

5. **Non-negotiable deadline**
   - Is the transition at a fixed time (school, work, appointment) or flexible?
   - This determines how much flex the protocol can have.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Use multiple warnings.** Most 4–8 yr olds need 2–3 spaced warnings, not one.
- **Include a preview of what comes next.** Transitions are easier when the child can see the "to," not just leave the "from." "After we leave the park, we're going home to eat the pasta you picked out."
- **Use visual or sensory time markers, not just verbal.** Visual timers, songs, countdown fingers.
- **First/then framing for demands.** "First shoes, then snack" is easier than "we're leaving now."
- **Connection before demand.** 30 seconds of joining the child's play before announcing the transition softens resistance.
- **Match the child's state.** Hyper-aroused child needs body-first regulation; hypo-aroused child needs activation.
- **Age-tier.** 4–5 can hold 2 warnings; 6–7 can hold 3; 8 can plan with a clock.
- **Include a specific script** for the exact transition the parent named.
- **Include "what the parent does NOT do."**
- **Include adaptations by profile.**
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not use countdown-as-threat ("5, 4, 3, 2, 1 or else…"). Countdowns are information, not threats.
- Must not require the child to stop mid-sentence, mid-swing, mid-turn in a game. Build in natural pause points.
- Must not bribe with a reward every transition; the routine itself becomes the reward.
- Must not yell from across the room ("IT'S TIME TO GO!" from the kitchen does not work). Physical proximity matters.
- Must not shame slow transitions.
- Must not design protocols that assume an audience-free environment.

## Instructions

Follow this six-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Classify the Transition Type

Which type?

- **High-interest → low-interest** (leaving play to eat dinner, screen-off to bedtime) — hardest.
- **Low-interest → high-interest** (finishing homework to play, school pickup) — easiest, but can still stick.
- **Known → unknown** (going somewhere new, new caregiver).
- **Public → private** (leaving a party or playdate) — audience adds pressure.
- **Uncomfortable start** (cold out, tired, hungry).

Different types need different protocols.

### Step 2 — Check State and Capacity

Before planning warnings, check:
- Is the child hyperfocused (genuinely won't hear at first call)? Needs proximity + eye-level attention.
- Is the child hungry / tired / overstimulated? Fix that first when possible.
- Is the parent rushing? A rushing parent's transitions always go worse.

### Step 3 — The Three-Warning Protocol (core structure)

Default pattern:

**10-minute warning (or "two more episodes, two more slides, two more scoops"):**
> "Hey, in 10 minutes we're going to head home. When the timer goes, we'll put the shovel in the bag together."

Physical proximity. Eye-level. Pause. Wait for acknowledgment (look, nod, even a grunt). Do not walk away yet.

**5-minute warning:**
> "Five more minutes. Last two trips down the slide."

Narrow the choice set. One more activity of that type, then we transition.

**1-minute / "now" warning:**
> "Last one. Coming down the slide — then shoes on, then to the car."

Preview the full next sequence as they move through it.

Adjust numbers for: younger child (shorter warnings: 5-3-1), older child (longer: 15-10-5), hyperfocus profile (add body contact at each warning), autistic child (consistent wording verbatim each time).

### Step 4 — Build the Specific Script

For the specific transition the parent named, produce:

1. **Connection opener** (30 sec): Join the play briefly. "That's a cool tower — what's that blue piece?"
2. **10-min warning** (with what comes next named).
3. **5-min warning** (narrowed).
4. **1-min warning** (preview the next step in the sequence).
5. **Go moment** (brief, clear, action-first).
6. **Transition bridge activity** (something small between leaving and the next thing — buckle up song, walk-counting game, silly voice).
7. **Post-transition acknowledgment** ("That was hard — you stopped when it was hard to stop. Thanks.").

### Step 5 — First/Then, Visual, and Sensory Tools

- **First/then statement:** "First [demand], then [desired]." Works best when "then" is a real, already-planned next activity, not a bribe.
- **Visual timer:** Time Timer, sand timer, phone countdown shown to child. External time is better than felt time for most kids.
- **Sensory bridge:** carry a fidget out of the park; put on a coat with a particular snap; sip water on the walk to the car.
- **Transition object:** same stuffed animal rides in the car; same song plays on pickup.
- **Physical cues:** hand-on-shoulder means "1-minute warning"; walking toward the door means "now."

### Step 6 — Adaptations

**ADHD**
- Proximity + eye-contact (or eye-level presence) at every warning.
- External time visual — a countdown the child can see.
- Offer a movement bridge (hopping to the car, animal walk).
- Short transition windows: long warning + long transition loses attention. 10-5-1 becomes 5-3-1 for some kids.
- Post-transition acknowledgment matters even more — ADHD kids are often praised for finishing, rarely for transitioning.
- See `parenting_adhd_executive_function_scaffold.md`.

**Autism / ASD Level 1**
- Consistent wording verbatim. Use same phrase each time ("When the song ends, we go").
- Visual schedule of the sequence: play → snack → car → home.
- Warn of any deviation from expected routine in advance.
- Allow finishing-a-thought: "Finish this Lego section, then we go."
- Sensory load of the new environment matters (car ride noisy? house smell strong?) — preview.
- See `parenting_hfa_social_situation_decoder.md`.

**Strong-willed**
- Choice inside the transition: "Do you want to put your shoes on by the door or carry them to the car?"
- Avoid direct contest ("I said we're going"); let the protocol be the limit.
- Acknowledge autonomy in the after-note: "You stopped when it was hard to stop. That's hard."
- See `parenting_strong_willed_power_struggle_defuser.md`.

**Sensory-sensitive**
- Transition bridges that reduce sensory load (headphones on in loud places; sunglasses outdoors).
- Fewer words at transitions (verbal load adds to load).
- Coats / shoes / car seat checks for tags, seams, temperature.
- See `parenting_sensory_at_home_toolkit.md`.

**Hyperfocused child (any profile)**
- Physical proximity is required — must get into the child's visual field.
- Warning may need to be repeated with eye contact or shoulder contact.
- Allow a closing beat — "save your place / take a picture / finish this one."
- Avoid flipping the attention instantly; bridge through the body first.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Transition Type Classification** (high→low, low→high, known→unknown, public→private)
2. **State Check** (child + parent capacity notes)
3. **Three-Warning Script** (10-min / 5-min / 1-min, customized to this transition)
4. **Connection Opener and Transition Bridge** (activities, not just words)
5. **Visual / Sensory Tools** (timer recommendation, transition object, physical cues)
6. **What the Parent Does NOT Do** (3–5 explicit items)
7. **Adaptations** (by child profile — only those that apply)
8. **One-Week Trial Plan** (use the protocol on this specific transition for 7 days; track friction count)
9. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
10. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If a transition routinely escalates to the child hurting themselves or others, destroying property, or running into unsafe environments → this is beyond the transition protocol and may need `parenting_behavior_function_decoder.md`, `parenting_meltdown_response_script.md`, and pediatric consultation. See `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
> - If transition difficulty is part of a broader regression pattern (new from previous baseline) → consult pediatrician to rule out medical, sleep, or stress-related causes.
> - If a parent cannot leave the house due to a child's transition refusal and it's affecting work, appointments, or care for siblings → this is occupational impairment-level; talk to pediatrician about a formal support evaluation.
> - If you are losing patience in ways that scare you at every transition → Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736).
> - Medical emergency: 911.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| One warning, then demand | "Two minutes!" then "let's go!" | 3 warnings with previews, not one. |
| Countdown as threat | "5-4-3-2-1 or no screens tomorrow" | Countdowns are information, not punishment. |
| Yelling from another room | No proximity | Go to the child, eye-level, physical presence. |
| Expecting the warning to land at first warning | Parent gives up after first ignored warning | First warning often doesn't land; that's expected. Second and third are not "reminders," they're part of the protocol. |
| Bribing every transition | Sticker, candy, phone time for every transition | Reward every transition and the transition itself has no meaning. |
| Interrupting mid-activity | Demanding stop in middle of Lego build | Allow a closing beat. |
| Ignoring hyperfocus | Speaking to a hyperfocused child from 10 feet away | Must be physically present, often touch shoulder, wait for acknowledgment. |
| Shaming slow transitions | "Why are you always so slow" | Transitions are hard; shame makes next one harder. |
| Forgetting the preview | Announcing departure without naming destination | Brain-level: "from X to unknown" is harder than "from X to Y." |
| Changing the protocol day by day | Inconsistency trains the child to wait out | Protocol is the same; warnings can vary in time. |
| Treating a sensory problem as a transition problem | Loud restaurant; child won't leave play area → "he just can't transition" | Route to sensory. |
| Public transition with audience pressure | Parent performs for other parents; kid resists harder | Reduce audience pressure with physical privacy, lower voice, move away from crowd. |
| Over-reliance on words | Verbal-only protocol for autistic or high-sensory-load child | Visual, physical, body cues. |
| No post-transition acknowledgment | Skipping the "that was hard, you did it" | Kids who transitioned through friction deserve acknowledgment. |
| Not adjusting for state | Same protocol at 8am rested vs. 5pm tired | Shorter warnings, more proximity when child is low-capacity. |
| Punishing the transition that didn't work | "No screens tomorrow because of today" | Connect the consequence only to the immediate behavior, not a next-day escalation. |
