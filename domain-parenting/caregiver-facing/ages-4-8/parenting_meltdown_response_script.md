**Purpose:** Give a parent a concrete, phase-by-phase script for handling a meltdown — what to say, what not to say, what your body should be doing, and what to do in the 30 minutes after. Meltdowns in 4- to 8-year-olds are a nervous-system event, not a manipulation. The goal is co-regulation, not compliance.

**When to use:** Recurring meltdowns (screaming, crashing, hitting, hiding, going boneless). Before or during a predictable flashpoint (pickup, bedtime, store checkout, homework start, screen transition). After a meltdown, for the repair step.

**When NOT to use:** A child who is injuring themselves or someone else beyond momentary flailing → jump to Phase 3 crisis response and see Safety Block. A child who is dissociating (blank eyes, unresponsive, limp) for more than a few minutes → pediatrician same-day.

---

## Safety Block

Stop the script and act now if:
- The child is hurting themselves in a targeted way (hitting head on hard surface repeatedly, biting self, swallowing things) → ensure safety, do not leave them alone, call pediatrician today.
- The child is causing real injury to another person (drawing blood, choking) → separate bodies safely, call for help if needed.
- The child is talking about wanting to die, disappear, or not exist — in any words — during the meltdown → take it seriously. Call the pediatrician same-day. In the US, 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
- You feel you might lose control and hurt your child → put the child somewhere physically safe (crib, locked gate, another adult), walk away, call a warmline (1-855-4APARENT in the US). Nothing about your child's meltdown is worth your losing control.

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles (Read Before the Script)

1. **Your regulation regulates them.** A dysregulated child cannot be reasoned, shamed, or bargained out of a meltdown. You are the outboard prefrontal cortex.
2. **A meltdown is not misbehavior.** Misbehavior has intent. A meltdown is an overflow. You don't punish a child for vomiting; you don't punish them for a meltdown either.
3. **Connect first, then redirect.** Explain, teach, and consequence AFTER the nervous system is back online — usually 20–60 minutes after the meltdown ends.
4. **Shorter is better.** In the peak, say less. Big sentences hit an offline brain as noise.
5. **The audience amplifies.** Siblings, in-laws, strangers in the grocery store — the child can feel them. Reduce the audience or give the child privacy.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Provide a phased script: pre-signals, peak, descent, recovery, repair.
- Give the parent exact words in both a warm-collaborative and firm-respectful register.
- Include what the parent's body should be doing (posture, voice, distance), not just words.
- Offer a neurodivergence / temperament adaptation that matches the input.
- Tell the parent what to do with siblings, bystanders, and co-parents during the event.
- Name what NOT to say, not just what to say.
- End with a repair step the parent can run later, not just survival.

**Must Not:**
- Prescribe a time-out as the primary intervention. Time-outs for a dysregulated child increase distress.
- Recommend "letting it burn out" with zero connection — that's extinction of a nervous-system event, not a strategy.
- Suggest rewards or consequences mid-meltdown. The child cannot cognitively access them.
- Shame the parent. Parenting a kid through a meltdown in public is hard.
- Use "manipulation" language for a 4- to 8-year-old meltdown. Manipulation requires theory-of-mind sophistication this age group mostly doesn't have at full dysregulation.
- Prescribe physical restraint. Holding is a last-resort safety move, not a strategy.

## Instructions

Produce a script organized by the five phases below. Each phase gets: parent body, parent words (two register variants), what to avoid, and audience handling.

### Phase 1 — Pre-Meltdown Signals (Last 30–120 seconds of warning)

Early signs vary by kid. Common ones: voice getting louder or higher, repetitive "no-no-no", stomping, stalling, fidgeting, eye contact dropping, sudden silence, a flush in the cheeks.

- **Parent body:** Slow down. Get low (physically, to eye level). Soften your face. One hand free, one hand neutral.
- **Parent words (warm-collaborative):** "I see your body is getting big. Let's slow down together."
- **Parent words (firm-respectful):** "Pause. I can see this is hard. We're going to take a breath."
- **What to avoid:** Lecturing, adding a consequence ("or else"), rushing, laughing.
- **Goal:** Catch the train before it leaves the station. Most meltdowns have a 30–120 second window where co-regulation still lands.

### Phase 2 — Peak (The Meltdown Itself)

Screaming, crashing, hitting, hiding. This phase often lasts 2–20 minutes. Your job is to be a steady presence, not to fix.

- **Parent body:** Still. Lowered posture. Slow breath — the child's nervous system will track yours. Arm's length or closer if they want contact; more distance if touch escalates them. Hands visible, not hovering.
- **Parent words (warm-collaborative), shortest form:** "I'm here." / "You're safe." / "I won't leave." / "Breathe with me."
- **Parent words (firm-respectful), shortest form:** "I'm here. You're safe." / "I'm right here. I won't move." / "Mouth closed, body safe, I'm right here."
- **What to avoid:**
  - Long sentences. "Why are you acting like this, I told you three times already..." is white noise.
  - Questions. "What do you need?" cannot be answered by an offline brain.
  - Threats. "If you don't stop, I'll take away..." — they cannot self-regulate TO the threat, so you'll have to follow through on an escalation.
  - Matching their volume. You are the thermostat.
  - Physical restraint unless there is an active safety risk and you've been trained.
- **If they hit, kick, or throw:** Move yourself out of range. Say "I won't let you hit. My body is safe." Move objects they could throw. Do not hit back, grab hard, or yell.
- **Audience handling:**
  - **Siblings:** Send them out of the room with an activity. "Your sister is having big feelings. I'm going to help her. You can [Lego / screens / snack at the counter]."
  - **Strangers in public:** You owe them nothing. A brief "We're having a hard moment, we're okay" is enough. Relocate if you can.
  - **Co-parent:** Decide in advance — one parent takes the lead, the other handles siblings and logistics. Don't both crowd the child.
  - **In-laws / grandparents:** "I've got this. Thank you." and stay the course. Mixed messages from three adults prolongs it.

### Phase 3 — Descent (The Child Starts Coming Back Online)

Signs: breathing slowing, body softening, reaching for you, making eye contact, tears replacing screams, asking for water.

- **Parent body:** Open arms without pulling. Let them come to you.
- **Parent words (warm-collaborative):** "Your body is slowing down. I'm right here. Take your time."
- **Parent words (firm-respectful):** "You're coming back. I'm right here. Breathe."
- **Offer (not demand):** Water. A weighted blanket, a stuffy, a favorite small object. Dimmer light. A quiet corner.
- **What to avoid:** Launching into "We need to talk about what just happened." You don't. Not yet.

### Phase 4 — Recovery (Next 10–30 minutes)

The child is back but fragile. Their nervous system just ran a marathon. Expect tiredness, hunger, clinginess, or a second wave.

- **Parent body:** Be nearby. Low-key activity — coloring, a snack, a quiet show, a walk, a bath.
- **Parent words:** "That was hard. You're okay now. I love you." And then — surprisingly — not much more.
- **What to avoid:**
  - Praising them for "finally calming down" (implies they did it on purpose earlier).
  - Asking them to explain what happened (they often can't).
  - Sneaking in a lesson ("See, that's why we can't...").
  - Putting them right back into the trigger environment.
- **Offer a snack + hydration.** Dysregulation is metabolically expensive. Low blood sugar is a second-wave trigger.

### Phase 5 — Repair (Later That Day or Next Morning)

Do this when the child is fully regulated — often bedtime, bathtime, or a car ride. See `parenting_repair_conversation_after_rupture.md` for the full version.

- **Opening (warm-collaborative):** "Earlier today your body had really big feelings. I want to understand. Can you help me?"
- **Opening (firm-respectful):** "Let's talk about what happened at [pickup / store / homework]. I want to hear what was going on for you."
- **Script the exchange:**
  1. Listen. Reflect back: "You felt [feeling] because [trigger]."
  2. Validate, don't co-sign: "That makes sense that you felt that way. Hitting isn't okay, and I know you weren't trying to hurt me. We're going to practice what to do next time."
  3. Co-design one small replacement: "Next time your body starts getting big, what's a sign you can give me? We could do a code word, a hand squeeze, or point to the couch."
  4. If you lost your cool: apologize cleanly, once. "I raised my voice, and I shouldn't have. That was my mistake."
- **What to avoid:**
  - Making the repair conversation a lecture.
  - Repair-as-consequence ("Now you have to write an apology note"). Repair is connection, not punishment.
  - Over-processing. One short conversation. Move on.

## Neurodivergence & Temperament Adaptations

Pick whichever applies:

- **ADHD:** Expect bigger, faster, more frequent meltdowns around transitions and task demands. Pre-teach transitions with 5- and 2-minute warnings. The recovery tail is often longer — plan buffer time.
- **Autism / ASD Level 1:** Distinguish a meltdown (nervous-system overflow) from a tantrum (goal-directed). Meltdowns often follow sensory or social overload, not denied wants. Do less talking during peak. Respect a child who wants space and silence — that is regulation, not rejection. Visual supports ("first calm, then cookie") often land better than verbal.
- **Strong-willed / high-autonomy:** Audiences make it worse. Move to a private space fast. Avoid ultimatums during peak — save the boundary for repair. Offer face-saving exits.
- **Sensory-seeking:** Firm proprioceptive input (bear hug if welcomed, weighted blanket, wall push-ups once descending) often shortens recovery. See `parenting_sensory_at_home_toolkit.md`.
- **Sensory-avoiding:** Reduce input. Dim lights, lower voices, no touch unless invited. A quiet tent or closet with a pillow is often the fastest path back.
- **Anxious profile:** A boring, predictable adult response is the intervention. Reassurance loops ("You're sure I'm not in trouble?") need a calm, short "You're not in trouble. I love you. We're okay."

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---------|-------|
| Treat a meltdown as manipulation ("she's just doing this for attention") | Treat it as nervous-system overflow; attention-seeking is a function, not a moral flaw |
| Give a long lecture mid-meltdown | Shortest possible sentences; save the teaching for repair |
| Threaten consequences during peak ("I'm going to take away...") | Set the boundary calmly ("I won't let you hit"), address consequences after recovery |
| Send a dysregulated 5-year-old to their room alone for 10 minutes | Stay nearby; co-regulation requires presence, not isolation |
| Try to reason, bargain, or explain during Phase 2 | The prefrontal cortex is offline. Use anchoring phrases, not logic |
| Match or exceed the child's volume to "show them who's boss" | Lower your voice; you are the thermostat |
| Restrain a child physically unless there's an active safety risk and you've been trained | Move yourself, move objects, keep distance; call for help if needed |
| Force eye contact or demand "look at me when I'm talking" during peak | Eye contact overwhelms a dysregulated brain; accept averted gaze |
| Pretend it didn't happen afterward | Run a short repair later; model that big feelings and reconnection coexist |
| Apologize-and-grovel if you lost your cool | Brief, clean apology, once. Then move on. Over-apologizing scares a 6-year-old |
| Confuse an autistic meltdown with a neurotypical tantrum | An ASD meltdown is often sensory/cognitive overload — less talk, less touch, more time |
| Assume a strong-willed child "just needs firmer consequences" | Escalating consequences in public guarantees escalation; reduce audience, save boundary for repair |
