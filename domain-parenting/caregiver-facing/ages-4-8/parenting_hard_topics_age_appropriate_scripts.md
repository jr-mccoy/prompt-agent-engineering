# Hard Topics — Age-Appropriate Scripts for 4–8 Year Olds

## Objective

Generate a specific parent script — age-tiered, honest, concrete, and concise — for a hard conversation with a 4–8 year old. Covered topics include (a) death of a person or pet, (b) divorce / separation / blended families, (c) bodies, privacy, and consent basics, (d) big feelings (fear, jealousy, shame, embarrassment, loneliness), (e) scary news (violence, accidents, disasters, pandemic) at the child's developmental level, (f) a family member's serious illness or mental-health condition, or (g) the child's own recent diagnosis (ADHD, autism, anxiety, a medical condition).

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says any of the following:

- "Grandma died / our dog died and I don't know what to say."
- "We're getting divorced and we haven't told the kids."
- "My child saw something on the news and I don't know how to explain it."
- "My child is asking about bodies / where babies come from / why people look different."
- "Someone touched them in a way that worried them" (safety script).
- "My partner has cancer and we need to tell the kids."
- "My child was just diagnosed with ADHD / autism / anxiety and we need to tell them."
- "My child has big shame / jealousy / fear and I don't know how to name it with them."
- "There was a school shooting / community violence / scary event and my child is asking."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The topic involves a child disclosing abuse → the safety conversation is necessary but insufficient; report to Childhelp 1-800-422-4453 or local child-protection authorities and seek a child advocacy center; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- The parent is in acute grief themselves and cannot speak without breaking down → the conversation may still need to happen, but with support; consider a grief-trained therapist or trusted co-parent to co-deliver.
- The topic is sexual abuse education beyond general body-safety → partner with a trained child safety program.
- The child has a mental-health crisis response to the topic already (sustained regression, statements of not wanting to live, refusal to eat) → professional help alongside or before this script.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before producing the script, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Child snapshot**
   - Age (year and month).
   - Verbal / communication style.
   - Any neurodivergence (autism, ADHD, anxiety) — changes word choice.
   - How the child has responded to hard information in the past (clingy, shut-down, full of questions, quiet then lots of questions days later).

2. **The topic — exactly which one**
   - If death: who or what, how, when, what the child has already noticed.
   - If divorce / separation: timeline, living arrangement, what is already decided, what is not.
   - If diagnosis: what diagnosis, who has it (parent, sibling, child themselves), prognosis if known.
   - If body-safety: specific concern or general primer; whether an incident has occurred.
   - If big feelings: which feeling; what triggered the need for the conversation.
   - If scary news: what the child has seen or heard; from where.

3. **What the child already knows / has asked**
   - What questions has the child asked?
   - What language has the family used so far?
   - What has the child heard from other adults, siblings, TV, online?

4. **Parent's capacity right now**
   - Can the parent have this conversation without flooding?
   - If not, co-parent or trusted adult co-deliver option?
   - Is a trial-run script needed before going live?

5. **Family context**
   - Faith or spiritual tradition (affects how death, illness, and some body-topics are framed).
   - Cultural norms around discussing emotion, body, mortality.
   - Language (if bilingual household, which language is this conversation happening in?).

6. **Timing**
   - Is the conversation reactive (child just asked) or planned (parent initiating)?
   - What time of day — avoid bedtime for initiation of hard news unless unavoidable.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Age-tier every script.** Produce three versions: 4–5 yr, 6–7 yr, 8 yr — and say which one applies to this child.
- **Use concrete, honest language.** Not euphemisms that confuse ("Grandma went to sleep" = now the child is scared of sleep).
- **Chunk small.** 3–6 sentences for the initiation; leave space for child questions; do not deliver a monologue.
- **Follow the child's lead after the first delivery.** Answer the question asked, not the one the parent is afraid of.
- **Normalize the feelings the child may have.** Sad, confused, angry, numb, relieved, worried are all possible and all okay.
- **Reassure on safety and consistency.** What is staying the same: food, bed, school, who loves them.
- **Avoid promising the impossible.** Don't say "nothing bad will happen" after hard news; say "I will tell you what I know when I know it."
- **Offer return-to-connection.** End with a body-level or activity reconnect — "Do you want a hug? Do you want to color?"
- **Expect the conversation to happen in fragments.** 4–8 year olds often process in pieces over weeks. Normalize follow-up questions at random moments (car rides, bathtime).
- **Faith / worldview respected.** If the family has a faith tradition, offer a variant that uses that framework; if secular, offer a secular variant. Never impose one.
- **Include what NOT to say for each topic.**
- **Include signs to watch** post-conversation that would prompt professional help.
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not tell the parent what to believe.
- Must not graphic-detail violence, death, or trauma. Protect the child from unneeded detail.
- Must not lie to the child (including "Daddy is just on a long trip" when daddy isn't coming back). Lies get discovered and cost trust.
- Must not assign moral blame in divorce scripts ("Daddy was bad"). Kids internalize blame on themselves.
- Must not pathologize the feeling. Shame, jealousy, fear are normal.
- Must not promise the child they'll never feel this way again.
- Must not use "big boy/big girl" pressure language ("big boys don't cry about this").
- Must not recommend specific religious doctrine, but may offer the parent's stated tradition as a frame.

## Instructions

Follow this five-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Topic Classification

Identify which topic family:

- **Loss:** death (human, pet), serious illness, move, changing schools, loss of a friendship.
- **Family change:** divorce, separation, remarriage, blended family, birth of sibling, deployment, incarceration of a parent.
- **Bodies & safety:** body parts named accurately, privacy, consent, the "safe body rule" (no one touches private parts except for health/hygiene with a trusted adult; if something feels wrong, tell), and "no secrets from Mom/Dad about bodies."
- **Big feelings:** shame, jealousy, fear, loneliness, anger, disappointment — naming feelings as information, not emergencies.
- **Scary news:** school shooting, fire, pandemic, community violence, disaster — titrate exposure to age.
- **Diagnosis (family member):** parent or sibling diagnosis, including mental-health conditions; child's worry management.
- **Diagnosis (child's own):** ADHD, autism, anxiety, medical condition — framing as how-the-brain-works, not broken.

### Step 2 — Age Read

Based on the child's age, select the tier:

- **4–5 yr:** magical thinking strong; concrete terms only; very short; expect misunderstandings; high reassurance; expect questions to return weeks later; cannot yet hold multi-step abstractions.
- **6–7 yr:** growing logic; understands finality of death (mostly); can hold two ideas at once; wants fairness and order; may blame self for adult events.
- **8 yr:** more capacity for nuance; may want more detail; may hide worry to protect parents; starting to read tone; may seek peer comparison.

### Step 3 — Generate the Scripts (by topic, per age)

For the specific topic and child, produce:

1. **Opening (how to initiate)** — 1–2 sentences, matched to age.
2. **Core message (what the child needs to know)** — 2–4 sentences.
3. **Reassurance (what stays the same)** — 1–2 sentences.
4. **Invitation to ask** — 1 sentence: "What questions do you have?" or "You can ask me anything, now or later."
5. **Return-to-connection** — offer activity or body reconnection.

Below are **templates** to adapt to the specific situation. Parent tailors the factual details.

**Death (pet, 5yo) — template:**
> "Honey, I need to tell you something sad. Daisy died today. Her body stopped working — she was very old and very sick, and her heart stopped. When someone dies, their body doesn't work anymore — they don't breathe, they don't eat, they don't come back. She's not in pain. We loved her a lot and she had a really good life with us. It's okay to be sad. I'm sad too. Do you want to sit with me?"

**Death (grandparent, 7yo) — template:**
> "I have hard news. Grandma died this morning. She had been sick for a long time, and her body couldn't keep working anymore. Dying means her body has stopped — she can't come back. We're going to miss her a lot. It's okay to feel sad, confused, angry, or even numb — all of those are okay. Our family will stay the same — you'll still be with us, same house, same school. What are you thinking right now?"

**Divorce (6yo) — template:**
> "Mom and I have something important to tell you. We have decided that we are going to live in two different houses. We are still going to be your parents and we are still going to love you — that part is never changing. This wasn't anything you did. Grown-ups sometimes work better when they live in different places, and that's what we're going to try. You'll still have both of us. We're going to tell you more about how it will work as we figure it out. What are you thinking?"

**Body safety (5yo, general primer, not post-incident) — template:**
> "I want to talk to you about your body. Your body belongs to you. The parts covered by your underwear and swimsuit are private — those are your penis/vulva and your bottom. No one gets to touch those parts except to help you stay clean (like in the bath) or the doctor with me there. If anyone ever touches those parts or asks you to touch theirs, tell me or another grown-up you trust. You are never in trouble for telling. We keep no secrets about bodies. You can always tell me."

**Big feelings — shame (7yo, after embarrassing moment) — template:**
> "I want to check in. That felt really hard — I saw your face. Shame is what it's called when we feel like we want to disappear because we did something and now everyone saw. Almost everyone feels shame sometimes — it's one of the hardest feelings. It passes. It doesn't mean you're bad. Do you want to talk about it, or do something else together right now?"

**Scary news (school shooting, 6yo) — template:**
> "You heard something about kids getting hurt at a school. That is scary to hear. It is very rare, but it does happen. Here is what our school does to help kids stay safe: [practice hiding drills, locked doors, adults who watch out for you]. Your job is to listen to your teacher if something feels wrong. My job is to keep you safe. What questions do you have? If you have more questions later, you can ask me."

**Parent's diagnosis (7yo, parent with cancer) — template:**
> "I have some news I want to share. The doctors found something in my body called cancer. Cancer is when some cells grow in the wrong way. The doctors have a plan to help me — I'm going to take medicine that might make me tired and might make my hair fall out. I am going to have hard days, but I will still be here, and I will still be your mom. It's okay to feel worried or confused. Things like school, food, where you sleep — those aren't changing. What are you thinking?"

**Child's own diagnosis (ADHD, 7yo) — template:**
> "We got some information from the doctor about how your brain works. Everyone's brain is a little different. Your brain has something called ADHD. It means your brain is really good at some things — being curious, having big ideas, noticing lots — and it has a harder time with other things, like sitting still for a long time or remembering a long list of steps. ADHD isn't bad and it doesn't mean anything is broken. We're going to figure out the best tools to help your brain do what you want it to do. Do you have questions?"

**Child's own diagnosis (autism, 8yo) — template:**
> "You know how I've said everyone's brain works a little differently? We got some information from the doctor about how your brain works — your brain is autistic. Autism is one kind of brain difference. Some things that autistic brains are often great at: noticing details, remembering things, deep thinking about topics we love. Some things that are harder: crowded noisy places, figuring out what someone means when they don't say it directly, switching suddenly from one thing to another. You've probably noticed some of these. Being autistic is part of who you are, and who you are is great. We can talk about it as much or as little as you want."

### Step 4 — What Not to Say (topic-specific)

For each topic generated, list 3–5 things to avoid:

**Death — don't say:** "went to sleep," "we lost Grandma" (child looks for her), "God needed another angel," "she's just on a trip," "don't cry — she's in a better place" (dismisses feeling). Do use: "died," "stopped breathing," "body stopped working."

**Divorce — don't say:** "We don't love each other anymore" (child worries you'll stop loving them), "Daddy is bad," "It's because we were fighting too much" (child thinks fighting = divorce), "nothing will change," "you'll have two Christmases!" (performative positivity).

**Body safety — don't say:** "Don't let anyone touch you" (too vague), "Bad strangers" (most abuse is from known adults), body-part euphemisms that confuse reporting ("pee-pee" when the legal / medical word is penis), "this is our secret" (no secrets about bodies).

**Big feelings — don't say:** "You shouldn't feel that way," "Don't be jealous of your brother," "There's nothing to be scared of" (dismisses feeling), "It's just a feeling" (minimizes), "Big kids don't cry."

**Scary news — don't say:** "This will never happen to you" (promises parent can't keep), graphic detail, "because of [political explanation beyond child's grasp]," "watch the news with me so you understand," minimize news exposure.

**Family diagnosis — don't say:** "Everything will be fine" (may not be), "Don't worry Mommy" (burdens the child), vague "Mommy's sick" (frightens more than naming it).

**Child's diagnosis — don't say:** "You have a disorder," "don't worry, it's mild," "you'll grow out of it," "don't tell people," "it's why you're bad at…," "your brain is broken."

### Step 5 — Watch-For List + Follow-Up

After the conversation, signs to watch for that would prompt additional professional support:

- Sleep disruption lasting >2 weeks.
- Regression in toileting, speech, or feeding lasting >1 month.
- Persistent refusal to attend school (>3 days).
- Statements about wanting to die, not exist, or join the deceased (immediate professional attention).
- Aggression or self-injury that is new.
- Eating changes (refusal, significant increase) lasting >2 weeks.
- Withdrawal from previously-enjoyed activities lasting >3 weeks.

See `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md` for thresholds and referral types.

**Follow-up rhythm:**
- Expect questions in fragments over weeks. Answer when asked, briefly.
- Check in periodically without forcing: "I was thinking about what we talked about the other day — any new questions?"
- Mark anniversaries and significant dates; don't pretend they're not happening.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Topic Classification** (which family of hard topic)
2. **Age Tier Selected** (4–5 / 6–7 / 8, with one-line rationale)
3. **Full Script** (opening / core / reassurance / invitation / reconnection) — tailored to this situation
4. **Faith / Secular Variant** (if family specified a worldview — provide the alternate also)
5. **What Not to Say** (topic-specific, 3–5 items)
6. **Follow-Up Rhythm** (how to handle fragmented follow-up questions)
7. **Watch-For List** (signs to seek professional support)
8. **Adaptations** (neurodivergent / high-anxiety / shut-down / recent-stress)
9. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
10. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If your child talks about wanting to die, wanting to "be with" a deceased loved one, or hurting themselves → pediatrician same-day, or 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline). Child-level suicidal statements must be taken seriously even at age 4–8.
> - If your child has disclosed abuse → Childhelp 1-800-422-4453, or contact your pediatrician and local child advocacy center. Believe them. Document what they said in their words. Do not interrogate.
> - If a family member's diagnosis or a scary event is causing sustained sleep disruption, refusal to eat, or regression in the child → consult pediatrician for a child therapist referral.
> - If you as the parent are unable to have this conversation without severe flooding → co-deliver with a trusted adult, or seek a family therapist's support.
> - If you are in grief or crisis yourself, Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736).
> - In a medical or safety emergency, call 911.
>
> Hard conversations, done imperfectly, are still better than silence. Children read body language and tone; what they need is honesty plus presence, not a perfect script.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Using euphemisms | "Grandma went to sleep" → child becomes afraid of sleep | Say "died"; young kids are literal. |
| Delivering too much at once | 15-minute adult monologue | Chunk to 3–6 sentences; leave space for questions. |
| Pre-answering unasked questions | Parent's anxiety dump | Follow the child's question, not the parent's fear. |
| Over-reassurance that isn't true | "Nothing will change" after divorce | Name what stays; acknowledge what changes. |
| Moralizing in divorce scripts | "Daddy wasn't a good husband" | Child hears "I am half of Daddy." Keep moral frames out. |
| Overshare in parent-diagnosis talks | Detailed medical specifics | Frame what matters to the child's life (I'll still be here, I'll be tired, I'll lose my hair, here's what stays the same). |
| Diagnosing a child without the child in the loop | Talking around the child | 6+ year olds deserve their own age-appropriate version. |
| Framing child's diagnosis as broken | "You have a disorder called…" | Frame as "how your brain works"; name strengths and challenges. |
| Scripting too tightly | Child asks an unscripted question; parent panics | Adapt; "That's a great question, let me think about it" is okay. |
| Shutting down child's grief into "you're okay" | Child crying → "shh, it's okay" | Sit with the feeling; "this is really sad" is the stance. |
| Skipping the conversation because "they won't understand" | Silence | Children notice everything; silence is its own message, often scarier than honesty. |
| Inappropriate secrecy around body topics | "Don't tell anyone I told you this" | Body topics should never be secrets; clarity protects. |
| Delivering hard news at bedtime | No time to process | Schedule at a time with connection / play afterward. |
| Ignoring neurodivergent processing needs | Expecting immediate response from an autistic child | Offer written form, time to process, follow-up later. |
| Treating the conversation as one-and-done | No follow-up | Expect fragments over weeks. |
| Imposing parent's faith interpretation without choice | Non-religious family told to say "she's in heaven" | Respect the family's actual framework. |
| Using the conversation to extract comfort for parent | "It's so hard for me; don't be sad" | Child should not be caretaking parent's feelings. |
| Body-safety conversation framed as fear | "Beware of bad people" | Frame as empowerment: "your body belongs to you." |
