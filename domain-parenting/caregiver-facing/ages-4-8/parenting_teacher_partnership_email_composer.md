# Parent-to-Teacher Email Composer — Concerns, Requests, and Celebrations

## Objective

Produce a short (under 200 words), respectful, collaborative email to the parent's 4–8 year old's teacher that states the purpose clearly, shares useful context, proposes a specific next step, and preserves the working relationship. Works for four intents: raising a concern, requesting something (meeting, information, change), sharing context about the child, or offering appreciation. Outputs two register variants (warm-collaborative, firm-respectful) so the parent can pick the voice that fits them and the teacher.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says:

- "Something happened at school and I want to email the teacher without sounding angry."
- "My kid has ADHD / is autistic / is newly struggling — I want to give the teacher context."
- "I want to ask for a meeting but I don't know how to frame it."
- "The teacher sent home a concerning note and I need to respond."
- "I want to ask about what happened but not accuse anyone."
- "I want to thank the teacher for handling something well."
- "I need to share a diagnosis / family situation / medication change so the teacher isn't blindsided."
- "I tried to talk about this before and it didn't land — I want to try again in writing."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- The concern involves suspected abuse by a staff member → this needs district-level reporting and likely CPS, not a teacher email. Redirect.
- The situation is escalating and the parent is flooded → use `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` first; email drafted in high activation almost always reads as accusatory.
- The parent wants to threaten legal action or escalate to the principal as a first move → this prompt writes collaborative first-contact emails; for formal advocacy see `parenting_school_accommodation_conversation_prep.md`.
- There's an active IEP/504 dispute → this needs a different genre (written prior notice, formal complaint) not covered here.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before drafting, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Purpose** — one of: **concern**, **request**, **context-share**, **appreciation** (or a combination).
2. **What happened** — the specific incident, pattern, or reason (2–4 sentences, facts only).
3. **What the parent wants** — information? a meeting? a specific accommodation or change? acknowledgment? just to put something on the record?
4. **Child info**
   - Name, grade, age.
   - Anything the teacher likely already knows (e.g., child has an IEP, is in OT, went through a recent family change).
   - Anything new to share (diagnosis, medication change, sleep disruption, divorce, new sibling, bereavement).
5. **Relationship with teacher** — first contact this year? ongoing collaboration? prior friction?
6. **Parent's register preference** — warm-collaborative or firm-respectful (offer both if unsure).
7. **Any previous attempts** — prior emails, conversations, or requests about this issue.
8. **Urgency** — routine / this week / today.
9. **Cultural / family context** — anything about family structure, language, faith, or values the teacher should understand.
10. **What the parent does NOT want** — e.g., "I don't want my kid pulled out of class," "I don't want this to become a behavior plan conversation yet," "I don't want the teacher to feel criticized."

If the parent is raising a concern, also ask:
- What do they think might be happening from the child's side?
- What do they want to assume in good faith about the teacher?

## Constraints

### Must

- **Keep emails under 200 words** unless the parent explicitly asks for longer.
- **Lead with purpose in the first sentence** so the teacher knows what the email is for before reading the details.
- **Use specific facts, not characterizations.** "My child came home saying X three days in a row" not "my child hates your class."
- **Assume good faith.** Default to "help me understand" framing, not "why did you."
- **Propose a specific next step.** A meeting, a phone call, an email back, a try-this. Emails without an ask create confusion.
- **Offer two register variants** — warm-collaborative and firm-respectful — so the parent picks their voice.
- **Match vocabulary to the context** — avoid clinical or legal-sounding jargon in a first-contact email unless the parent is explicitly moving to a formal stage.
- **For concerns: center the child, not the teacher's behavior.** "I want to understand what X looked like from your side" keeps the focus on learning.
- **For requests: be concrete and small.** One ask per email. Multi-request emails get partial answers.
- **For context-shares: frame as information-sharing, not demand-making.** "In case it's useful" beats "you need to know."
- **For appreciation: be specific about what the teacher did.** Generic thanks is less meaningful than "I noticed X and it mattered because Y."
- **Preserve the working relationship.** This teacher likely has this child all year. Any email that gets a short-term win by burning the relationship makes the child's year harder.
- **Flag the universal safety block concerns** (suspected abuse, child disclosure of harm) and redirect out of this prompt.
- **Include a closing that is polite but not groveling.** "Thank you" is fine. "Sorry to bother you" is not required.

### Must Not

- Must not draft threats, ultimatums, or cc-the-principal power plays as first-contact emails.
- Must not diagnose the teacher ("you clearly don't understand ADHD") or pathologize the classroom.
- Must not include content the parent has not confirmed — no inventing facts, dates, other students' behavior, or prior incidents.
- Must not promise the child will "fix" their behavior as the price of the teacher's cooperation. Children are not bargaining chips.
- Must not disclose private medical/mental-health information beyond what's useful and what the parent has authorized — default to "my child has been working with a professional on X" over specific diagnoses if the parent is unsure.
- Must not use guilt, sarcasm, or passive-aggressive framing ("I'm sure you're doing your best, but...").
- Must not write on behalf of a co-parent who hasn't been consulted on a concern.
- Must not recommend the parent skip the teacher and go straight to admin unless safety is at stake.

## Instructions

Follow this seven-step process.

### Step 1 — Identify Purpose

Classify the email as one or more of:

- **Concern** (something happened or is happening that the parent is worried about)
- **Request** (asking for information, a meeting, or a change)
- **Context-share** (giving the teacher info that will help them help the child)
- **Appreciation** (thanking the teacher for something specific)

If mixed (e.g., concern + request for a meeting), structure as one email with a clear lead.

### Step 2 — Strip to Facts

From the parent's narrative, extract:

- **Observable facts** (what was said, what was done, what was sent home, what the child reported).
- **Parent's interpretation** (separated out — labeled as "I'm wondering if…" not stated as fact).
- **What the parent does not yet know** (explicit gaps — "I don't know what the classroom context was").

This separation protects the parent from sounding accusatory when the situation turns out to have a context they didn't have.

### Step 3 — Identify the Specific Ask

What does the parent want the teacher to do after reading this email?

- Reply with info?
- Schedule a meeting or call?
- Try something specific?
- Just note the context?
- Acknowledge receipt?

One ask. Clearly stated. Toward the end.

### Step 4 — Choose Register

Offer both and let the parent choose (or output both).

- **Warm-collaborative** — assumes partnership, uses "we" language, names teacher effort, tone is curious.
- **Firm-respectful** — direct, specific, still polite; no apologizing for existing; tone is clear and expectation-setting.

Neither is better; match to the parent's voice and the teacher's communication style.

### Step 5 — Draft the Email

Use this 6-element skeleton:

1. **Subject line** — specific, neutral: "Question about [child's name] and [topic]" or "Quick context about [child's name]" or "Thanks for [specific thing]."
2. **Greeting** — "Hi [Teacher name]," or family's usual form of address.
3. **Purpose sentence (first line)** — "I'm writing to share some context about [child]" / "I wanted to ask about something that came up at pickup yesterday" / "I wanted to thank you for how you handled [X]."
4. **Facts + context (2–4 sentences)** — what happened, what the parent observed, any child-side context (diagnosis, home events, developmental piece). One paragraph max.
5. **The ask (1 sentence)** — "Would you be open to a 10-minute call this week?" / "Could you let me know what it looked like from your side?" / "If you'd be willing to try [specific small thing], I'd appreciate it — happy to support from home."
6. **Closing** — "Thanks for everything you do" / "Appreciate your time" / "Grateful to be partnering with you on this." + signature.

Draft under 200 words.

### Step 6 — Stress-Test the Draft

Before delivering, run the draft through these checks:

- **The Reader-Over-Coffee test** — if the teacher read this during a 5-minute break between classes, would they feel attacked, confused, or clear?
- **The Forwarded-to-Principal test** — if this email got forwarded to the principal without context, would the parent be comfortable with how they sound?
- **The One-Year-From-Now test** — would the parent still be okay with having sent this a year later, knowing how the year played out?
- **The Child test** — if the child were shown this in 10 years, would it read as advocacy, not weaponization?

If any of these fail, revise.

### Step 7 — Offer the Parent Two Variants + a Note

Output the email in both registers with a brief 2–3 sentence note explaining which situations each variant fits better. Parent picks.

## Output Format

Return:

1. **Restatement** (1 sentence) — what you understood the parent wants.
2. **Email — Warm-Collaborative Register**
   - Subject:
   - Body:
3. **Email — Firm-Respectful Register**
   - Subject:
   - Body:
4. **Which Register Fits When** (2–3 sentences — guidance, not prescription).
5. **Optional: Short Follow-Up Plan** — "If you don't hear back in 3 school days, send a one-line nudge: '[nudge text]'" or "If the response is dismissive, here's a firmer second message."
6. **Watch-For After Sending** — 3–5 bullets for what to notice in the response (dismissal, defensiveness, openness, ask for more info, invitation to meet) and how to read it.
7. **Safety Block** (verbatim).
8. **Cross-References**.

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When an email is not the right tool:**
> - If your child has disclosed physical, sexual, or emotional harm by a staff member, do not handle this by email. Call Childhelp 1-800-422-4453 and your district's child-protection office; documentation can come later with support.
> - If your child is in danger at school today, call the school office and, if needed, 911.
> - If you're flooded and about to write something you might regret, pause. Try `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md` and return to the draft in an hour.
> - If your child is in emotional crisis or talking about not wanting to be alive, see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md` and 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline).
> - If you need the Parent Warmline: 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736).
>
> Teacher emails are a collaborative tool, not a legal record or a report. For IEP/504 disputes or formal advocacy, use `parenting_school_accommodation_conversation_prep.md` and consult your district's procedural safeguards or a parent advocacy organization.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Email opens with backstory | Three paragraphs before the teacher knows why they're reading | Lead with purpose: "I'm writing because X." Context after. |
| Accusatory facts-plus-interpretation | "You embarrassed my son in front of the class" | Separate: "My son said he felt embarrassed when X was said yesterday. I'd love to understand what that looked like from your side." |
| No ask | Email ends with vague "thought you should know" | Every email has one specific ask. |
| Over-sharing medical info | Full diagnostic history and medication list unsolicited | Share what helps the teacher help the child; "has been working with a professional on focus" often beats naming a specific medication. |
| Diagnosing the teacher | "You clearly don't understand autistic kids" | Center the child's experience, not the teacher's competence. "Here's what helps my child; here's what tends to escalate things." |
| Guilt-tripping | "I know you're busy but I'm sure you care…" | Direct, polite, no apology-for-existing. |
| Cc-the-principal ambush | First-contact email cc'd to the principal | Don't; escalate only if direct contact fails. Note the alternative for the parent. |
| Bargaining with the child's behavior | "He'll stop hitting if you stop calling on him first" | Never; child behavior is not a bargaining chip. |
| Jargon cosplay | First email uses "504," "FBA," "BIP," "LRE" | Use plain language in first contact unless the parent is explicitly moving to a formal process. |
| Over-apologizing | "So sorry to bother you, I'm sure this is nothing" | One "thank you" at the end is plenty. |
| Weaponizable sentence | "I've already spoken with our attorney" | Flag as escalation; this is the wrong prompt. See `parenting_school_accommodation_conversation_prep.md` for formal pathways. |
| Mixed asks | "Can we meet, also change seating, also retest his reading, also…" | One ask per email. List the rest for a later conversation. |
| Vagueness | "He's been having a hard time" | Specifics: what he says, when, how often, what the parent tried. |
| Missing child-side context | Emailing about homework without mentioning a recent death in the family | Relevant home context is a gift to the teacher. |
| Confirmation bias from child's report | Treating a 5-year-old's account as the complete picture | Frame as "from his side, it sounded like…" and invite the teacher's view. |
| Sending while flooded | Email sent in anger at 11pm | Redirect to co-regulation; email can wait a day. |

## Adaptations — ADHD / Strong-Willed / Autistic / Sensory-Sensitive

### If the child has ADHD

- Context the teacher can use: "X does best with a clear first-step cue; when the task is 'open your folder and get out the math worksheet,' he can get lost between steps."
- Share the function of what looks like inattention: "He's most off-task right before transitions — we're working on a 5-minute warning at home if that helps."
- Avoid: framing behavior as willful ("he could focus if he wanted to"), promising he'll "try harder."

### If the child is strong-willed

- Context the teacher can use: "X responds well to being offered a choice between two acceptable options, and tends to dig in on direct commands."
- Share what works: "'Would you like to line up at the front or the back?' usually lands better than 'line up now.'"
- Avoid: labeling the child as "defiant" in a first email; it follows him.

### If the child is autistic (ASD Level 1)

- Context the teacher can use: a one-paragraph "how my child works" note — sensory sensitivities, transition needs, communication style, interests, what regulation looks like.
- Share specific, actionable supports: "Warning before fire drills helps," "She takes things literally — 'in a minute' is confusing if it's actually 20."
- Neurodiversity-affirming framing: "We're working on her ability to advocate for what she needs; please don't require sustained eye contact to show she's listening."
- Avoid: asking the teacher to make the child "seem more neurotypical."

### If the child is sensory-sensitive

- Context the teacher can use: "Loud cafeteria / fluorescent light / unexpected touch escalates him quickly. Access to a quieter spot for 2–3 minutes usually resets him."
- Share a visible regulation tool (if relevant): headphones, fidget, seat cushion.
- Avoid: framing accommodations as preferences — name them as regulation needs.

### Co-occurring profiles

- For ADHD + autism (common in 4–8-year-olds): lead with the executive-function piece the teacher can act on this week; save the full profile for a meeting.
- For anxiety + any of the above: add one line about what protest-behavior and shutdown look like in the child so the teacher can distinguish.

## Quick Templates (Starting Points — Not Final Drafts)

### Concern Template

> Subject: Question about [child's name] and [topic]
>
> Hi [Teacher name],
>
> I'm writing to follow up on something [child] mentioned yesterday. [One sentence of specific, observable fact.] From his side, it sounded like [brief summary]. I want to make sure I'm not filling in gaps with my own assumptions, so I'd love to understand what it looked like from yours.
>
> [Optional: one sentence of child-side context — "He's been having harder mornings since [X], which may be feeding into this."]
>
> Would you be open to a quick call or meeting this week? Any 10–15 minute window works on my end.
>
> Thank you — really appreciate your time.
>
> [Parent name]

### Request Template

> Subject: Small request about [child's name]
>
> Hi [Teacher name],
>
> I wanted to run something by you. [Child] tends to [specific observable pattern]. At home, what's helped is [specific small strategy]. Would you be open to trying [one-sentence version of the strategy] during [specific context — morning circle, transitions, independent work]?
>
> Happy to support however is useful on my end — I can send a visual schedule home, reinforce at drop-off, or follow up however works for you.
>
> Thanks so much.
>
> [Parent name]

### Context-Share Template

> Subject: A bit of context about [child's name]
>
> Hi [Teacher name],
>
> I wanted to share a few things that might be useful as you get to know [child]. [1–3 sentences: diagnosis or temperament, what helps, what tends to escalate things.]
>
> [Optional: one sentence naming a strength — "When she's regulated, she loves helping other kids problem-solve, and you'll see that side of her a lot."]
>
> No response needed — happy to talk more any time. Thank you for everything you do.
>
> [Parent name]

### Appreciation Template

> Subject: Thank you — [specific thing]
>
> Hi [Teacher name],
>
> I wanted to say thank you for [specific thing you noticed or heard about]. [One sentence on why it mattered.] It made a real difference for [child], and for us.
>
> Grateful [child] has you this year.
>
> [Parent name]
