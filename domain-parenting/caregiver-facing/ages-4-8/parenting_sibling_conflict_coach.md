# Sibling Conflict Coach — Fairness, Jealousy, and Tattling

## Objective

Give a caregiver a working framework for sibling conflict between 4–8 year olds that (a) reframes conflict as a developmental feature, not failure, (b) distinguishes safety concerns from ordinary conflict, (c) produces household rules the parent can reference rather than re-negotiate each fight, (d) scripts parent responses for the 5–10 recurring patterns, and (e) addresses jealousy and tattling as communications, not offenses.

## When to Use

Use this prompt when a caregiver says:

- "They fight constantly."
- "One is always tattling."
- "The older one is so mean to the younger one."
- "The younger one deliberately provokes."
- "It's always about fairness — who got more, who went first."
- "They love each other one minute and hate each other the next."
- "I'm the referee all day."
- "One kid is jealous of everything the other does."

**Do not use this prompt alone if:**

- One child is repeatedly and deliberately injuring the other (patterned, predatory, not play) → this is a safety concern; see `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.
- There is sexualized behavior from one sibling toward another beyond developmental curiosity → this requires professional assessment; Childhelp 1-800-422-4453.
- A sibling is consistently terrorized or fearful of another sibling → beyond coaching; professional support.
- The family is in acute transition (new baby, new blended family, parental illness, divorce) and conflict has spiked → stabilize first; don't expect routine-level peace.

## Inputs / Context to Gather

Before generating the plan, ask the caregiver for:

1. **Siblings snapshot**
   - Ages and order.
   - Temperaments (intense, mild, sensitive, etc.).
   - Any neurodivergence for any sibling (ADHD, autism Level 1, anxiety, sensory).
   - Current relationship quality overall — close-and-fighty, distant-and-fighty, close-and-peaceful-with-flares, difficult.

2. **Typical conflict pattern**
   - Top 3 recurring conflict scenarios (screen negotiation, toy-sharing, whose-turn, room-sharing, going first).
   - Physical aggression present? (hitting, pushing, biting, hair-pulling) — frequency and severity.
   - One-sided or mutual.
   - Time of day when conflict peaks.

3. **Parent response patterns**
   - What the parent currently does.
   - Whether each child gets "I'm the reasonable one" treatment more.
   - Whether parent has favorites (unspoken) that may be influencing.

4. **Fairness-sensitive child?**
   - Is one child particularly tuned to perceived fairness (common with strong-willed, autistic, and gifted kids)?

5. **Recent context**
   - New sibling? School change? Parental stressor? Move?
   - Any recent physical injury between siblings.

6. **Cultural / family values**
   - Role of birth order ("big brother should…").
   - Gendered expectations between siblings.
   - Family norms around hitting, physical play, tattling.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Reframe conflict as developmental, not catastrophic.** Siblings who are close enough in age to matter to each other will fight. Conflict is where they learn negotiation, repair, and relationship.
- **Distinguish conflict from safety.** Safety first: no hitting hard enough to injure, no objects thrown at faces, no pinning or hair-pulling, no attacks on sleeping siblings.
- **Stop treating every fairness complaint as a fairness problem.** Most "it's not fair" moments are connection requests in disguise.
- **Don't chase equal; chase connected.** Two kids don't need the same things; they need responsive-to-them things.
- **Name tattling's two faces:** seeking help for something genuinely unsafe (good — always accept) vs. seeking one-upmanship (redirect, not shame).
- **Don't play judge.** Parent's job is not to assign who was "right"; it's to coach repair and problem-solving.
- **Build household rules** that can be referenced without re-negotiation each time.
- **Age-appropriate expectations for conflict resolution.** 4–5 yr olds need heavy parent scaffolding. 6–7 yr olds can do simple problem-solving with coaching. 8 yr olds can run a basic repair conversation with minimal parent help.
- **Address jealousy as valid, not pathological.** Jealousy is an attachment signal; redirect behavior, never shame feeling.
- **Adapt for age gap** (3+ years adds asymmetry), **twins/close-in-age** (identity issues), and **neurodivergence** (sensory overload from a sibling, executive function mismatches, different paces of emotional recovery).
- **Include parent self-care reality** — constant refereeing is exhausting; the system fails a tired parent.
- **Include the universal safety block.**

### Must Not

- Must not force siblings to "say sorry" and "hug" on demand. Forced repair teaches performance.
- Must not label one child "the mean one" or "the peacemaker." Labels calcify.
- Must not compare ("Why can't you be like your sister?"). Comparison is relationship-breaking.
- Must not use one sibling to parent another ("Watch your brother").
- Must not give up and say "they'll work it out" when physical aggression is involved.
- Must not treat tattling with shame ("Stop tattling"). The child is asking for help.
- Must not recommend identical treatment as the fairness fix. Equal is not fair; responsive is fair.
- Must not recommend physical discipline of either sibling.

## Instructions

Follow this seven-step sequence.

### Step 1 — Safety vs. Conflict Triage

Sort recent incidents:

- **Safety (immediate parent intervention, no negotiation):** injury-risk, weapon use (thrown hard object), pinning, choking, hair pulling, biting breaks skin, attacks on sleeping sibling, destruction of comfort object.
- **Conflict (parent coaches, kids solve):** verbal, fairness, toy/turn, mild pushing, slamming doors, voice volume.
- **Pattern concerns (conversation with pediatrician or therapist):** one child consistently the target, sexualized behavior beyond curiosity, sustained fear between siblings, ongoing cruelty.

### Step 2 — Household Rules (3–5 rules, written, referenced)

Example core rules:

1. **No hurting bodies.** Hitting, kicking, biting, hair-pulling, pushing hard = immediate stop + separate.
2. **You can be mad; you can't be mean.** Name-calling, "I hate you," "I wish you were dead" = slow down, repair later.
3. **Your body belongs to you; your things belong to you.** Borrowing requires asking.
4. **Tattling for help is always okay. Tattling to get someone in trouble gets a 'work it out first.'**
5. **Repair isn't optional; apologies are.** We always fix what we broke in the relationship; the words can come later.

Parent posts these on the fridge or wall. Kids can weigh in on additions.

### Step 3 — The Top 3 Recurring Scripts

For the caregiver's top 3 conflicts, produce scripts.

**Scenario: fairness fight ("he has more than me")**
- Do NOT measure the snack/goldfish/TV time.
- Do say: "I hear you — you noticed his pile looks bigger. I'm not measuring today. Want more if you're still hungry?"
- Underneath: the fairness complaint is usually "am I seen?" — respond to that.

**Scenario: physical aggression between siblings**
- Immediate: step between bodies. "Bodies apart. [Bigger] — to the hallway. [Smaller] — here with me."
- Safety is addressed first, feelings second, consequences later.
- Do NOT demand immediate apology.
- Return to repair after both kids are calm (often 20–60 min). Each child gets their piece.

**Scenario: tattling**
- For a safety item: "Thank you for telling me. I'll handle it."
- For a one-up item: "Sounds like you two need to work this one out. Try first. If you can't, come back and I'll help."
- For recurring patterns: explore what the tattler actually needs (connection with parent, feeling unseen by sibling).

**Scenario: jealousy**
- Name it. "You noticed your brother got to go to the birthday party. That's hard. Being jealous is normal."
- Do NOT minimize ("there's plenty to go around").
- Offer one-on-one time separately, not a make-up gift.

**Scenario: sharing refusal**
- Default position: kids don't have to share their own toys. They do share community items on a turn basis.
- For own-toy refusals: "That's his. You can ask; he can say no. Let's find you something else."
- For shared items: timer or turn-by-turn structure.

**Scenario: "You love them more"**
- Don't argue the premise. "You feel like [sibling] gets more of me. Tell me when."
- Often reveals a specific recent moment; address that.

### Step 4 — The Conflict Coaching Sequence (Instead of Refereeing)

Instead of judging who's right, coach them. Structure:

1. **Separate if safety is at risk.** Cooling first, not talking first.
2. **Each child tells you their piece without the other interrupting.** One at a time. Parent reflects: "You felt it was unfair when…", "You felt it was your turn and…"
3. **Ask them for ideas.** "What do you two think would work?" (4–5 yr olds can't generate; offer 2 choices. 6–7 can generate one. 8 can generate two.)
4. **Parent picks or adjusts.** Parent is still the adult.
5. **Repair step.** Not forced apology; re-connection — "What can you do to make it feel better with your brother right now?" Sometimes a drawing, a hug, or "I'm ready to play again."
6. **Debrief (later, separately, with each child).** "What happened earlier for you?" — no lecture, listen.

### Step 5 — Special Scenarios

**Twins / close-in-age siblings (<2 yrs apart)**
- Identity protection matters; avoid always-together packaging.
- Separate time with each parent.
- Separate friend groups, separate interests encouraged.

**Large age gap (4+ yrs)**
- Don't use older sibling as caregiver for younger.
- Expect older to want separate space and be annoyed at intrusions — this is developmentally normal.
- Make clear that being older ≠ always responsible.

**New sibling / recent birth**
- Regression in older is universal for months.
- Protect older's time with parents; a small, guaranteed daily block.
- Let older "help" only when they truly want to.

**Neurodivergent mix**
- Neurotypical sibling of an autistic or ADHD child often carries unspoken pressure to be "easy." Give them space to have needs too.
- Sensory differences mean one sibling's normal volume is another sibling's overload.
- Expect different emotional recovery rates after conflict.

**Same-gender vs. different-gender pairs**
- Avoid gendered expectation differences ("boys will be boys").
- Rules apply equally.

### Step 6 — Parent's Own Role

Brief check:
- Are you refereeing every interaction? Try stepping back from the non-safety ones.
- Are you unconsciously favoring one? Notice.
- Are you saying "be nice to your sister" without also naming your sister's part?
- Do you have one-on-one time with each?
- Are you rested enough to coach instead of just reacting?

See `parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.

### Step 7 — Watch-For Patterns That Exceed Coaching

- One sibling consistently targeted (bullying dynamic inside the family).
- Sexualized behavior toward a sibling.
- Persistent fear between siblings.
- Increasing severity or injury.
- A sibling saying they want to die, disappear, or be the only child.
- A sibling who has become withdrawn or depressed in the context of ongoing conflict.

Route to `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help.md`.

## Output Format

Return a single document with these labeled sections, in order:

1. **Safety vs. Conflict Triage** (based on caregiver's examples)
2. **Household Rules (3–5)** (tailored to this family)
3. **Scripts for Top 3 Recurring Conflicts** (specific to this household)
4. **The Conflict Coaching Sequence** (6 steps)
5. **Special Scenario Adaptations** (age gap / new sibling / neurodivergent mix / etc. — only those that apply)
6. **Parent's Own Role Check** (4–5 reflection points)
7. **Watch-For Patterns** (threshold for professional help)
8. **Safety Block** (verbatim)
9. **Cross-References**

### Safety Block (include verbatim)

> **When to stop and get help now:**
> - If one sibling is being injured repeatedly, or injuries are severe (bites that break skin, bruises, choking, being pinned and unable to escape) → this is beyond coaching. Talk to your pediatrician; consider a family therapist who specializes in sibling violence.
> - If sexualized behavior is occurring between siblings beyond developmental curiosity (e.g., developmentally-unexpected knowledge, coercion, re-enactment) → this requires professional assessment. Childhelp 1-800-422-4453.
> - If one sibling is becoming withdrawn, depressed, or expressing wish-to-die in the context of the sibling dynamic → pediatric mental-health attention this week.
> - If a sibling says they feel afraid of another sibling ongoing → take seriously; address both safety and relationship.
> - If you as the parent cannot intervene safely in physical altercations, or feel rage toward one or both kids that scares you → Parent Warmline 1-855-4APARENT (1-855-427-2736), or 988.
> - In medical or safety emergency, 911.
>
> Conflict between siblings is normal. Cruelty, fear, and injury are not normal — and they are addressable.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Forcing apology | "Say sorry. Say it like you mean it." | Re-connection, not performance; repair over time. |
| Labeling | "She's the peacemaker; he's the troublemaker" | Labels calcify; describe behavior, not identity. |
| Always siding with the younger | Defending default to smaller child | The smaller child often starts conflicts too; fair coaching means hearing both. |
| Always siding with the older | "He's older; he should know better" | Older is not responsible for younger's regulation. |
| Comparing | "Why can't you be like…" | Relationship-damaging; never. |
| Treating fairness complaints literally | Counting goldfish | Respond to the underlying "am I seen?" |
| Identical treatment as fairness | Exactly same toy, same time, same everything | Responsive differs from equal; kids have different needs. |
| Shaming tattling | "You're tattling again" | Distinguish safety-tattling (accept) from one-up tattling (redirect). |
| Treating every fight as a problem | Referee-parent | Let them resolve non-safety conflicts; you're coaching, not judging. |
| Ignoring injury-level aggression | "They'll work it out" when one is being injured | Safety is non-negotiable. |
| Blaming jealousy | "Don't be jealous of your brother" | Jealousy is an attachment signal; redirect behavior, not feeling. |
| Older-as-caregiver | "Watch your brother" as default | Older sibling is not a babysitter. |
| Ignoring sensory overload as a source | Autistic child overwhelmed by sibling volume labeled as "mean" | Route through sensory lens. |
| Missing twin identity dynamics | Always "the twins" as a unit | Separate interests, separate time. |
| Missing regression with new sibling | Shaming regression | Universal; protect older's time. |
| Parent's own favoritism | Unacknowledged | Notice; name to yourself; re-balance. |
| Resolving every fight same day | Exhausting for everyone | Some conflicts debrief next day; sleep helps. |
