**Purpose:** Help a caregiver reconnect with an infant or toddler after a sustained absence — through observed phases of reunion (avoidance, ambivalence, gradual rapprochement) — without misinterpreting the child's protective behavior as rejection.

**When to use:** Returning from a hospitalization, work travel, military deployment, custody handoff, separation due to caregiver illness, return after the child stayed elsewhere (grandparent, foster respite). Includes adoptive caregivers reuniting with a child after placement disruption.

**When NOT to use:** Active trauma (acute child reaction to a frightening event) — coordinate with child mental health. Foster placement initial bonding (use foster-specific resource). Suspected attachment disorder — clinical referral.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Sustained avoidance, no reunion behaviors at all over 4+ weeks → child mental health.
- Aggression toward returning caregiver that does not soften over 4+ weeks → child + family therapy.
- Child appears flat, joyless, withdrawn from all caregivers → attachment / depression evaluation.
- Caregiver experiencing PTSD/postpartum mood/grief from the absence → caregiver mental health support in parallel.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Reunion behavior in young children is a recognizable sequence**: protest → despair → detachment was Bowlby's frame for prolonged separation; the recovery sequence often runs avoidance → ambivalence → gradual rapprochement.
2. **Avoidance is a protective behavior, not rejection.** A child who is hurt by a separation and is unsure whether you will leave again does not run into your arms immediately.
3. **The relationship is the goal, not behavioral compliance.** Bedtime, eating, routines may slip during reunion. Don't address those first.
4. **The other caregiver(s) who stayed are your bridge, not competition.** Lean on them.
5. **Pace by the child, not by the calendar.** Some children re-attach in days; some take weeks; foster/medical-context reunions can take months.
6. **Caregivers' own grief, exhaustion, and re-entry stress are real.** A regulated caregiver predicts repair quality.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Frame the child's behavior in attachment terms.
- Provide a 1–4 week pacing plan.
- Address the caregivers (returning + bridging) as a team.
- Address caregiver self-regulation.
- Set expectations for sleep, eating, mood disruption during reunion.
- Honor the child's pace.

**Must Not:**
- Force closeness, push the child to be affectionate.
- Frame avoidance as rejection.
- Bypass the bridging caregiver to "win back" attention.
- Promise full reattachment by specific date.
- Punish reunion-related behavior dysregulation.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Frame the Reunion Behavior

Most common reunion patterns:

- **Avoidance** (looks away, won't engage, goes to other caregiver) — protective. The child is unsure whether you will leave again.
- **Ambivalence** (clings then pushes away, asks for you then refuses) — relational reaching with risk-management.
- **Aggression** (hitting, biting, defiance) — anger at the absence; communicates relationship importance.
- **Regression** (sleep, eating, toileting, language slipping) — return to earlier coping.
- **Apparent indifference** (acts like nothing happened) — sometimes relief; sometimes deeper detachment if absence was very long or repeated.

Tell the caregiver: "Your child is not rejecting you. They are processing."

### Stage 2 — Pacing Plan

**Days 1–3:**
- Be present without demanding closeness.
- Let the child seek you, not the reverse.
- Do parallel activities — read nearby, play with their toys without picking them up uninvited.
- Let the bridging caregiver continue primary care for routines (bedtime, feeding) if the child requests.
- Do not pressure for hugs or kisses.

**Days 4–10:**
- Offer one daily ritual the returning caregiver leads (bath, story, breakfast).
- Let the child invite more.
- Re-establish small predictable patterns.
- Reintroduce caregiver-specific activities (the song, the game, the morning ritual you did before).

**Weeks 2–4:**
- Routines transition back gradually.
- Address regression with patience, not pressure.
- Schedule small one-on-one outings (15–30 min, low-key).
- Replace bridging caregiver as primary attachment figure on the child's pace.

### Stage 3 — Bridging Caregiver Coordination

- The caregiver who stayed is a co-parent in the repair, not a competitor.
- Public alignment ("Mama/Dada is back; we're so glad") supports the child.
- Do not undercut the bridging caregiver in front of the child to "make space" — destabilizes.
- Bridging caregiver may have grief or relief feelings; address adult-to-adult.

### Stage 4 — Disrupted Routines

Expect:
- Sleep disruption 2–6 weeks.
- Eating shifts.
- Toileting regression in older toddlers.
- Mood lability.
- Increased separation distress for several weeks.

Do not address all of these at once. Stabilize relationship first; routines follow.

### Stage 5 — Caregiver Self-Regulation

Returning caregivers often experience:
- Grief about missed time.
- Guilt.
- Frustration ("Why won't they hug me?")
- Exhaustion from re-entry.
- Comparison ("They're closer to the other caregiver now").

Plan for it:
- Talk to a friend, partner, therapist, or veteran parent.
- Don't make repair conditional on the child's response.
- Allow yourself the grief without making it the child's problem.

### Stage 6 — Special Cases

- **Hospitalization (caregiver or child):** Add medical processing; bring familiar transitional objects from home; reintroduce home environment slowly.
- **Military deployment:** Reintegration counseling resources for the family; many bases offer programs.
- **Custody schedule:** Consistency across handoffs; do not pump for information about other home; predictable script.
- **Foster respite return:** Foster-specific support; trauma-informed.
- **Adoptive disrupted placement:** Specialized adoption support and therapy.

### Stage 7 — Tripwires

- 4+ weeks of total avoidance or unrelenting hostility → consult.
- Caregiver despair or postpartum mood resurgence → caregiver support.
- Concurrent regression in milestones → developmental check.

## Adaptations

- **Infant under 9 months:** Object permanence not yet stable; "absence" experienced more as "new caregiver." Re-bond through predictable feeding, comfort, voice.
- **9–24 months (peak separation anxiety window):** Hardest reunion phase. Most pronounced avoidance and clinginess.
- **2–3 years:** Verbal processing possible. "I went to the hospital so a doctor could help my body. I missed you. I'm home now." Repeat. Children re-tell with toys, drawings.
- **Multiple caregivers (extended household, polyamorous, queer family with co-parent network):** All caregivers part of the repair plan.
- **Custody schedule with regular short separations:** Build a goodbye + hello ritual that holds across handoffs; treat reunions as repair, not just routine.
- **Long-deployed military parent:** Reintegration over months, not weeks; child may have changed substantially during absence; rebuild rather than resume.
- **Returning birth parent after foster placement:** Coordinate with caseworker; pace by child; bridging caregiver matters most.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Frame avoidance as rejection | Frame as protective; child is processing |
| Force hugs to "show love" | Let child set pace; presence over closeness |
| Bypass bridging caregiver to "reclaim" | Bridging caregiver is the bridge; align publicly |
| Punish aggression at reunion | Address aggression with co-regulation; the message is "I'm still here" |
| Promise full reattachment by date | Pace by child; weeks to months possible |
| Address all routine slippage at once | Stabilize relationship; routines follow |
| Push the toddler to "say hi" formally | Reunions are not performances |
| Tell the child to feel grateful for the caregiver's return | Place adult feelings on adult conversation |
| Tell the caregiver their grief is misplaced | Grief is a feature; honor it, just don't burden the child |
| Confuse expected reunion behavior with attachment disorder | Disorder = severe, sustained, dysfunctional pattern; refer if criteria |
| Pressure for verbal processing in pre-verbal children | Co-regulation, predictability, presence |
| Compete with the bridging caregiver for affection | Triangulation harms the child |
