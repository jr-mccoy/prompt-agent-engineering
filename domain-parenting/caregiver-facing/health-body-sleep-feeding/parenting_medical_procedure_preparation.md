# Medical Procedure Preparation

**Objective:** Produce a preparation and coping plan for one child and one procedure:
when to tell them, what to say in honest sensory terms, which choices they get, the
coping plan and comfort position, the questions to ask the medical team, day-of
logistics, and aftercare with clear thresholds for extra help.

**When to Use:**
- A child has a scheduled procedure: vaccines, blood draw, IV, allergy testing,
  dental work under sedation, MRI or CT, endoscopy, stitches removal, surgery, or an
  overnight admission.
- A previous procedure went badly and the next one is coming.
- A child is very afraid of needles.
- **Not this prompt if** you need to tell a 4–8-year-old about a **diagnosis** (their
  own or a family member's) — use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_hard_topics_age_appropriate_scripts.md`.
  If a young child is struggling to reconnect after a long hospitalisation or
  separation, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-0-3/parenting_attachment_repair_after_long_absence.md`.
  A clinician treating procedural anxiety should use
  `domain-psychology/populations/child-adolescent/psychology_pediatric_anxiety_protocol_session_plan.md`.

## Safety Block

- **Follow the hospital's fasting (nil-by-mouth) instructions exactly.** Eating or
  drinking outside them can cancel surgery or cause aspiration under anaesthesia.
  Rules differ by hospital; do not substitute general guidance.
- **Tell the team** about recent coughs, fever, or vomiting; loose teeth; previous
  anaesthesia reactions in the child or family (e.g. malignant hyperthermia);
  allergies (including latex); and all medications and supplements.
- **After discharge**, call the team or seek care for fever, breathing difficulty,
  uncontrolled pain, bleeding, vomiting that prevents drinking, or signs of
  dehydration; emergency → 911.
- **Fainting with needles** (vasovagal) in a child or parent: tell staff before the
  procedure so it can be done lying down.
- A child who says they want to die or harm themselves at any point → 988 (US).

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, developmental level, temperament, neurodivergence, past medical
   experiences (good and bad), fears, what helps them cope.
2. **Procedure:** what, where, how long, awake or sedated/anaesthetised, whether a
   parent can be present (for induction, during MRI, in recovery).
3. **Hospital supports:** child life specialist, play/preparation sessions, mock MRI
   scanner, numbing cream policy, comfort-positioning practice.
4. **Family:** parent's own medical anxiety; siblings; language.

## Method

1. **Time the telling by age (ST-02).**
   - **2–3:** same day or the day before; very short.
   - **4–6:** 1–3 days before; use play with a toy medical kit.
   - **7–12:** about a week before; can handle more detail and questions.
   - **Teens:** as soon as it's scheduled; include them in decisions and consent
     discussions with the team.
2. **Tell the truth, in sensory steps (CM-11).** What they will see, hear, feel,
   smell, and do, in order — because accurate expectations reduce fear more than
   reassurance, and a child who is told "it won't hurt" learns not to trust the next
   explanation. Say "you'll feel a quick pinch or poke, then it's done" — not "it
   won't hurt."
3. **Use non-threatening words (RT-04).** Replace "shot" with "medicine through a
   tiny straw"; "cut" with "make a small opening"; "put to sleep" with "special sleep
   medicine called anaesthesia — a different kind of sleep where you don't feel
   anything, and the doctor wakes you up when it's done" (because "put to sleep" is
   how many families describe euthanising pets). Analogies fit the child: MRI as "a
   big camera shaped like a tunnel that knocks loudly while it takes pictures."
4. **Offer real choices, never false ones.** "Which arm?" "Sitting on my lap or on
   the bed?" "Watch or look away?" Never "Are you ready for your shot?" if the answer
   cannot be no.
5. **Build the coping plan.**
   - **Comfort positioning:** child sits upright, hugged by parent (chest-to-chest or
     back-to-chest) rather than held down flat.
   - **Numbing:** ask for a topical anaesthetic cream (applied 30–60 minutes before),
     vapocoolant spray, or a vibration-cold device.
   - **Distraction:** bubbles, pinwheel breathing, I-spy, a video or game, counting.
   - **One voice:** one adult (often the parent or child life specialist) coaches;
     others stay quiet.
   - **Under-1s:** breastfeeding or sucrose during the needle where offered.
6. **Validate during and after (NE-07).** "That was hard, and you did it." Avoid
   "big boys don't cry" and avoid apologising repeatedly (it signals danger).
7. **Rehearse.** Medical play at home; mock scanner or hospital tour if available;
   practice lying still for MRI (count how long they manage).
8. **Plan aftercare.** Comfort item, favourite food when allowed, pain plan from the
   team, and a debrief: "What was the hardest part? What helped?" Note what to repeat
   next time.
9. **Screen for medical traumatic stress** at 2–4 weeks: nightmares, avoidance of
   doctors or reminders, new fears, regression, irritability lasting beyond a month →
   pediatrician and a trauma-informed therapist. Severe needle fear that blocks
   necessary care (refusing vaccines or blood tests with panic) → CBT with graded
   exposure; applied tension for those who faint.

## Output Format

```
# Procedure Prep Plan — [child, age], [procedure, date, location]

## When we tell (date) + opening script
## Step-by-step sensory description (see / hear / feel / smell / do)
## Words we use (and words we avoid)
## Choices offered
## Coping plan (position, numbing, distraction, coach)
## Rehearsal (medical play, tour, mock scanner)
## Questions to ask the team
## Day-of logistics (fasting, what to bring, who stays)
## Aftercare + debrief
## Watch-for at 2–4 weeks → when to get help
```

## Verification

- [ ] Telling time fits the child's age.
- [ ] Description is step-by-step and sensory, with no "it won't hurt."
- [ ] Euphemisms like "put to sleep" are replaced.
- [ ] Only real choices are offered.
- [ ] Coping plan names a position, a numbing request, a distraction, and one coach.
- [ ] Fasting instructions deferred to the hospital.
- [ ] Medical traumatic stress and needle-phobia thresholds are explicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| False reassurance | "It won't hurt at all" | Honest, brief sensory truth. |
| Telling a toddler a week ahead | Days of dread | Same day or day before for 2–3s. |
| Telling a teen on the day | Teen feels ambushed | Include them early. |
| Holding a child flat | Restraint by several adults | Upright comfort positioning. |
| Too many voices | Everyone talking at once | One coach. |
| Calling post-procedure clinginess trauma | Referral after 3 clingy days | Short-term regression is common; screen at 2–4 weeks. |

## Adaptations

- **Autistic child:** visual step-by-step story with photos; ask for a quiet room,
  dimmed lights, and the first or last slot; sensory items; extra time.
- **Previous bad experience:** name it ("Last time was really hard. This time we
  have a plan."); involve child life early.
- **Chronic illness / frequent procedures:** a written coping plan the child helps
  design that travels with them.
- **Parent with medical anxiety:** choose the calmer adult as coach; parent's own
  breathing plan.

## Example Output

```
# Procedure Prep Plan — Mia, 5, MRI of the head (no sedation planned),
# Children's Hospital, Thurs 10 Apr, 9:00

## When we tell
Tues 8 Apr, after dinner, with toy camera and a shoebox "tunnel."
"On Thursday we're going to the hospital so a doctor can take special
pictures of your head with a big camera. No needles for this one."

## Sensory steps
See: a big round tunnel and a bed that slides in. Hear: loud knocking and
buzzing — you'll wear headphones. Feel: a soft cushion holding your head
still. Do: lie very still like a statue while the pictures happen —
about 30 minutes. Mum will be in the room holding your foot.

## Words
Use: special pictures, tunnel camera, statue game. Avoid: "scan you,"
"tube," "trapped."

## Choices
Which movie on the goggles; holding her bunny; which sock Mum holds.

## Coping plan
Statue game practice at home (target 10 minutes, rewards sticker);
breathing: smell the flower, blow the candle; Mum is the one coach.
Ask child life about a mock-scanner visit. If she cannot stay still,
the team will discuss sedation — not a failure.

## Questions for the team
Can Mum stay in the room? Video goggles available? What if she moves?
When will results come?

## Day-of
No fasting (no sedation) — confirm with the call the day before. Bring
bunny, metal-free clothes.

## Aftercare
Pancake breakfast after. Debrief: "Loudest part? What helped?"

## Watch-for at 2–4 weeks
Nightmares, refusing doctor visits, new fear of loud noises lasting >1
month → pediatrician.
```

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 Structured Sequential Instructions** — age-timed telling and step-by-step sensory sequence.
- **NE-07 Emotional Validation First** — fear named during and after, without false reassurance.
- **RT-04 Analogical Reasoning** — child-sized analogies (tunnel camera, tiny straw).
- **CM-11 Reasoning-Based Constraint Design** — each language rule explains why (trust, euthanasia association).
