# Interfaith or Mixed-Belief Household

**Objective:** Produce a written family agreement on religion and belief for two
parents with different traditions (or one with none): what each parent cannot give
up, what each can flex on, the concrete decisions for rites, holidays, education, and
everyday practice, the words the children hear about the difference, the response to
relatives, and the review points as the children grow.

**When to Use:**
- Parents of different religions, denominations, or levels of observance — or one
  religious and one non-religious parent — are expecting or raising children.
- A specific decision is pressing: a naming or welcoming ceremony, a coming-of-age
  rite, a religious school, which holidays to host.
- Grandparents or relatives are pressing for one tradition.
- A child is asking, "Which one are we?" or "Who's right?"
- **Not this prompt if** you want to **learn about** or **compare** religions — this
  prompt deliberately does not teach any tradition's beliefs or practices; consult the
  family's own clergy, teachers, or texts. If the parents are **separated** and
  disagree, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/co-parenting/parenting_coparenting_shared_decision_framework.md`
  (and custody counsel for legal disputes). For the household's broader values and
  rules, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/cross-age/parenting_household_constitution_designer.md`.
  For a relative who will not let it go, use
  `domain-personal-development/prompts/relationships/relationships_difficult_family_member_strategy.md`.

## Safety Block

- **Religion is being used to control, threaten, or isolate a partner or child**
  (threats to take the children abroad for upbringing, forced practice under threat,
  cutting a parent off from the child) → National Domestic Violence Hotline
  1-800-799-7233 (US); a family-law attorney about abduction-prevention orders where
  travel is threatened.
- **A disagreement involves the child's medical care** (refusing vaccinations,
  transfusions, or treatment on religious grounds, or decisions about body-altering
  rites) → those are medical decisions to make with the child's clinician; a child's
  urgent medical care is not delayed for the parents' dispute.
- **A teen is distressed, cut off, or threatened** over their own beliefs or
  identity → talk with a counsellor; 988 (US) for any self-harm talk.
- Outside the US, use your local domestic-abuse, legal, and crisis services.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Each parent's tradition** in their own words, level of practice, and what it
   means to them (belief, culture, community, family loyalty).
2. **Children:** ages, what they already do and ask.
3. **Decisions on the table** and their deadlines.
4. **Extended family:** who presses, for what, how hard.
5. **Community and school options** near you.
6. **Past agreements** (e.g. a promise made at the wedding) and how each now feels.

## Method

1. **Keep scope to parenting logistics (CM-03).** This plan decides *what the family
   does* — not which beliefs are true. Every item is phrased as an action ("Child
   attends X's weekend class until 12"), not a doctrine.
2. **Build each parent's value hierarchy (DP-29).** Each parent separately sorts
   items into **non-negotiable** (would feel like losing themselves or betraying
   family), **important** (would grieve but could flex), and **preference**. Write
   the reason beside each non-negotiable. Common items: a welcoming or naming rite,
   a coming-of-age rite, specific holidays, dietary practice at home, religious
   education, the children's identity label, burial and end-of-life wishes.
3. **Map positions and interests (MP-10).** Behind a position ("they must be raised
   in my faith") is usually an interest (belonging, honouring a parent, community,
   moral framework, continuity after a loss). Interests can often be met in more than
   one way.
4. **Compare options without judging either tradition (NE-16).** For each decision
   lay out options neutrally: one tradition, both, neither formally, child chooses
   later; with pros and cons for the child, each parent, and relatives. Common family
   models: **one primary tradition** with the other honoured culturally; **both
   traditions** in parallel; **shared values with exposure** to both and a later
   choice.
5. **Decide the concrete items.** Rites (which, when, who officiates — check what
   each community permits for interfaith families), holiday calendar (which are
   hosted, which visited), religious education, school, home practice, what the
   children call themselves.
6. **Script what the children hear.** "Mum is Hindu and Dad is Catholic. We
   celebrate Diwali and Christmas. People believe different things, and our family
   respects both. You can ask either of us anything." Avoid making the child choose
   between parents or carry a parent's grief.
7. **Plan the relative responses.** A shared line both parents use: "We've decided
   together, and we're happy with it. The kids will spend Diwali with you."
8. **Set review points** — before each rite age, at a school transition, and when a
   child asks for something different. Older children's own convictions get
   increasing weight.

## Output Format

```
# Family Agreement on Faith and Belief — [parents], [children, ages]

## Scope note: what this plan decides (and does not)
## Value hierarchies (each parent): non-negotiable / important / preference, with reasons
## Interests behind positions
## Decisions: rites / holidays / education / home practice / identity label
## Options considered (neutral comparison) for any contested item
## What the children hear (script by age)
## Our shared answer to relatives
## Review points and the child's growing say
```

## Verification

- [ ] No item teaches, compares, or ranks religious beliefs.
- [ ] Each parent's non-negotiables are recorded with reasons.
- [ ] Each contested decision shows at least two options compared neutrally.
- [ ] Children are not asked to choose between parents.
- [ ] Relatives get one shared response both parents use.
- [ ] Medical decisions and coercion are routed out.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Teaching religion | Explaining one faith's doctrine to "help decide" | Logistics only; doctrine goes to the family's own teachers. |
| Ranking traditions | "Christianity is more flexible, so..." | Compare options for this family, not faiths. |
| False equivalence of stakes | Treating a parent's core identity as a preference | Record non-negotiables and their reasons. |
| Child as tie-breaker | "Let the 5-year-old choose" | Parents decide early items; the child's say grows with age. |
| Ignoring the non-religious parent | Belief vs. "nothing" | A secular parent's values are a full tradition here. |
| One-time agreement | Wedding promise treated as fixed | Review at life stages. |

## Adaptations

- **One partner converted or left a tradition:** grief, family pressure, and
  identity may be sharp; pace decisions and consider couples support.
- **Blended family:** stepchildren may already have a tradition from their other
  parent; respect it.
- **Living near only one community:** plan deliberate exposure to the other.
- **Teen with their own convictions:** respect them, including choosing neither;
  agree what family events they still attend.
- **Cultural, not religious, practice:** treat food, language, and festivals as
  heritage that can continue regardless of belief.

## Example Output

```
# Family Agreement on Faith and Belief — Anjali (Hindu) and Tom (Catholic),
# with Maya (4) and Rohan (1)

## Scope
This decides what our family does. It does not decide whose beliefs are true.

## Value hierarchies
Anjali — non-negotiable: kids celebrate Diwali with her parents (her
mother's health; continuity). Important: a naming ceremony for Rohan;
vegetarian food at home. Preference: some Sanskrit prayers at bedtime.
Tom — non-negotiable: kids baptised (promise to his late father).
Important: Christmas and Easter Mass; Catholic school considered.
Preference: grace at dinner.

## Interests
Anjali: belonging for her parents and the kids. Tom: honouring his dad;
a moral community.

## Decisions
Rites: Rohan's naming ceremony at Anjali's parents' home (Nov); baptism
for both kids at St. Joseph's in spring (parish allows interfaith
families; godparents one from each side).
Holidays: we host Diwali and Christmas; visit for Holi and Easter.
Education: Maya at the public primary; weekend options revisited at 7.
Home: vegetarian at home; grace at dinner in turns, either tradition.
Identity: "We're a Hindu and Catholic family."

## Options compared (school)
Catholic school: community for Tom; less exposure to Hindu practice.
Public school + weekend activities: balance; more organising.
Chosen: public school, review at 7.

## What the children hear
Maya (4): "Mummy is Hindu and Daddy is Catholic. We do Diwali and
Christmas. You can ask either of us anything."

## Shared answer to relatives
"We've decided together and we're happy with it. We'd love you at
Rohan's naming day and at the baptism."

## Review points
Before Maya's 7th birthday (first communion question); when Maya asks.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DP-29 Value Hierarchy Construction** — each parent's non-negotiables, important items, and preferences with reasons.
- **MP-10 Position Mapping** — positions traced to underlying interests that can be met in more than one way.
- **NE-16 Non-Judgmental Comparison** — options compared for the family without ranking traditions.
- **CM-03 Scope Definition** — the plan decides family logistics, never doctrine.
