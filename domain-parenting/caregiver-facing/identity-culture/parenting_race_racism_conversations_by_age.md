# Talking with Children About Race and Racism

**Objective:** Produce a race-conversation plan for a specific child and family: what
they are noticing at their age, the words and stories to use now, ready scripts for
the unplanned moments, identity and bias preparation suited to the family's
background, small anti-bias actions, and when race-related harm needs outside help.

**When to Use:**
- A parent wants to talk about race and racism but is unsure what is right at their
  child's age, or worries about saying the wrong thing.
- A child has said something about skin colour or race in public, repeated a slur,
  or asked a hard question after a news event.
- A child of colour has experienced a racist comment or exclusion, or a family is
  preparing a child for that possibility.
- A transracially adoptive or multiracial family is building a child's racial
  identity.
- **Not this prompt if** you need a one-off script for a 4–8-year-old across many
  hard topics — use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_hard_topics_age_appropriate_scripts.md`.
  If a child is being repeatedly targeted, run
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-9-12/parenting_bullying_target_response_protocol.md`
  alongside this. If a parent is looking for a therapist who understands their
  family's culture, use
  `domain-psychology/client-self-use/pre-therapy/clientself_culturally_affirming_therapist_screening.md`.

## Safety Block

- **Racial harassment, threats, or violence** against the child or family →
  document (date, words, witnesses, screenshots), report to the school and, for
  threats or violence, police; in the US ask whether it will be recorded as a hate
  incident or crime.
- **Race-related distress that does not ease** (persistent fear, sleep or school
  problems, hating their own skin or hair, withdrawing for >2–4 weeks) →
  pediatrician and a culturally responsive child therapist.
- **Self-harm or suicidal talk** → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US) now.
- **A child is joining or being drawn into hate content online** → see
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-13-18/parenting_teen_friend_group_concerns.md`
  (radicalisation vector) and talk to the school.
- Outside the US, use your local reporting and crisis services.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, racial and ethnic identity, how they describe themselves.
2. **Family:** parents' racial backgrounds; multiracial, transracially adoptive, or
   mixed-heritage details; extended family views.
3. **Context:** neighbourhood and school diversity, recent events, what the child has
   seen or heard.
4. **What prompted this** — a question, a comment, an incident, or planning ahead.
5. **Parents' comfort and own experiences** of race and racism.

## Method

1. **Start from what children actually notice.** Infants distinguish faces by race
   within the first year; by 3–5 many children show preferences and pick up adult
   bias from silence and patterns around them. "Colour-blind" silence does not
   prevent bias — it leaves children to draw their own conclusions.
2. **Use a racial-socialisation frame (DS-01).** Families combine four messages
   (Hughes et al.): cultural pride and heritage; preparation for bias; equal worth of
   all people; and — to avoid — promoting mistrust of whole groups. The
   anti-bias education goals (Derman-Sparks) add: a confident identity, comfort with
   difference, recognising unfairness, and acting against it.
3. **Stage the conversation (ST-44).**
   - **2–4:** name differences neutrally and positively: "Yes, Kai's skin is dark
     brown and yours is light peach. Skin colour comes from melanin. Everyone's
     body has some." Books and dolls showing many skin tones.
   - **5–7:** fairness and unfairness: "Some people are treated unfairly because of
     their skin colour. That's called racism. It's wrong, and we can help stop it."
   - **8–11:** history and systems in simple form (laws that kept people apart,
     the people who changed them); spotting stereotypes in media; what to do as a
     bystander.
   - **12+:** current events, structural racism, their own identity questions,
     discussion rather than lecture.
4. **Script the unplanned moments with good/bad contrasts (NE-04).** A 4-year-old
   says loudly in a shop, "Why is that man so dark?" — **Less helpful:** "Shh, don't
   say that!" (teaches that skin colour is shameful). **More helpful:** "People have
   lots of skin colours. His is dark brown, like Grandma's coat. Yours is light
   brown." Later, in private: "It's fine to notice. Let's talk about how we say it
   so it's kind." A child repeats a slur: stay calm, ask where they heard it, name
   it plainly ("That word is used to hurt Black people. We don't use it."), and
   follow up with the source.
5. **When the child is hurt by racism, validate first (NE-07).** "That was racist,
   and it was wrong. It's not about anything wrong with you." Then act together
   (school, adult in charge) and rebuild pride.
6. **Prepare children of colour for bias** at an age-matched level: naming what may
   happen, scripts, trusted adults, and pride. Many Black families also have a
   specific conversation about police and safety in adolescence; it is the family's
   call how and when, and it sits alongside joy and pride, not instead of them.
7. **Commit to small actions:** diverse books, friendships, and media; noticing
   who is missing; speaking up when family members make racist comments.

## Output Format

```
# Race & Racism Plan — [child, age], [family context]

## What [child] is noticing now
## Our messages (pride / preparation / equal worth / action)
## Words and stories for this stage; next stage at [age]
## Scripts for unplanned moments (2–3, with less-helpful vs. more-helpful)
## If [child] experiences racism: our response
## Identity building (for children of colour / multiracial / adopted)
## Our actions this term
## When to get help
```

## Verification

- [ ] Plan addresses race directly; no colour-blind default.
- [ ] Language is staged to the child's age with a next stage.
- [ ] Scripts include a calm public response and a private follow-up.
- [ ] Response to racism experienced validates first, then acts.
- [ ] Bias preparation is included for children of colour and balanced with pride.
- [ ] Harassment and distress routes are explicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Shushing | "Don't say that!" to a 3-year-old's observation | Noticing is normal; name it neutrally. |
| Colour-blind messaging | "We don't see colour" | Children see it; silence teaches bias. |
| Reading curiosity as racism | Treating a toddler's question as prejudice | Distinguish curiosity from hurtful intent. |
| Fear-only preparation | All warnings, no pride | Pair preparation for bias with pride and joy. |
| One big talk | A single conversation at 10 | Short, repeated, age-staged. |
| Over-assigning identity | Parent choosing a mixed child's label | Let the child describe themselves; support their language. |

## Adaptations

- **Transracially adoptive family:** parents of a different race need deliberate
  mirrors — adults, mentors, communities, hair and skin care skills, and honest talk
  about the parents' blind spots.
- **Multiracial child:** support all heritages; expect "what are you?" questions and
  give them answers they choose.
- **White family in a white area:** diversity of books, media, and friendships takes
  intention; focus on fairness and allyship.
- **Neurodivergent child:** explicit social rules about commenting on bodies, with
  examples; scripts practised.
- **Recent immigrant family:** race categories may be new; connect them to the
  family's own experience.

## Example Output

```
# Race & Racism Plan — Theo, 5, white mum (Hannah), Black British dad (Marcus)

## Noticing now
Theo asked at the park: "Why is my skin browner than Mummy's but lighter
than Daddy's?" Kids at nursery asked if Hannah is his "real mum."

## Our messages
Pride in both families (Jamaican and English); preparation (some people
are unfair about skin colour); everyone has equal worth; we stand up.

## Words now
"Your skin is light brown. It comes from melanin, and you got some from
Daddy and some from Mummy. Daddy's family is from Jamaica, Mummy's from
Yorkshire. You're both." Next (6–7): what racism is, using a story.

## Unplanned moments
At nursery, asked "Is that your real mum?": Theo: "Yes, she's my mummy."
Hannah later: "Families can have different skin colours. Ours does."
Less helpful: "That's a rude question." More helpful: answer simply,
then talk to the teacher about family books.

## If Theo experiences racism
"That was unkind and unfair. There's nothing wrong with your skin."
Tell the nursery the same day; Marcus and Theo pick a favourite book about
a boy like him that evening.

## Identity
Hair care learned from Marcus's sister (Hannah practising twists); Saturday
football with cousins in Brixton; books: dual-heritage characters.

## Actions this term
Ask nursery for books showing mixed families; Hannah speaks to her uncle
about his comments at Christmas.

## When to get help
Theo says he hates his hair or skin, or is scared to go to nursery for
>2 weeks → GP and a culturally responsive child therapist.
```

## Techniques Used

- **ST-44 Progressive Complexity Scaffolding** — the same honest message deepened stage by stage.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — racial-socialisation dimensions and anti-bias education goals organise the plan.
- **NE-04 Good vs Bad Example Calibration** — less-helpful and more-helpful scripts for unplanned moments.
- **NE-07 Emotional Validation First** — a hurt child hears "that was wrong, not you" before any action.
