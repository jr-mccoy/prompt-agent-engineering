# Raising Multilingual Children

**Objective:** Produce a family language plan: which language each adult uses when,
how many hours a week the child actually hears and uses the heritage language, the
real reasons the child needs to speak it, what to do when they answer in English (or
the community language), and when a concern is about language development rather than
language choice.

**When to Use:**
- Parents speak a language at home that differs from school and community, and want
  the child to keep it.
- A child understands the heritage language but answers only in the community
  language ("receptive bilingual"), or has started refusing it.
- Parents in a mixed-language couple disagree on who speaks what.
- Grandparents or relatives can no longer talk easily with the child.
- **Not this prompt if** you are worried a child under 3 is **late to talk** —
  start with `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-0-3/parenting_speech_language_milestone_check.md`
  (which handles bilingual milestones) and use this plan alongside. For broader
  identity and race conversations, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/identity-culture/parenting_race_racism_conversations_by_age.md`.
  To agree the plan as a family, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/cross-age/parenting_family_meeting_facilitator.md`.

## Safety Block

This is mostly a planning prompt; the escalation points are developmental:
- **Signs of language disorder in both languages combined** — no babbling by 12
  months, no words by about 16–18 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months, loss of
  words or skills at any age, or a child much harder to understand than peers in
  both languages → pediatrician and a speech-language pathologist (SLP) who will
  assess **both** languages. Never stop the home language because of a delay;
  bilingualism does not cause language disorder.
- **A child is mocked or bullied for their language or accent** → school response;
  persistent distress → pediatrician.
- **Refusal is part of wider distress** (withdrawal, school refusal, low mood) →
  look at the wider picture; see a clinician if it lasts >2–4 weeks.
- Outside the US, use your local health and school services.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Languages:** each adult's languages and fluency; community and school language.
2. **Child:** age, which language they understand and speak, how they respond.
3. **Week map:** who the child is with, in which language, hour by hour.
4. **Assets:** grandparents, heritage-language school or playgroup, media, travel,
   community.
5. **Goal:** understand only, speak, read and write; why it matters to the family.
6. **Disagreements** between adults, and any language-related shame or history.

## Method

1. **Choose a family language policy (DS-01).** Common strategies:
   - **One parent, one language (OPOL):** each parent consistently uses one
     language. Works best when the heritage-language parent has many waking hours
     with the child.
   - **Minority language at home (MLAH):** everyone uses the heritage language at
     home; the community language comes from school. Strongest for maintenance; needs
     both adults able to use it.
   - **Time and place:** heritage language on weekends, at meals, or in the car.
   Annick De Houwer's "harmonious bilingual development" goal: the child and family
   feel good about the languages, not only about fluency.
2. **Do the exposure arithmetic (NE-11).** `Heritage share = heritage-language waking
   hours ÷ total waking hours per week`. Research (e.g. Pearson and colleagues)
   suggests children hearing a language less than roughly 20–25% of the time are much
   less likely to speak it. Count honestly: a parent who speaks it but switches when
   tired counts less.
3. **Find why the child is refusing (DP-06).** The dominant driver is usually one of:
   **no need** (everyone understands the community language), **effort** (they are
   less fluent and it is harder), **identity** (they want to sound like peers),
   **conflict** (being corrected or nagged), or **emotion** (language tied to a
   stressful relationship). The driver decides the response.
4. **Create genuine need.** People with whom the heritage language is the only
   option — grandparents on video, a babysitter, a summer visit, a heritage-language
   class or club, a playdate with another heritage-language family. A parent can
   "not understand" in a playful way for young children; avoid it with older ones.
5. **Respond to refusal without shame.** Keep speaking the heritage language
   yourself; accept answers in either language; recast ("Ah, you want *agua*? Here's
   your *agua*") rather than demanding repetition; never mock their accent or errors;
   make the language fun (songs, games, cooking, cartoons, comics). For teens,
   connect it to their own interests and identity, and to future value.
6. **Know normal bilingual patterns.** Code-mixing ("I want the *pelota*") is
   normal and skilled; a silent period in a new language; vocabulary split across
   languages (total words matter); temporary dominance shifts after school starts.
7. **Run a 6-week trial (DP-11).** One or two changes, tracked weekly: heritage
   share, how often the child uses it unprompted.

## Output Format

```
# Family Language Plan — [child, age], languages [..]

## Goal and why it matters to us
## Our policy (OPOL / MLAH / time-and-place) and who does what
## Exposure now: heritage hours ÷ waking hours = [..]%; target [..]%
## Why [child] refuses (dominant driver) and our response
## Sources of genuine need (people, places, activities)
## How we respond in the moment (recast, no shaming)
## Normal patterns we won't worry about
## When to see an SLP
## 6-week trial: changes, tracking, review date
```

## Verification

- [ ] Exposure share is calculated from an hour-by-hour week, not estimated.
- [ ] Strategy fits who actually spends time with the child.
- [ ] Refusal driver is named and the response matches it.
- [ ] At least two sources of real need are listed.
- [ ] No shaming, forced repetition, or mocking in the response plan.
- [ ] Language-disorder signs are judged across both languages, with SLP route.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Bilingualism blamed for delay | "Stop the home language so she talks" | Bilingualism doesn't cause disorder; assess both languages. |
| Code-mixing seen as confusion | Worry about "Spanglish" | Mixing is normal and rule-governed. |
| Refusal read as rejection of family | "He's ashamed of us" | Usually need, effort, or peer identity; find the driver. |
| Correction as teaching | "Say it properly in Polish!" | Recast and keep talking; correction drives refusal. |
| Overestimating exposure | "We speak it at home" (30 min/day) | Count hours; often under 20%. |
| All-or-nothing | Giving up when the child answers in English | Receptive skill is valuable and can grow later. |

## Adaptations

- **One heritage-language parent who works long hours:** shift heritage time to
  bedtime routines, weekend mornings, and grandparents by video; consider MLAH if the
  other parent can learn.
- **Three or more languages:** decide which ones get active support; one may be
  receptive only.
- **Neurodivergent child or developmental delay:** keep the heritage language — current
  evidence does not support dropping it; work with an SLP on priorities.
- **Adopted child with a birth language:** the language often fades fast; keep links
  through community and media, and support the child's choices.
- **Teen:** connect to identity, travel, work, and friends; heritage-language credit
  or exams can motivate.

## Example Output

```
# Family Language Plan — Sofia, 6; Spanish (Dad) + English (Mum, school)

## Goal
Sofia can talk with Abuela in Bogotá and read simple Spanish by 10.

## Policy now: OPOL (Dad Spanish, Mum English)

## Exposure
Waking hours/week ≈ 91. Spanish: Dad weekday mornings 1 h × 5 + bedtime
0.5 h × 5 + Saturday 4 h + Abuela video 0.5 h = 12 h. Share = 12 ÷ 91 =
13%. Target ≥ 25% (≈ 23 h).

## Why Sofia refuses (answers Dad in English since starting school)
Driver: no need — Dad understands English, and her friends all speak
English. Some effort (smaller Spanish vocabulary).

## Response
Dad keeps speaking Spanish; accepts English answers and recasts.
No "say it in Spanish."

## Sources of need
Abuela video calls up from 1× to 3×/week, 20 min each, Spanish only (Abuela
has no English): +0.5 h. Saturday Spanish story-time at the library: +1.5 h. Spanish-speaking
babysitter (Lucía) Wednesday 15:30–18:30: +3 h. Spanish cartoons for
weekend screen time: +2 h. Sunday family dinner in Spanish (Mum is
learning): +1.5 h. Bogotá trip in July.
New total ≈ 20.5 h → 20.5 ÷ 91 = 22.5%; still short of 25%, so Dad
takes over bath and bedtime on the 2 nights Mum did them (+1 h) →
21.5 ÷ 91 ≈ 23.6%; the Bogotá trip in July will lift it further.

## Normal patterns we won't worry about
"Papi, can I have more *arepa*?" Mixing is fine.

## When to see an SLP
If Sofia has trouble being understood or learning words in **both**
languages compared with peers.

## 6-week trial (from 6 Oct)
Track weekly: Spanish hours; times Sofia uses Spanish unprompted (now ~0).
Review 17 Nov.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DS-01 Framework Application** — OPOL, MLAH, and time-and-place strategies with the harmonious-bilingual-development goal.
- **NE-11 Embedded Calculation Formulas** — heritage share = heritage hours ÷ waking hours, against a working threshold.
- **DP-06 Dominant Driver Identification** — need, effort, identity, conflict, or emotion behind refusal.
- **DP-11 Safe Experiment Design** — a 6-week trial with weekly exposure and spontaneous-use tracking.
