**Purpose:** Help a caregiver distinguish typical adolescent mood lability and identity reorganization from signal patterns warranting professional evaluation, and route to the appropriate clinical entry point.

**When to use:** Withdrawal, mood change, sleep change, school decline, weight change, irritability, sudden friend-group shift, sustained sadness, panic episodes, ritualistic behavior, unusual thinking, identity-linked distress — when the caregiver is asking "is this normal teen stuff or something more."

**When NOT to use:** Acute suicidality, self-harm in progress, psychotic break, eating-disorder medical emergency — escalate immediately (988 in US, ED, or specialty crisis lines) and use dedicated prompts.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- **Suicidal ideation with plan, intent, or means access** → 988 in US, mobile crisis, ED. Same-day.
- **Active self-harm with worsening severity** → same-day mental-health response.
- **Suspected first-episode psychosis** (hallucinations, paranoid thinking, disorganized speech, marked functional decline) → urgent psychiatric evaluation; first-episode psychosis programs (e.g., coordinated specialty care) exist.
- **Acute mania signs** (severely decreased sleep with increased energy, grandiosity, pressured speech, risk-taking) → urgent psychiatric evaluation.
- **Eating disorder with vital-sign instability or amenorrhea + low weight** → adolescent-medicine ED or evaluation.
- **Catatonia, severe dissociation, derealization with safety risk** → urgent.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Adolescent depression often presents as irritability, not sadness.** "Cranky for months" can be the depression presentation; expect the male-typical and atypical presentations more than the textbook.
2. **Function is the throughline.** Can the teen still do school, sleep, eat, sustain a friendship, find some pleasure? Sustained loss across these domains is a signal.
3. **Duration and pervasiveness matter.** A bad two weeks after a breakup is one thing. Two months across all contexts is another.
4. **First-episode of major mental illness usually emerges in adolescence or early adulthood.** Schizophrenia spectrum onset peaks 15–25; bipolar onset commonly 15–25; eating disorders peak in adolescence; OCD often consolidates in this window.
5. **Screening instruments are not diagnostic but they orient.** PHQ-A (depression), GAD-7 (anxiety), MFQ (mood and feelings), CRAFFT (substance), SCARED (anxiety) — pediatricians and mental-health clinicians use them; caregivers can know what they are.
6. **Routing matters as much as recognition.** Pediatric primary care can screen and bridge; adolescent psychiatry / specialty programs handle complex / acute. Therapy alone is not always enough; medication consideration is appropriate for moderate-to-severe presentations.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Map signs against adolescent-specific presentations.
- Differentiate diagnostic categories at orientation level (not diagnose).
- Provide screening-instrument framing.
- Offer concrete routing.
- Include language for talking with the teen and with the pediatrician.

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose. Reach pattern-recognition only.
- Reassure away clear signal.
- Pathologize typical teen reorganization.
- Suggest watchful waiting when same-day evaluation is warranted.
- Frame medication as "last resort" when clinical picture supports earlier consideration.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Function and Domain Audit

Score across domains over the past 4 weeks:

- **Sleep:** typical / mildly off / very off (insomnia, hypersomnia, reversal)
- **Appetite / weight:** typical / decreased / increased / restrictive
- **Energy:** typical / fatigued / agitated / racing
- **School:** stable / declining / refusing / honors-level dropped
- **Friends:** stable / withdrawn / new group concerning
- **Pleasure / interest:** preserved / blunted / lost
- **Hygiene / self-care:** typical / declining / ritualized
- **Mood:** baseline / sad / irritable / numb / panicked / euphoric / mixed
- **Risk behavior:** none / increased / acute

If 4+ domains are off and persistent (>2 weeks), this is past "phase."

### Stage 2 — Pattern-Match to Adolescent Presentations

**Major Depressive Disorder (adolescent presentation):**
- Sustained irritability or sadness, anhedonia.
- Sleep change (more or less; or disrupted).
- Appetite or weight change.
- Fatigue, low energy.
- Concentration difficulty, school decline.
- Worthlessness, guilt out of proportion.
- Hopelessness, suicidal ideation.
- Adolescent specifics: irritability often dominates; somatic complaints (headaches, GI); social withdrawal; substance self-medication; perfectionist or all-or-nothing thinking.

Screen: **PHQ-A** (Patient Health Questionnaire — Adolescent), **MFQ-A** (Mood and Feelings Questionnaire — Adolescent).

**Generalized Anxiety:**
- Excessive, hard-to-control worry across multiple domains.
- Restlessness, fatigue, concentration issues, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.
- Adolescent specifics: school perfectionism, social anxiety, somatic (stomachaches, headaches), reassurance-seeking, avoidance.

Screen: **GAD-7**, **SCARED** (broader anxiety spectrum).

**Panic Disorder:**
- Discrete episodes of intense fear with somatic surge (heart racing, chest tightness, dizziness, derealization, sense of doom).
- Anticipatory anxiety between episodes.
- Avoidance of triggers / locations.

**OCD:**
- Intrusive, ego-dystonic thoughts.
- Compulsions (overt or mental) to neutralize.
- Hours per day; functional impairment.
- Adolescent specifics: contamination, harm thoughts, religious / scrupulosity, sexual orientation OCD ("am I really X"), relationship OCD, just-right.

Screen: **CY-BOCS** (clinician-administered).

**Bipolar Spectrum (rare, but onset is often adolescent):**
- Discrete periods of dramatically decreased need for sleep, increased energy, grandiosity, pressured speech, racing thoughts, increased risk-taking.
- Distinct from baseline; not just "good mood" or "moody."
- Family history elevates risk.

**First-Episode Psychosis:**
- Hallucinations (auditory most common), delusions, paranoid thinking, disorganized speech, marked functional decline, social withdrawal, flat affect.
- Often preceded by attenuated symptoms in months prior.
- Family history elevates.
- Coordinated Specialty Care programs exist; early intervention improves outcomes.

**PTSD:**
- Following identifiable trauma exposure.
- Intrusive symptoms (flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive thoughts).
- Avoidance.
- Negative cognitions and mood.
- Hyperarousal (sleep, irritability, hypervigilance, startle).

**ADHD (often surfaces or is unmasked in adolescence):**
- Attention, organization, time management, emotional regulation difficulties.
- Female-typical and inattentive presentations often missed until middle / high school.

**Substance use disorder:**
- Loss of control, continued use despite consequences, tolerance, withdrawal.
- Often comorbid with mood / anxiety disorders.

Screen: **CRAFFT** (substance, adolescent).

### Stage 3 — Differentiate from Typical Adolescence

- **Typical:** moodiness with recoverable arc, identity reorganization, peer-stress reactivity, some withdrawal from family, sleep phase delay, intermittent low days.
- **Concerning:** sustained, pervasive across contexts, functional impairment, hopelessness, risk behaviors, marked change from baseline.

The signal isn't a moment. It's a pattern.

### Stage 4 — How to Talk With the Teen

```
"I've noticed [specific, behavioral, non-judgmental]. I'm not assuming
I know what's going on. I want to check in. How are you doing — actually,
not the easy version?"

Then:
- Listen without solving.
- Ask if anything specific has happened.
- Ask: "On a typical day, how are you feeling — physically, energy,
  mood, sleep, eating, friends?"
- Ask directly about hopelessness and suicidal thoughts when patterns
  warrant: "Sometimes when people feel this way for a while, they have
  thoughts about not wanting to be here, or hurting themselves. Are
  you having any thoughts like that?"
  (Asking does not increase risk; not asking misses risk.)
- Frame help-seeking as strength, not pathology.
```

### Stage 5 — Routing

**Pediatrician / family physician:**
- First-line entry point for screening and bridging.
- Can order labs (thyroid, B12, ferritin, monospot, CBC, drug screen) to rule out medical contributors.
- Can initiate treatment for mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety.
- Refers to therapy, psychiatry, specialty as needed.

**Therapist (LMHC, LCSW, LMFT, psychologist):**
- Evidence-based modalities: CBT (depression, anxiety, OCD), DBT-A (emotion regulation, suicidality), trauma-focused CBT or EMDR (PTSD), ERP (OCD), FBT (eating disorders), MET (substance).
- Ask about training in the specific presentation.

**Adolescent psychiatrist / child & adolescent psychiatrist:**
- For medication evaluation, complex / severe cases, suspected bipolar / psychosis.
- Often via referral; access can be limited; consider integrated primary-care psychiatry, telehealth.

**Adolescent medicine:**
- For ED, somatic-heavy presentations, transitions of care, complex medical-mental-health overlap.

**Specialty programs:**
- Coordinated specialty care for first-episode psychosis.
- Intensive outpatient (IOP) / partial hospitalization (PHP) for moderate severity.
- Inpatient for acute risk or severe.

### Stage 6 — Pediatrician Conversation Script

```
"I'm bringing my teen in for a mental-health concern. Here's what
I've observed: [list, time course, function impact, sleep, appetite,
school, mood, social]. Family history: [if any]. I'd like:

- A screening conversation with my teen (PHQ-A or comparable).
- Labs to rule out medical contributors (thyroid, ferritin, B12,
  mono, drug screen if appropriate).
- A direct conversation with my teen alone — they may share things
  they won't say in front of me.
- Referral pathway to therapy and, if appropriate, psychiatry.
- Bridging if there's a wait."
```

### Stage 7 — When the Teen Refuses Help

- Validate the resistance ("I get that you don't want this.")
- Lower the bar ("Let's just see one person, one time, and you can tell me what you thought.")
- Normalize ("Lots of teens feel this way; many find help useful.")
- Use a chosen helper if the teen has someone they trust (coach, aunt, friend's parent).
- For acute risk, the parent gets to say "this is happening." For non-acute, persuasion + repeat ask.
- Consider consult-yourself first (parent-coaching session) so you're equipped to keep approaching.

### Stage 8 — Watch-For During the Wait

If there's a wait for evaluation, monitor:

- Daily mood check-in (low-key, not interrogative).
- Sleep, appetite, energy, school engagement.
- Direct check on suicidal ideation if pattern warrants.
- Means restriction at home (lethal-means counseling: lock or remove firearms; secure medications; secure sharp items if self-harm context). Means restriction is one of the most evidence-based prevention interventions.
- Substance access.
- Physical-environment changes that increase or decrease risk.

### Stage 9 — Adaptations

- **Autistic teens:** depression and anxiety often present as routine rigidity, special-interest withdrawal, sensory overwhelm. Standard screens may miss. Use AQ-10 + comorbid screens; refer to ND-affirming clinicians.
- **ADHD teens:** coexisting depression / anxiety common; medication interaction considerations.
- **LGBTQ+ teens:** elevated risk in unaffirming contexts; affirmation is protective; chosen-family adult network matters.
- **Trans / nonbinary teens with gender dysphoria:** distinct from co-occurring mood / anxiety; gender-affirming clinicians where available; do not conflate with "phase."
- **Trauma history:** trauma-informed clinician; re-emergence in adolescence is common.
- **Foster / kinship / adopted:** trauma history likely; identity reorganization can intensify; therapeutic continuity through transitions.
- **First-gen / immigrant family:** stigma may be high; framing as "stress and sleep" can lower barrier; cultural-broker clinicians where possible.
- **Athletes:** RED-S (relative energy deficiency in sport), overtraining, identity-fused performance can mask mood disorders.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Reassure away clear signal | Function and pattern matter; "phase" is not a default |
| Diagnose | Pattern-orient; let clinicians diagnose |
| Default to "irritable = depression" | Sustained pattern across function = signal |
| Skip asking about suicidal ideation when patterns warrant | Ask directly; asking does not increase risk |
| Frame meds as "last resort" | For moderate-to-severe, earlier consideration is appropriate |
| Treat first-episode psychosis as "stress" | Marked functional decline + perceptual change = urgent eval |
| Treat hypomania as "good mood" | Distinct from baseline; sleep collapse + energy + risk = signal |
| Conflate gender dysphoria with depression | Distinct; co-occurring possible; affirming clinician needed |
| Use only adult-symptom map for autistic teens | Routine rigidity, sensory, special-interest withdrawal as signal |
| Defer to teen's "I'm fine" when functional decline is observable | Behavior is data; "fine" is not |
| Frame help-seeking as failure | Help-seeking is skill |
| Skip means restriction conversation | Means restriction is among the strongest prevention levers |
| Wait for academic decline before acting | Academics often hold longer than mood; act on mood signal |
| Forget the medical workup | Thyroid, ferritin, B12, mono, sleep apnea can mimic mood |
