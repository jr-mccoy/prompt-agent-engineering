**Purpose:** Help a caregiver repair the parent–teen relationship after a rupture — calibrated to adolescent development, slower than early-childhood repair, autonomy-respecting, and explicit about caregiver accountability where it applies.

**When to use:** Post-blowup; after caregiver yelled, said something cutting, lost control, broke a confidence, or overreacted; after the teen withdrew, slammed door, went silent, said something cutting; after a multi-day or multi-week cold war; after sustained estrangement; before launching a teen who left under strain; reconnecting after long silence; repairing after caregiver violated the teen's privacy.

**When NOT to use:** Acute safety concerns (suicidality, self-harm, abuse) — handle the safety issue first; return for repair work after stability. If the rupture involves caregiver physical violence toward the teen — the path is professional help and accountability beyond a repair conversation.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Teen estrangement + suicidal ideation → mental-health response.
- Teen estrangement + substance escalation → integrated evaluation.
- Sustained communication shutdown (weeks) without functional contact → family-therapy consideration.
- Caregiver pattern of cutting / shaming / threatening language during conflict → caregiver-side support (own therapy / parent coaching).

---

## Core Principles

1. **Repair is the foundation, not the finish.** Adolescents who have ruptures with caregivers and never see repair learn that ruptures are permanent. Adolescents who see repair learn that conflict can be survived.
2. **The 4–8 repair pattern doesn't transfer cleanly.** Toddler repair is fast (minutes to hours). Adolescent repair is slow (hours to days, sometimes weeks). The teen needs space and the timing of the approach.
3. **Pace from the teen, not from the caregiver's discomfort.** The caregiver's urgency to fix it can intrude on the teen's need to process. Patience is the work.
4. **Accountability is the differentiator.** A clean apology — without justification, without explanation, without dragging the teen back in — repairs more than a hundred reasonable explanations.
5. **Repair is not negotiation.** Don't repackage the operational disagreement as part of the repair. Repair is for the relationship; negotiation is a separate conversation.
6. **The relationship is more important than being right.** Teens reading "you have to admit you were wrong before I'll talk to you" should be met with "you're right, I was wrong" when that's true. Insisting on parental rightness during repair fails repair.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Distinguish repair from re-litigation.
- Use clean-apology structure where caregiver overreached.
- Pace from the teen.
- Address sustained estrangement explicitly when relevant.
- Surface co-parenting alignment.
- Provide both the immediate-after-blowup script and the long-cold-war script.

**Must Not:**
- Conflate repair with operational negotiation ("I'll apologize if you agree to my rule").
- Use over-apology / fake apology / JADE (Justify, Argue, Defend, Explain).
- Force the teen to accept the apology on the caregiver's timeline.
- Skip caregiver accountability when it applies.
- Use repair attempts to lecture.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Diagnose the Rupture

Before attempting repair, name what actually happened.

**Caregiver-side contributors:**
- I yelled.
- I said something cutting (specific words).
- I shamed the teen.
- I broke confidence (told someone something private).
- I overreacted to the situation.
- I took something personally that wasn't.
- I projected my own anxiety / history onto the teen.
- I refused to listen.
- I imposed without dialogue.
- I violated privacy (read texts, journal, etc.).
- I weaponized something the teen had told me.
- I matched the teen's intensity instead of holding ground.

**Teen-side contributors:**
- They said something cutting.
- They violated an agreement.
- They lied.
- They refused to communicate.
- They withdrew.
- They escalated.
- They acted in a way that scared me.

**Mutual contributors:**
- We were both flooded.
- We were both running on too little sleep.
- We were in a long-running pattern.
- We were caught in a third-party trigger (school, partner, friend, money).

Name the caregiver-side honestly. This is the basis of clean apology.

### Stage 2 — The Pacing

Adolescent repair pacing:

- **Same hour:** rarely the right window for sustained conversation. Brief acknowledgment of presence is okay ("I'm here when you want to talk; no rush").
- **Same day:** sometimes possible for short pieces — bringing food, sitting nearby, offering a non-pressuring presence.
- **Within a few days:** the substantive conversation, when both parties are regulated.
- **A week or more:** still appropriate for sustained ruptures; don't force.

Signs the teen is ready:
- Coming into shared space voluntarily.
- Initiating low-stakes contact.
- Body language softer.
- Direct or indirect bid for connection.

Signs the teen is not ready:
- Walking away when you enter.
- Silence sustained.
- Curt single-word responses.
- Door closed.

### Stage 3 — The Brief Acknowledgment (First Window)

```
[Whether minutes or a few hours after.]

"I'm not going to push you to talk now. I just want to say — I
saw what happened. I want to talk when you want to. No rush.
I love you."

[Then leave the space. Don't wait for response.]
```

Variations:
- Text instead of in-person if that lands better.
- Note slipped under door.
- Brief presence (sitting nearby, doing something parallel).

The point: signal availability without imposing presence.

### Stage 4 — The Substantive Repair Conversation

When the teen has signaled some readiness:

```
"Thanks for talking with me.

I want to start with what I did wrong. [Specific, not generic.]

I yelled at you. I said [specific words]. I shouldn't have.
That wasn't about you, that was about me being [tired / overwhelmed
/ scared / angry at myself / something else]. None of that makes
it okay.

I'm sorry.

I'm not going to ask you to forgive me on the spot. I just want
you to know that I know.

[Pause. Let them respond. Or not.]

Is there anything you want to say to me?

[Listen without defending. Even if they say cutting things back.
This is their turn.]

[After they've finished:]

I hear you. [Reflect what they said.]

What I want from here: [your goal — reconnection, repair, working
through the operational thing later, some specific relationship
move]. I'm open to your version of that too.

[If operational issue is part of the rupture:]

I want to talk about [the rule / the situation], but not right
now. We can pick that up tomorrow / later this week. The
conversation about us is what matters most right now."
```

### Stage 5 — Clean Apology vs. The Patterns to Avoid

**Clean apology:**
- Specific (what I did).
- Acknowledging impact (not "I'm sorry if you felt").
- Without justification ("I shouldn't have, full stop").
- Without "but you also."
- Pause for response.

**Over-apology:**
- "I'm such a terrible parent." Pulls the teen into reassuring you.
- Repeated, hand-wringing apologies. Become noise.

**Fake apology:**
- "I'm sorry that you felt hurt." (You're not sorry; you're framing it as their feeling.)
- "I'm sorry, but you have to understand…" (cancels itself.)

**JADE (Justify, Argue, Defend, Explain):**
- "I yelled because you were yelling."
- "Look, I had a really hard day at work, and you came home and…"
- Each of these reverses the apology and re-litigates.

The clean apology takes 30 seconds. JADE takes 30 minutes.

### Stage 6 — When the Teen Was Cutting

If the teen also said cutting things:

```
"I want to come back to something you said. You told me [specific].
I want you to know that landed hard. I'm not asking for an apology
right now. I'm asking — was that what you meant, or was that
heat-of-the-moment?

[Listen.]

I can hear when you're angry. I can hear hard things from you.
What I can't do is pretend we said nothing. So when you're ready,
I'd like to hear what you actually meant."
```

The caregiver doesn't have to absorb everything. Modeling that hard things spoken in heat get re-examined is part of the repair.

### Stage 7 — When You Overreached on Autonomy

Common pattern: caregiver intrudes on teen autonomy (read texts, intervened with friend group, called school without telling, made a decision unilaterally).

```
"I want to talk about what I did. I [specific overreach]. I told
myself it was because [my reason]. Maybe that was true and maybe
it was me being scared. Either way — that wasn't my decision to
make alone, and it wasn't the way to handle it.

I'm sorry.

Going forward: [specific commitment about how you'll handle that
domain]. I'm telling you what I'll do, not asking you to forgive
me for what I did.

If you want to tell me what landed worst about it, I want to
hear."
```

### Stage 8 — Long Cold War / Sustained Estrangement

If days or weeks have passed:

- Don't pretend it didn't happen by walking in cheerful.
- Don't escalate by demanding engagement.
- Stay available, low-pressure.
- Find low-stakes shared activity (driving, cooking, walking the dog) where conversation can happen alongside doing something.
- Use written communication if in-person is too intense (a note, a text, a card).
- Consider a third-party convener — family therapist — if the estrangement is structural and recurring.
- Don't over-rely on co-parent as messenger.

For very long estrangement:

```
"I know it's been a long time since we really talked. I'm not
trying to fix it in one conversation. I want to start by saying:
I miss you. I miss us. I'm sorry for what I did. I'm willing to
work on this for as long as it takes.

I'm not asking for an answer now. I'll be here."
```

### Stage 9 — When Repair Doesn't Land

Sometimes the teen isn't ready. Sometimes they need time. Sometimes they need a different relationship structure (more space, less contact, different topics off-limits for now).

- Don't force.
- Stay present without intruding.
- Repair what you can in your behavior; the teen will see this.
- Address the structural pieces (do you have your own therapist? are you running yourself empty? is the household environment contributing?).
- Family therapy for sustained patterns — particularly when the rupture is recurring.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations

- **Autistic teens:** literal, predictable repair — not metaphor; concrete language; processing time may be longer; written may land better than verbal.
- **ADHD teens:** time-blindness affects "I'll talk later"; gentle re-bid may help; emotional flooding is faster.
- **Anxious teens:** repair can re-trigger anxiety; calm presence; reassurance about the relationship surviving.
- **Trauma history:** rupture-and-repair is core trauma-informed work; therapeutic support helpful.
- **LGBTQ+ teens, especially after caregiver mishandled identity:** affirming explicit accountability; the rupture from identity-rejection is structural; specialist support.
- **Teen with substance / mental-health context:** repair is part of treatment plan; therapist can help structure.
- **Co-parented household:** alignment matters; one parent's repair undermined by another's continued criticism.
- **Foster / adopted teens:** rupture lands into earlier rupture history; pacing extra-slow; specialist input.
- **Cultural context:** apology norms vary; some families don't have a repair tradition; introducing one is the work.
- **Caregiver own history:** caregivers who didn't experience repair from their own parents often struggle to do this; own therapy / parent coaching helps.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Use over-apology that pulls teen into reassuring you | Clean, specific, contained apology |
| Use fake apology ("sorry you felt") | Take responsibility for action / impact |
| JADE (justify, argue, defend, explain) | Apology stops at the apology |
| Force the teen to accept on your timeline | Pace from teen |
| Conflate repair with operational negotiation | Separate the two conversations |
| Demand the teen apologize first | Caregiver going first models the move |
| Lecture during repair | Repair is not a teaching moment |
| Use repair to relitigate the rule | Different conversation |
| Walk in cheerful pretending nothing happened | Acknowledge the rupture |
| Refuse to apologize because "they were wrong too" | Both can be true; apologize for your part |
| Use co-parent as messenger | Direct repair |
| Repeat the apology repeatedly until teen responds | Becomes pressure / noise |
| Skip caregiver self-care / therapy when pattern is recurring | Pattern needs structural work |
| Default to family therapy as failure | Family therapy is appropriate, not shameful |
| Insist on full reconciliation when teen needs space | Space is part of repair |
| Treat one repair as fixing a long pattern | Repair is ongoing maintenance |
