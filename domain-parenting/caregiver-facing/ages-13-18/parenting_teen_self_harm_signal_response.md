**Purpose:** Help a caregiver respond to discovered or disclosed self-harm in a teenager — calmly, function-aware, with appropriate clinical routing, safety planning, and means restriction.

**When to use:** Discovery of cuts, burns, or other self-injury; teen disclosure of self-harm; suspected self-harm based on behavior (long sleeves in summer, hidden bandages, refusal of pool / locker room); after a known episode; setting up a safety plan; supporting a teen already in treatment.

**When NOT to use:** Acute suicidal crisis with plan / intent / means → 988 (US), mobile crisis, or ED. Severe wound requiring sutures or with infection signs → urgent care or ED. Active self-injury in progress → safety first, then return to this prompt.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- **Suicidal ideation with plan, intent, or means** → 988 in US, mobile crisis team, or ED. Same-day.
- **Wound requiring sutures, deep, or infected** → urgent care or ED.
- **Self-injury escalating in frequency or severity** → mental-health response within days, not weeks.
- **Self-injury + suicidality** → both addressed; do not assume one substitutes for the other.
- **Self-injury + active eating disorder** → coordinated care; functional overlap common.
- **Severe dissociation during self-injury** → trauma-focused clinician.
- **Self-injury with deteriorating function (school, sleep, interest)** → mood-disorder evaluation.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Self-injury is most often a coping behavior, not a suicide attempt.** It usually functions to regulate intolerable emotion (relief), to feel something when numb, to express what cannot be said, to punish, or to communicate distress. It serves a function. Removing the behavior without addressing the function leads to substitution.
2. **NSSI and suicidality can co-occur.** A history of NSSI elevates suicide risk. Always assess both. Don't assume one rules out the other.
3. **Reaction matters.** Caregiver disgust, panic, anger, or excessive emotion can intensify shame and drive the behavior underground. The first response shapes whether the teen tells you next time.
4. **Means restriction is evidence-based.** Reducing access to preferred method (sharps, pills, ligature, ignition source) reduces frequency and severity. This is true for NSSI and for suicidality.
5. **Treatment exists and works.** DBT-A, CBT, and other modalities have strong evidence for NSSI in adolescents. Therapy is the lever; consequences are not.
6. **The relationship is the safety net.** Teens who can talk about self-injury with a caregiver have better outcomes than those who can't.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Differentiate NSSI from suicidality while assessing both.
- Provide first-response language that doesn't shame.
- Address function (what is the behavior doing for the teen?).
- Build a safety plan grounded in Stanley-Brown structure.
- Apply means restriction at home.
- Provide clinical routing.

**Must Not:**
- Treat NSSI as attention-seeking ("for attention" is a function; the function is real distress).
- Force the teen to "promise not to do it again" as the primary intervention.
- Use ultimatums or contracts as substitute for treatment.
- Skip the suicidality assessment.
- Confiscate all sharp objects in the home as the only intervention without addressing function.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Differentiate NSSI from Suicidality

NSSI: intent is not to die. Function is regulation, expression, or punishment. Frequency can be high.
Suicide attempt: intent is to die (or behavior with high risk and ambivalent intent). Lethality usually higher.

Ask directly. The Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) framing is useful:
- "When you do this, are you trying to end your life, or trying to feel better / different?"
- "Do you have thoughts of wanting to die or not be here?"
- "Have you had thoughts of how you might do it?"
- "Have you had any intention of acting on those thoughts?"
- "Have you ever attempted to take your own life?"

Both can co-exist. Treat both.

### Stage 2 — The First Response

When you discover or are told:

```
[Internal first: take a breath. Do not match panic to discovery.]

[If wound visible and active, treat the wound calmly first. Clean,
bandage, decide if medical care needed. Then talk.]

"Thank you for telling me." (If disclosed.)
"I want to talk about what I saw." (If discovered.)

"I'm not going to scream. I'm not going to punish you. I want to
understand what's going on for you.

Tell me about it. When did this start? What's it doing for you?
What feeling is it trying to change?

[Listen. Don't interrupt with solutions. Don't moralize.]

I want to ask you something, and I want a real answer: when you
do this, are you trying to die, or trying to feel different?

[Listen.]

Do you have thoughts of not wanting to be here? Of ending your
life?

[Listen.]

[If yes to suicidality: shift to mental-health response prompt
and 988 / crisis pathway.]

Here's what I want us to do:
- We're going to find someone who knows this work.
- I want you to know I'm here. You can tell me when it's
  happening, when you want to, when you didn't, when you did.
- I'm not going to lock everything up like you're a problem. I
  am going to make some changes at home so the access piece is
  different.
- This is hard. We're in it together."
```

### Stage 3 — Function Analysis (What Is It Doing?)

Self-injury commonly functions in one or more ways:

- **Affect regulation:** intolerable emotion → relief.
- **Anti-dissociation:** numbness → feeling something.
- **Self-punishment:** I-am-bad → external evidence.
- **Communication / expression:** can't-say → can-show.
- **Identity / community:** belonging to a group / aesthetic.
- **Compulsion / ritual:** OCD-spectrum drive.
- **Trauma response:** re-enactment, dissociative.

Once you understand function (with the teen), you can think about replacement skills that serve the same function. This is core to DBT-informed work.

### Stage 4 — The Stanley-Brown Safety Plan

Six-step safety plan. Co-create with the teen.

**Step 1: Warning signs.**
- "What are the early signs that you're heading toward an episode?" (Sleep change, fight, specific person, specific app, specific time of day, body sensations, specific thought.)

**Step 2: Internal coping strategies.**
- "What can you do in the moment, by yourself, that helps without using self-injury?" (Walk, cold water on face, music, drawing, writing, breath, grounding, ice cubes, intense exercise, shower.)

**Step 3: Social distraction.**
- "Who or what can you contact for distraction (not necessarily talking about it)?" (Friend, sibling, online community, gaming, group chat.)

**Step 4: Social support / talking to someone.**
- "Who can you tell when you're in it?" (Parent, friend, therapist, sibling, specific adult.)

**Step 5: Professionals and crisis lines.**
- Therapist's name and number.
- Crisis: 988 (call or text) in US.
- Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741 in US.
- Local mobile crisis team / pediatric ED.

**Step 6: Means restriction.**
- "What will we do at home to make access harder?"

Print or save the plan. Teen has access. Caregiver has access. Therapist has access if in treatment.

### Stage 5 — Means Restriction at Home

Means restriction is one of the most evidence-based interventions for both NSSI and suicidality.

For NSSI:
- Identify preferred method.
- Reduce access without total surveillance: sharps in a locked drawer, lighters away, specific items removed from the bedroom.
- Don't strip the home of every potentially-injurious object — that's neither possible nor therapeutic. Reduce easy access.

For suicidality (if also present):
- **Firearms:** locked, separate ammunition, off-premises during high-risk period if possible.
- **Medications:** all medications (prescription and OTC) locked or removed from the bedroom; especially acetaminophen, antidepressants, opioids.
- **Sharps:** locked.
- **Ligature points / cords:** awareness; not always actionable but consider in bedroom.
- **Vehicle keys:** if relevant.

Frame the restriction as "we're making the easy access not easy" rather than as "we don't trust you." Teens often understand this when explained.

### Stage 6 — Replacement Skills (DBT-Informed)

Common replacement skills, by function:

For affect regulation / "I need release":
- Cold-water plunge, ice on wrists, hold ice in hands.
- Intense aerobic exercise (running, jumping jacks, push-ups to failure).
- Loud music + movement.
- Tearing paper, hitting pillows.
- TIPP skill (Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Paired muscle relaxation).

For anti-dissociation / "I need to feel":
- Strong sensory: ice, cold water, intense flavor (sour candy), strong scent.
- Grounding (5-4-3-2-1 senses).

For self-punishment / "I am bad":
- Compassion-focused practices.
- Writing the punishing thought, then a counter-thought.
- Therapeutic work on shame.

For communication / "I can't say it":
- Write it instead. Draw it. Voice memo.
- Pre-arranged signal to caregiver: "I'm having that day."

For ritual / OCD-spectrum:
- ERP-trained clinician.

For identity / community:
- Address with therapist; alternate-belonging work.

These are bridges, not substitutes for treatment.

### Stage 7 — Clinical Routing

- **Therapist trained in DBT-A or CBT for NSSI.** Ask: "What's your training in adolescent NSSI?"
- **Comprehensive DBT-A program** if available; gold standard for chronic NSSI + emotion dysregulation.
- **Adolescent psychiatrist** if mood / anxiety co-occurring; SSRIs may be indicated for co-occurring depression / anxiety; medication for NSSI itself is limited but co-occurring conditions matter.
- **Adolescent medicine** for severe wound care, recurrent infection, scarring concerns.
- **IOP / PHP** for moderate severity or treatment escalation.
- **Inpatient** for acute suicidality (NSSI alone is rarely inpatient indication).
- **Family therapy** alongside individual work; family communication often part of the picture.

### Stage 8 — School Coordination

- Teen's preference: most teens want minimal school involvement.
- School counselor can be useful; balance against teen's privacy.
- 504 plan possible if academic functioning impacted.
- Bathroom-pass, nurse-visit options for in-the-moment regulation.
- Friend contagion: NSSI can spread in friend groups; school may be aware of cluster.

### Stage 9 — Caregiver Self-Care During This

This is hard. Discovering self-injury in your kid lands hard.

- Find your own support: therapist, NAMI Family-to-Family, friend.
- Don't manage your distress on the teen.
- Take the hour after an incident to regulate yourself.
- Sleep. Eat. Move. Don't run yourself empty.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations

- **Trauma history:** trauma-focused care (TF-CBT, EMDR, sometimes specific trauma-informed DBT); function often re-enactment or dissociative.
- **Autistic teens:** sensory-regulation function common; ND-affirming clinician; sensory toolkit.
- **ADHD teens:** impulsivity in episodes; medication consideration if co-occurring.
- **LGBTQ+ teens:** affirming clinician; family acceptance is a major protective factor.
- **Eating disorder co-occurrence:** address both; often functionally linked.
- **Substance co-occurrence:** combined treatment; substance use often functions similarly to NSSI.
- **Cultural context:** stigma around mental health may delay help-seeking; culturally-matched clinicians where possible.
- **Custody / split household:** consistent safety plan and means restriction across both homes.
- **Friend-group contagion:** the friend group has high overlap; school counselor and parent network as appropriate.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Frame NSSI as "for attention" | Function is real distress; even communicative function is signal |
| Punish or shame | Reaction shapes whether they tell you next time |
| Force a "I promise not to do it again" contract as primary intervention | Function-driven; promise without skill is empty |
| Confiscate every sharp in the house and call it done | Means restriction is one piece; address function |
| Skip the direct suicidality question | NSSI elevates suicide risk; assess directly |
| Match teen panic with caregiver panic | Calm, present, non-shaming |
| Rush to inpatient as default | Inpatient is for acute suicidality; NSSI alone often outpatient |
| Treat scars as moral judgment | Body autonomy + therapeutic processing of scars matters |
| Skip means restriction because "they could find another way" | Even modest reduction in access reduces frequency / severity |
| Use ultimatums | Treatment is the lever, not threats |
| Surveil text messages as primary intervention | Erodes trust; treatment is the lever |
| Frame replacement skills as substitutes that just work | Bridges; treatment underneath |
| Skip caregiver self-care | This is sustainable only with caregiver support |
| Treat school disclosure as required | Teen voice matters; balance |
| Conflate NSSI with attention-seeking when peer disclosure occurs | Function is real; assess each case |
| Skip the "you can tell me" repeat message | This is the safety net |
