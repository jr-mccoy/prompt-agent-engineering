**Purpose:** Help a caregiver recognize eating-disorder signals across the diagnostic spectrum, conduct a body-size-neutral assessment, route appropriately to adolescent medicine and ED-specialist care, and prepare for Family-Based Treatment (FBT/Maudsley) if anorexia-spectrum.

**When to use:** Restriction observed; rapid weight loss; weight stagnation during expected growth; food rituals; binging; purging (vomiting, laxatives, exercise); body-image distress with behavior change; orthorexic patterns; ARFID-pattern restriction; suspected ED in athlete; concern about a male-typical body presentation; supporting a teen already in treatment.

**When NOT to use:** Vital-sign instability, severe electrolyte imbalance, syncope, bradycardia, suicidality with active intent → ED. Acute medical emergency overrides this prompt; return to it post-stabilization.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- **Vital-sign instability:** bradycardia (resting HR <50, or <40 in athletic teen), orthostatic vitals shift, hypothermia, hypotension → ED.
- **Severe electrolyte abnormality** (especially hypokalemia from purging) → ED.
- **Amenorrhea or menstrual irregularity in low-weight or restricting teen** → adolescent-medicine evaluation; sign of energy deficit.
- **Rapid weight loss** (>10% body weight in <6 months) → urgent eval.
- **Weight stagnation during pubertal growth period** → eval (often missed).
- **Refeeding syndrome risk** if severe restriction has been prolonged → medical management of refeeding.
- **Suicidality** + ED → integrated care; ED population has elevated suicide risk.
- **Type 1 diabetes + insulin manipulation** ("diabulimia") → urgent endocrine + ED specialist.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Eating disorders affect bodies of all sizes.** A teen does not need to be "underweight" to have a serious eating disorder. Atypical anorexia (full anorexia behavior in a normal-weight or larger body) is common and often missed.
2. **Behavior is the diagnosis, not weight.** Restriction, purging, binging, ritual, fear of weight gain, identity-fused with body — these are the signal. Weight is one data point.
3. **Earlier intervention dramatically improves outcomes.** Adolescent-onset ED with early treatment recovers much better than chronic adult ED.
4. **Family-Based Treatment (FBT/Maudsley) is first-line for adolescent anorexia and many adolescent EDs.** Caregivers are the agents of nutrition restoration. This is a structural shift from older "the parents caused it" framings.
5. **You can't see binge / purge / restriction by looking at the kid.** Signs are behavioral. Many ED kids look "fine."
6. **Athlete contexts mask EDs.** RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) is widely under-recognized. Coach culture can drive ED.
7. **Male-typical body and muscle dysmorphia presentations look different from textbook anorexia.** Restriction + extreme exercise + protein-obsession + lean-mass-fixation are core. Often missed.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Use body-size-neutral assessment.
- Pattern-match across the diagnostic spectrum.
- Route to adolescent medicine + ED specialist (not generic therapist alone).
- Prepare for FBT framing if anorexia-spectrum.
- Surface male-typical and athlete presentations explicitly.
- Address weight-talk and food-talk in the home.

**Must Not:**
- Use BMI as the only criterion.
- Use weight-based reassurance ("you don't look anorexic to me").
- Comment on bodies (teen's, others', or your own) during the conversation.
- Default to "let her work it out" with restriction patterns.
- Recommend dieting / "healthier eating" / specific food restrictions.
- Treat the teen as the manager of their refeeding (this is the FBT shift).

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Pattern Match Across the ED Spectrum

**Anorexia Nervosa:**
- Restriction of intake leading to significantly low body weight in context of age, sex, developmental trajectory, and physical health.
- Intense fear of weight gain or persistent behavior interfering with weight gain.
- Disturbance in body experience or undue influence of body weight on self-evaluation.
- Restricting subtype vs. binge-eating/purging subtype.

**Atypical Anorexia:**
- All criteria for anorexia except body weight is in normal or above-normal range.
- Just as medically dangerous as anorexia despite weight; widely under-diagnosed.

**Bulimia Nervosa:**
- Recurrent binge episodes (loss of control + objectively large amount).
- Recurrent compensatory behaviors (vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, excessive exercise, fasting).
- Self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight.
- Often normal-weight or above; visible signs subtle (parotid gland swelling, callused knuckles, dental erosion).

**Binge Eating Disorder:**
- Recurrent binge episodes without regular compensatory behavior.
- Distress, eating alone, eating to discomfort, eating without hunger.
- Often associated with shame.

**ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder):**
- Restriction without body-image disturbance.
- Drivers: sensory aversion (texture, color, smell), fear of aversive consequence (choking, vomiting), low interest in eating.
- Often co-occurs with autism, anxiety, GI conditions.
- Often presents earlier than other EDs; can persist or worsen in adolescence.

**OSFED (Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder):**
- Doesn't meet full criteria but clinically significant.
- Includes atypical anorexia, sub-threshold bulimia, sub-threshold BED, purging disorder, night eating syndrome.

**Orthorexia (not a formal DSM diagnosis but clinically real):**
- Obsessive focus on "clean" / "pure" / "healthy" eating.
- Functional impairment from food rules.
- Identity fused with eating philosophy.

**Muscle dysmorphia / "bigorexia":**
- Preoccupation with leanness and muscularity.
- Restriction + extreme protein focus + extreme exercise.
- Often male-typical bodies.
- Steroid / SARM use risk.

### Stage 2 — Body-Size-Neutral Assessment

Score across:

**Behavioral signs:**
- Restriction (skipping meals, cutting food small, eating very slowly, eating alone).
- Food rules (no fat, no carb, no sugar, no after X PM, no eating in front of others).
- Compensatory behavior (vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, excessive exercise).
- Binging (loss of control, large amounts, hidden).
- Ritual (specific order, specific cutting, specific arrangement, weighing food).
- Weighing self (frequency, mood-after).
- Mirror checking / body-checking.
- App tracking (calorie, macro, exercise).

**Cognitive / emotional signs:**
- Body-image distress.
- Self-evaluation tied to body.
- Fear of specific foods.
- Fear of weight gain.
- Mood crash after eating "off-rule."
- Identity fused with eating philosophy.

**Physical signs (do not require to act):**
- Weight loss or weight stagnation during growth.
- Hair thinning, hair on face / body (lanugo).
- Cold intolerance, hypothermia.
- Dizziness, syncope, fatigue.
- Amenorrhea or menstrual irregularity.
- Bradycardia.
- Parotid swelling, dental erosion (purging).
- Callused knuckles (Russell's sign, purging).
- GI complaints.
- Low energy in workouts.

**Social signs:**
- Withdrawal from food-involving social contexts.
- New "interest" in cooking for others (often without eating).
- Friend group change to other ED-vulnerable peers.
- ED content in social-media feed.

If 4+ behavioral signs are present, this is past "phase."

### Stage 3 — The First Conversation

```
[Choose a time when you are not flooded. Not at a meal. Not after
a fight. Not at night.]

"I want to talk about something I've been noticing. I'm not going
to comment on your body. I'm not going to tell you what to eat.
I want to talk about behavior I've noticed.

[Specific behavioral observations, not judgments.]

I notice you've been skipping breakfast.
I notice you eat lunch alone.
I notice meals have gotten more rule-based.
I notice you've been working out twice a day.
I notice you've stopped eating dessert with us.
I notice the bathroom door closes after you eat.

I'm worried about what these patterns can become — even if you're
not in trouble yet. Eating disorders are sneakier than people
realize. They start with rules that feel reasonable.

[Listen.]

Here's what I want us to do:
- We see your pediatrician this week. Not to weigh and shame —
  to look at the whole picture.
- We talk to someone who knows eating-disorder work in teens
  specifically.
- I'm not your judge here. I'm on your team.
- I won't make food a fight. We'll figure out what's needed."
```

### Stage 4 — Routing (This Is Specialist Care)

Generic therapist or generic pediatrician is often not enough. Look for:

- **Adolescent medicine** physician or **pediatrician with ED experience.**
- **ED-specialist therapist** trained in FBT (Maudsley) for adolescent anorexia, or CBT-E for bulimia / BED, or specialized ARFID treatment.
- **Registered dietitian** trained in ED (Health-At-Every-Size aligned, ND-aware).
- **Adolescent psychiatrist** if co-occurring depression, anxiety, OCD, or for severe cases.
- **Higher level of care** as needed: outpatient → IOP → PHP → residential → inpatient.
- **Inpatient medical** for vital-sign instability, electrolyte abnormality, refeeding-syndrome risk.

Resources:
- **NEDA** (National Eating Disorders Association): nationaleatingdisorders.org; helpline.
- **F.E.A.S.T.** (Families Empowered and Supporting Treatment of Eating Disorders): feast-ed.org; specifically for caregivers of teens with ED.
- **Project HEAL:** financial assistance and access to ED treatment.

### Stage 5 — Pre-Loading Family-Based Treatment (FBT / Maudsley)

If anorexia-spectrum and FBT recommended:

```
"FBT (Maudsley) is the most evidence-based treatment for adolescent
anorexia. The core idea:

- The eating disorder is the enemy. The teen is not the enemy.
- The teen, while in the eating disorder, cannot be in charge of
  refeeding.
- The parents take over food decisions in the early phase. This
  is hard, both for the teen and the parents.
- This isn't punishment, and it isn't because the parents caused
  the ED. It's because the eating disorder is loud, and the teen
  needs the family's structure to push through it.
- Therapy supports the family doing this work. The whole family
  is the unit of treatment.
- Phase 1: full nutrition restoration with parental control of
  meals.
- Phase 2: graduated handover of food responsibility back to the
  teen.
- Phase 3: addressing other adolescent issues, including the
  identity work the ED was sometimes filling in for.

This is going to be hard. Meals will be intense. The teen will
sometimes feel like a different person. The therapist coaches
you through it."
```

### Stage 6 — Home Environment Adjustments

- **Stop diet talk.** Yours, your partner's, the household's.
- **Stop body comments.** Even positive ones. Especially about other people's bodies.
- **Stop "good food / bad food" framing.** All foods fit.
- **Stop labeled-food-aesthetic discussions.** "Clean," "pure," "healthy" framing fuels orthorexic patterns.
- **Eat together where possible.**
- **Don't comment on the teen's plate.**
- **Remove the bathroom-after-meals access** if purging is the pattern (door open, supervised window after meals — this is FBT-aligned).
- **Reduce body-image-heavy social-media exposure.**
- **Eliminate scales** at home if relevant; clinical weighing only.
- **Don't bargain or threaten with food.**

### Stage 7 — Athlete-Specific Adaptations

If teen is athlete:

- RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) — energy intake insufficient for sport demands; affects bone density, hormones, immune, performance.
- Coach culture: weigh-ins, weight cuts, "lean for performance" framing — the system itself can drive ED.
- Sports especially associated: distance running, gymnastics, dance, wrestling, cycling, rowing, figure skating, weight-class martial arts, cross-country.
- Sport may need to be paused during nutritional restoration. This is appropriate; explain to coach (with specialist guidance).
- Talk to athletic trainer / team physician if available.
- Some athletes recover within sport; some need to leave the sport to recover.

### Stage 8 — Male-Typical Body / Muscle Dysmorphia Adaptations

- Often missed because doesn't match "anorexic" stereotype.
- Behaviors: protein obsession, supplement use, gym every day, body-checking in mirror, fixation on lean-mass / fat percentage, restriction disguised as "cutting," steroid / SARM consideration.
- Apps tracking macros, body-fat percentage, training metrics.
- Body-image content (manosphere, bodybuilding) reinforces.
- Conversation: same body-size-neutral approach; specifically address the lean-mass-fixation.
- Specialist who knows male-typical-body ED presentations.

### Stage 9 — ARFID-Specific Considerations

- Not a body-image disorder.
- Restriction by sensory, fear, or low interest.
- Often starts early; persists.
- Co-occurring autism, anxiety, OCD common.
- Treatment: specialized feeding therapy (ARFID-trained); often slower trajectory than other EDs; SLP / OT involvement common.
- Caregiver role: gentle exposure, reduced battle-at-meals, supportive structure.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations Across Profiles

- **Autistic teens:** ARFID-presentations more common; sensory adaptations matter; ND-affirming clinician.
- **Trans / nonbinary teens:** body dysphoria can interact with body-image disturbance; gender-affirming + ED-specialist care.
- **Type 1 diabetes:** "diabulimia" (insulin manipulation for weight) is high-risk; endocrine + ED specialist.
- **Trauma history:** ED often functions as control or numbing in trauma; trauma-informed care.
- **OCD co-occurrence:** ritual-heavy ED; ERP-trained clinician.
- **Cultural context:** ED is not just a "white girl thing"; missed in BIPOC teens at higher rates; weight-and-food-cultural-context matters.
- **Lower-SES family:** treatment access is a real barrier; F.E.A.S.T. and Project HEAL resources.
- **Single-parent / split-custody:** FBT requires household coordination; both households need to be aligned for refeeding to work.
- **Sibling effects:** other kids in the household watching; brief, age-appropriate framing for siblings.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Use BMI / weight as the only criterion | Behavior is the diagnosis; atypical anorexia is dangerous |
| Reassure away with "you don't look anorexic" | Behavior matters; don't comment on bodies |
| Recommend "healthier eating" or any restriction | This fuels ED; specialist care, not advice |
| Default to generic therapist | ED specialist; FBT / CBT-E / ARFID-trained |
| Treat the teen as manager of refeeding | FBT structure: parents take over in phase 1 |
| Skip athlete-context conversation | Sport may need pause; coach culture often part of driver |
| Miss male-typical body / muscle dysmorphia presentation | Lean-mass / protein / extreme-exercise fixation = ED signal |
| Conflate ARFID with anorexia | ARFID is not body-image-driven; treatment differs |
| Comment on teen's plate | Fuels the ED's voice; trust the structure |
| Comment on other people's bodies in the home | Body-talk culture is the soil ED grows in |
| Treat menstrual cessation as "just stress" | Energy deficit signal; eval |
| Wait for medical instability before acting | Earlier intervention >> later |
| Treat eating in front of others as the goal | Function and nutrition restoration are the goals |
| Default to "she'll work it out" with restriction | Untreated ED kills; second-leading mortality among psych conditions for adolescents |
| Skip the FBT framing because it's hard | Hard, evidence-based, and recoverable |
| Skip caregiver support (F.E.A.S.T., NAMI) | Caregiver capacity is part of the treatment |
