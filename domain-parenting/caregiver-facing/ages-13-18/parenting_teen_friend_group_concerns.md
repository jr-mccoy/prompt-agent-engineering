**Purpose:** Help a caregiver respond to a concerning friend-group situation in a way that addresses real risk without forbidding (which usually backfires), keeps communication open, and engages the structural levers — adult network, transportation, time-and-space, identity-direction work — that actually shift outcomes.

**When to use:** New friend group; observed substance use in friend group; risk-taking behaviors; exclusionary or cruel friend-group dynamics; teen's identity / values appearing to shift in concerning direction; perceived radicalization (manosphere, white-nationalist, conspiracy, extremist); abusive friendship; older friend group; online-only friend group concerns.

**When NOT to use:** Acute safety risk (predator, gang involvement, exploitation, dating violence) — escalate to safety-action steps and law-enforcement / specialist consultation if relevant. Suspected friend in active suicidal crisis — that's a separate intervention.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Friend in suicidal crisis affecting teen → mental-health support for teen.
- Friend group with substance escalation including hard substances → adolescent SUD evaluation for teen.
- Friend group involved in criminal activity → safety planning + legal counsel.
- Teen radicalization toward specific violent ideation → mental-health response + law-enforcement consultation if specific threat.
- Teen in friendship-as-coercive-control pattern → see dating-consent prompt; same dynamics apply to non-romantic friendships.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Forbidding the friend group rarely works and often makes it worse.** Teens go underground, and the caregiver loses the information channel. Influence boundary, not isolation, is the lever.
2. **Distinguish "I don't like them" from "they're harming my teen."** Caregiver discomfort with style, identity, or class is different from observable harm. Separate them.
3. **Friend groups serve developmental functions.** Belonging, identity, autonomy, identity-direction. The work is sometimes substituting better-fitting forms of those functions, not eliminating the group.
4. **The adult network is the structural lever.** Other parents, coaches, mentors, neighbors. Teens whose adults know each other have more guardrails by default.
5. **Identity-direction work matters more than friend-group control.** A teen consolidating their own values is harder to pull into harmful directions; a teen without identity is easier to pull anywhere.
6. **There are real cases where forbidding is right.** Adult predator, abusive friend, criminal activity, identity-rejection by family member's friend. They're rarer than they feel.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Distinguish discomfort from observable harm.
- Use influence-boundary as primary lever.
- Address the underneath function the group is serving.
- Engage adult-network if accessible.
- Reserve forbidding for specific threshold cases.
- Provide language for talking with the teen and (sometimes) with the friend group.

**Must Not:**
- Default to forbidding as first move.
- Pathologize the friend group on the basis of style / identity / class.
- Treat the teen as the only thing that needs to change.
- Ignore real harm because "they need to choose their own friends."
- Frame all influence as bad.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Distinguish Discomfort from Harm

Audit the concern:

**Caregiver-discomfort drivers (not necessarily harm):**
- Style: clothing, hair, music, aesthetic.
- Class: socioeconomic difference.
- Identity: queerness, gender expression, religion, race, neurodivergence.
- Personality: louder, quieter, more social, more withdrawn than caregiver expects.
- Age: slightly older (1–2 years).
- Subculture: skater, gamer, theater, alt, religious, etc.

**Real-harm drivers (require response):**
- Substance escalation in the group.
- Risk-taking with safety (driving impaired, weapons, dangerous activity).
- Cruelty pattern within the group (toward each other, toward outsiders).
- Coercive-control dynamic (group isolating teen, pressuring against family, pressuring against other friends).
- Identity-erasure of the teen by group.
- Radicalization vector (specific harmful ideology).
- Significant age gap (3+ years; teen + adult is grooming, not friendship).
- Criminal activity.

If the concern is mostly discomfort: do the caregiver-side work; don't act on the friend group.
If real-harm: this prompt's main territory.

### Stage 2 — The Function the Group Is Serving

What is the teen getting from this group?

- Belonging where they didn't have it before.
- Identity — being seen as a kind of person.
- Romantic / sexual proximity.
- Acceptance for an identity (queer community, specific subculture).
- Safety (group as protection in a hostile school).
- Autonomy — first group not chosen by parents.
- Stimulation / risk / novelty.
- Mentorship from older teens.
- Meaning / purpose.
- Avoidance of family or school.

Naming this honestly shapes what intervention can substitute or supplement.

### Stage 3 — The Direct Conversation With the Teen

```
"I want to talk about [the group] — not to forbid you from
seeing them, but because I'm noticing some things and I want
to understand.

[Specific, behavioral observations:]
I notice you've been coming home different.
I notice your money / time / sleep is different.
I notice you've stopped seeing [previous friend] who used to
matter to you.
I notice [substance smell / vape / mood pattern].
I notice you've been quieter about what you're up to.

I want to understand what these friends are about. What do you
like about them? What do they get? What do you do together?

[Listen without prosecuting.]

Here's what I'm worried about: [specific, not catastrophizing].

[Listen.]

I'm not telling you who to be friends with. I am telling you
what I see and what concerns me. I want to keep the conversation
open. If something starts to feel off to you about this group,
I want to be the person you can tell."
```

### Stage 4 — Influence-Boundary Levers

Without forbidding, you have levers:

**Time and space:**
- Where the friends hang out matters: house with adults present vs. unsupervised.
- Time of day / night.
- Frequency.
- Volume of unstructured time.

**Adult-network engagement:**
- Meet the other parents. Have them in your home. Have your teen in theirs.
- Coordinate norms across households (curfew, transportation, substance, safety-call).
- This is often the highest-leverage intervention.

**Counter-positive engagement:**
- Other group memberships (sport, art, religious, volunteer, work, special interest).
- Mentors in other domains.
- Family time that doesn't compete with friend time but adds.

**Substance-specific levers:**
- Substance contract (separate prompt) made independent of friend group.
- Naloxone availability if opioid risk.
- Safety-call commitment.

**Transportation:**
- Who's driving matters.
- Code-exit option.

**Communication channels open:**
- The teen knowing they can come to you = they tell you when something happens.

### Stage 5 — Surface the Identity-Direction Work

A teen with consolidated identity is harder to pull. Identity work in this window:

- Help the teen articulate their own values (separate from family, separate from group).
- Mentor relationships — a coach, a teacher, an aunt, a community elder.
- Domains of mastery — something the teen is good at and proud of.
- A second-context group — a non-friend-group context where the teen has a different identity slot.
- Meaning-work — what does the teen want to do, who do they want to be.

This is slower and more important than friend-group control.

### Stage 6 — When to Engage With the Friend Group

Engaging directly with the friend group:

- Have them at your house. Feed them. Watch them. They become known.
- Make your home a default location.
- Be friendly without infantilizing.
- Watch for individual relationships within the group — one teen may be the energy; another may be the moderating influence.
- Without the teen's knowledge, talk to other parents you know. With the teen's knowledge if they're consenting.

When NOT to engage directly:
- When the engagement would shame the teen.
- When the friend group is a refuge from a hostile family environment (queer teen with rejecting family, e.g.) — engagement may threaten the refuge.

### Stage 7 — Radicalization-Specific Response

If you suspect radicalization toward harmful ideology (manosphere, white nationalism, conspiracy, extremism):

- **Don't lead with confrontation about the ideology.** Lead with the relationship.
- **Get into the content.** Watch what they're watching. Read the subreddits. You can't engage what you don't understand.
- **Identify the underneath.** Loneliness, status, identity, meaning, anger, fear. These ideologies offer answers.
- **Substitute the underneath.** Belonging, mentorship, meaning, identity in a non-harmful direction.
- **Work with a clinician** experienced in deradicalization / extremism (Parents for Peace, Life After Hate, formers' organizations).
- **Avoid forbidding the platform alone.** It removes the ideology from your visibility but not from the teen.
- **Address weapons / violence specifically.** If specific violent ideation toward identifiable targets — mental-health response + law-enforcement consultation. Threat-assessment teams exist in many jurisdictions.

### Stage 8 — When Forbidding Is Right (Rare)

Cases where forbidding contact is appropriate:

- **Adult predator or significant age gap (3+ years for younger teens):** this is grooming, not friendship.
- **Friend group involved in criminal activity** (selling drugs, theft, violence) where teen is being recruited.
- **Specific person actively abusing the teen** (physical, sexual, financial, coercive-control).
- **Friend who has demonstrated intent to harm the teen specifically.**
- **Cult-like context** where group is structurally cutting teen from family / outside relationships.

Even in these cases:

- Explain why. The teen needs to understand the criterion.
- Provide alternatives for the function the friend was serving.
- Engage clinical / legal support.
- Recognize that forbidding may be evaded; structural levers (transportation, location, contact information) are part of enforcement.
- Don't extrapolate from these to less clear cases.

### Stage 9 — When the Concern Is Cruelty Within the Group

If the teen is the target of group cruelty (exclusion, mockery, manipulation), or the perpetrator:

**Target:**
- Listen without immediate response.
- Differentiate ordinary friendship friction from sustained cruelty.
- Intervention: see bullying-target prompt; school engagement if school-context.
- Help teen find alternate-belonging contexts.
- Therapy if the wound is structural.

**Perpetrator:**
- See bullying-perpetrator prompt; address the driver (status-seeking, anxiety, modeling, group dynamics).
- Accountability without shame.
- Repair where possible.

### Stage 10 — Adaptations

- **Autistic teens:** friend-group access may be hard-won; protect the friendship within reason; explicit social-rule conversations about red flags.
- **ADHD teens:** impulsivity in friend-group risk; structural scaffolding (transportation, time-of-day, location).
- **Anxious teens:** any friend group at all is sometimes the win; calibrate.
- **LGBTQ+ teens:** queer community may be the only affirming context; do not strip without alternatives; chosen-family adults critical.
- **Teen with identity-rejecting family:** friend group may be safety net; engagement careful.
- **Trauma history:** friend groups can re-enact trauma dynamics; therapeutic support.
- **Foster / kinship / adopted:** friend group may carry identity-belonging weight that family can't; respect and support.
- **Cultural context:** family / community expectations about peers may differ from teen's actual context; navigate without erasing teen's reality.
- **Newly arrived in school / area:** any friend group is the win in early months; calibrate concerns to time-arc.
- **Custody / split household:** consistent adult-network engagement across households where possible.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Forbid as first move | Influence boundary; teen goes underground if forbidden |
| Confuse style / identity / class with harm | Audit honestly |
| Skip the underneath function | What's the group giving the teen |
| Ignore real harm because "they need to choose" | Some harms warrant intervention |
| Treat teen as the only thing to change | Group dynamics, environment, your levers all matter |
| Default to surveillance | Communication channel + adult network is the better lever |
| Strip queer teen's friend group without alternative | Queer community is often the protective context |
| Confront ideology head-on in radicalization case | Lead with relationship; substitute the underneath |
| Treat 3+-year age gap as friendship | Grooming, not friendship |
| Skip adult-network engagement | Highest-leverage intervention |
| Force teen to reveal everything | Trust = information; pressure = silence |
| Use friend group as the only conversation topic | Identity-direction work matters more |
| Default to therapeutic boarding | Many programs harm; rigorous vetting |
| Forbid without alternatives for the function | Function unmet drives substitution to worse |
| Treat one bad friend as the whole group | Differentiate within group |
| Skip your own caregiver self-work | Discomfort can drive overreach |
