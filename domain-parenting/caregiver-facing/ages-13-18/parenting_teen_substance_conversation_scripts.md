**Purpose:** Equip a caregiver with pre-loaded substance conversations and discovery-response scripts that are honest about teen contexts, harm-reduction-informed, and built on what the adolescent-medicine evidence base actually supports.

**When to use:** Pre-loading the conversation before exposure (12+ for many families); after discovery (smell on clothes, vape found, drunk arrival, social-media evidence, school report, peer disclosure); after a near-miss (driving, hospitalization, overdose); for ongoing dialogue with a teen who uses.

**When NOT to use:** Acute overdose (call emergency services / Narcan if opioid suspected); substance-related medical crisis; suspected substance-use disorder beyond conversation scope (use the CRAFFT-positive routing in this prompt to escalate, then move to formal SUD evaluation).

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- **Suspected opioid overdose** (slow / shallow breathing, pinpoint pupils, unresponsive, blue-tinged lips) → call 911, administer naloxone (Narcan) if available, place in recovery position. Naloxone is over-the-counter in most US states.
- **Severe alcohol intoxication** (unable to stay awake, vomiting while unconscious, slow breathing, cold / clammy / blue) → ED.
- **Cannabis-induced psychosis** (paranoid, agitated, dissociated, lasting beyond intoxication) → ED if severe; psychiatric evaluation.
- **Stimulant overdose** (chest pain, very high HR, hyperthermia, agitation, seizure) → 911.
- **Withdrawal from alcohol or benzos** (tremor, sweating, autonomic surge, seizure risk) → medical, not cold-turkey alone.
- **CRAFFT score ≥2** → adolescent SUD evaluation.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Pre-loading beats reaction.** Most teens encounter substances before parents notice. Conversation before contact lands differently than conversation after.
2. **The relationship is the lever.** Teens whose parents talk substance early, honestly, and without panic are more likely to call when something goes wrong.
3. **Harm reduction does not equal permission.** It equals: a teen who uses anyway is safer if they know how to reduce risk and can call you. The two messages can coexist: "I would prefer you don't, and if you do, here is how you stay safer and what you do if it goes sideways."
4. **The safety call always wins over the consequence.** Pre-commit: any safety call gets a ride home, no consequences that night. Conversation later. This single commitment saves lives.
5. **Honesty about your own use, calibrated.** Teens see through "I never tried it." Some honest framing — without glorification — opens the door.
6. **Substance use in adolescence is rarely just substance use.** It often co-travels with mood, anxiety, ADHD, trauma, social context, or identity. Address the substance and the context.
7. **Brain maturation matters.** Earlier and heavier use during ages 12–25 has greater neuroplastic effect; this is empirical, not moralistic.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Provide substance-specific accurate information.
- Differentiate pre-loading from post-discovery scripts.
- Include the safety-call commitment language.
- Use SBIRT-light (Screening, Brief Intervention, Referral to Treatment) framing.
- Include CRAFFT-based escalation criteria.
- Address polysubstance and emerging-substance reality (THC vape potency, nicotine vape addiction velocity, fentanyl-contaminated supply).

**Must Not:**
- Use scare tactics (DARE-style; evidence shows they backfire).
- Be naive about teen contexts.
- Conflate experimentation with addiction.
- Skip harm reduction.
- Frame any substance use as "phase" without checking driver and severity.
- Pretend the parent's own use never happened if it did.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Substance-Specific Reality Check (What's Actually Out There)

**Alcohol:**
- Still the most prevalent. Liquor concentrated more than teens estimate.
- Binge-pattern more dangerous than total volume.
- Alcohol + driving (own or others') is the highest-acuity risk.
- Alcohol + opioid / benzo combinations in some peer groups.

**Cannabis:**
- Modern flower THC concentration much higher than 1990s comparisons.
- Concentrates / waxes / dabs much higher still (60–90% THC).
- THC-induced anxiety, paranoia, and acute psychosis are real, especially with high-potency.
- Cannabis-induced psychosis risk elevated in those with personal or family psychotic-spectrum history.
- Vomiting cyclic syndrome (CHS) with heavy use.
- Daily use during adolescence associated with mood and cognitive effects.

**Nicotine vape:**
- Pod systems deliver high-concentration nicotine; rapid addiction velocity.
- Adolescent nicotine dependence consolidates fast.
- Stealth use in school bathrooms common.
- Withdrawal real (irritability, concentration, urge).

**THC vape / cartridges:**
- Potency variability (regulated vs. unregulated).
- Unregulated cartridges can contain contaminants (vitamin E acetate caused EVALI lung injury in 2019).
- Concentration much higher than smoked cannabis.

**Prescription misuse:**
- Stimulants (Adderall, Vyvanse) for studying or weight; cardiac and stimulant-overdose risk.
- Benzodiazepines (Xanax) for anxiety / sleep; overdose risk especially in combination with opioids or alcohol; counterfeit pills.
- Opioid pain meds; counterfeit fentanyl-contaminated pills.

**Counterfeit / contaminated pills:**
- Fentanyl contamination of counterfeit pressed pills (especially Xanax-shaped, oxy-shaped, Adderall-shaped) is widespread. Fentanyl overdose can occur from a single pill.
- Naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid overdose; over-the-counter in most US states; reasonable to keep at home.
- Fentanyl test strips can detect contamination but do not measure quantity.

**Other:**
- Psychedelics (mushrooms, LSD): low overdose risk but real psychiatric risk in vulnerable teens; bad trips.
- Dissociatives (ketamine, DXM): risk varies.
- Inhalants in younger adolescents: cardiotoxic, neurotoxic.
- Energy drinks + alcohol combinations.
- "Lean" / codeine cough syrup: opioid risk.

### Stage 2 — The Pre-Loading Conversation (Before Contact)

For middle-school and early-high-school years, before exposure is likely. Best done in a low-stakes setting (driving, walking, cooking).

```
"I want to talk about substances. Not because I think you're using
anything, but because I want you to have real information from me
before you have it from anyone else.

Here's what I want you to know:

1. I'd prefer you don't use anything. The teen brain is still wiring,
   and earlier use changes more than later use.

2. I know that some teens use anyway. So I want you to know what's
   actually going on and how to stay safer if you do.

3. The biggest deal: any safety call wins. If you are anywhere, with
   anyone, and you need a ride — for any reason — you call me, you
   text me, you use a code word. Same night, no consequences. Wrong
   time of night, drunk friends, sketchy party, didn't know what was
   in something — call. We talk later. You don't get in trouble for
   the call.

4. Don't drive. Don't get in a car with anyone who has used. Ever.
   Use the code word. I will come."

[Then, calibrated to age and what's likely:]

5. Specifics about the substances most likely to be in their context.

6. Specific about counterfeit pills and fentanyl: 'If anyone offers you
   a pill — Adderall, Xanax, anything — don't take it unless it came
   directly from a labeled pharmacy bottle with your name on it. Pills
   from anywhere else can be counterfeit and contain fentanyl. People
   die from one pill. This is not me being dramatic; this is what's
   actually happening.'

7. Surface their questions. Listen. Don't punish for honesty."
```

### Stage 3 — The Discovery-Response Script (After You Find Something)

```
[NOT in the moment of discovery if you are flooded. Take a beat.]

[NOT a confrontation if they are intoxicated. Get them safe; talk
later.]

When you do talk:

"I want to talk about [what I found / what I noticed]. I'm not going
to interrogate you. I'm not going to scream. I want to know what's
actually going on.

[Pause for response.]

Tell me about it. What were you using? How often? With whom? In what
context? What was it doing for you?

[Listen. Don't interrupt with consequences yet.]

I'm asking because I need to know what we're actually dealing with.
The conversation is different if this was once at a party than if
it's been every weekend, and different again if it's daily.

[After hearing:]

Here's what I'm thinking. [Specific to severity.]

If experimentation: 'This is the conversation we have now. Here are
the safety pieces I need from you. Here's the safety-call commitment.
Here's what I'm worried about.'

If repeated: 'This is more than I knew. I want us to look at what
this is doing for you, and to think about whether we need help.'

If concerning: 'I need us to see someone. This is past me. Let's
find someone who knows this work.'

In all cases:

- Repeat the safety-call commitment. Say it twice.
- Tighten autonomy in the relevant rung (transportation, party
  attendance) without dynamiting the relationship.
- Don't lock down the friend group as the first move; understand the
  context.
- Schedule the next conversation. This is not one-and-done."
```

### Stage 4 — CRAFFT-Based Severity Read

CRAFFT is the standard adolescent SUD screen. Six questions; ≥2 yes = consider SUD evaluation.

- **C** — Have you ever ridden in a Car driven by someone (including yourself) high or having used alcohol or drugs?
- **R** — Do you ever use to Relax, feel better about yourself, or fit in?
- **A** — Do you use while you are by yourself, Alone?
- **F** — Do you ever Forget things you did while using?
- **F** — Do your Family or friends ever tell you that you should cut down?
- **T** — Have you ever gotten into Trouble while using?

Caregiver-side application:
- Pediatrician administers formally; you can listen for these signals.
- Solo use, use to cope, forgetting, trouble = move from conversation to evaluation.

### Stage 5 — Harm Reduction Without Permission (The Both / And)

Coexisting messages are not contradiction. Frame:

```
"I would prefer you don't use. And: if you do anyway, I want you
alive, conscious, and in touch with me. So:

- Never use alone if it's anything stronger than the alcohol you've
  tried.
- Never combine substances. Especially: alcohol + opioids, alcohol +
  benzos, opioids + benzos.
- Never take a pill that didn't come from a sealed pharmacy bottle
  with your name. Counterfeit fentanyl is real.
- Don't drive. Don't ride with anyone who used. Code word.
- If someone is unresponsive, hard to wake, breathing wrong — call
  911. Good Samaritan laws protect you in most states. Don't leave
  them.
- Carry naloxone if your context warrants. We can keep it at home.
- Stay hydrated, eat, know your limit, don't accept open drinks from
  others, don't leave drinks unattended.
- Tell me if anything happens. I'd rather know than not."
```

### Stage 6 — Address the Driver

If use is patterned (not one-time experimentation), surface what it's doing for the teen:

- **Anxiety / social anxiety:** substance is making something tolerable.
- **Depression:** substance is filling a numbness.
- **ADHD:** substance is regulating attention or arousal.
- **Trauma:** substance is anesthetizing.
- **Sleep:** substance is making sleep happen.
- **Identity:** substance is part of who the teen is being seen as.
- **Peer:** substance is the cost of belonging.
- **Boredom / understimulation:** substance is novelty.

The substance conversation that ignores the driver tends to fail. Address both.

### Stage 7 — When to Escalate to Formal Evaluation

- Daily or near-daily use of any substance.
- Use across school day.
- Use before driving.
- Withdrawal symptoms.
- Failed attempts to stop.
- CRAFFT ≥2.
- Co-occurring mental-health symptoms.
- Functional decline (school, sport, friends, family).
- Legal involvement.

Routing:
- **Pediatrician:** SBIRT screen, refer.
- **Adolescent SUD specialist / adolescent psychiatry:** outpatient, MAT (medication-assisted treatment) for opioid use disorder (buprenorphine, naltrexone) — yes, including in adolescents, evidence supports.
- **Outpatient SUD program / IOP / PHP** with adolescent track.
- **Residential** for severe.
- **Family therapy** alongside any individual treatment; CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) for families of users.

### Stage 8 — Adaptations

- **Anxious teen:** alcohol and cannabis often function as anxiety self-medication; treat the anxiety directly.
- **ADHD teen:** stimulant misuse risk; cannabis self-medication common; ADHD treatment (including medication) reduces SUD risk.
- **Depressed teen:** substance + suicidality combination is high-risk; means restriction.
- **Trauma history:** substance often anesthetizes; trauma-informed care.
- **Athlete:** identity-driven sobriety often holds; performance frame can land.
- **LGBTQ+:** elevated risk in unaffirming contexts; affirming care.
- **Family history of SUD:** elevated risk; honesty about family pattern.
- **First-gen / immigrant family:** stigma may be high; consider cultural-broker clinicians.
- **Single parent / co-parent disagreement:** unify on the safety-call commitment minimum, even if other rules diverge.
- **Custody / split household:** substance contracts cross households or get exploited.

| Pretend you never used if you did | Calibrated honesty; no glorification |

| Punish the safety call | Punishing the call = no future calls |
| Use yes/no thinking on harm reduction | Both / and: preference + safer-use information |

| Forbid the friend group as first move | Understand the context first |
