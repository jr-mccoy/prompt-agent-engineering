**Purpose:** Help a parent produce a short, factual handoff brief that travels with the child between homes — covering what the receiving parent genuinely needs to know (an upcoming school event, where homework stands, a medical update, a social thing, how the child slept or felt) so they aren't caught off guard. The brief is about the child, not a report on the other parent's household. The output is a ready-to-send template the parent can reuse at every exchange.

**When to use:** Regular transitions where the receiving parent keeps getting surprised ("I didn't know she had a project due"); after a medical appointment or a rough patch you want to hand off cleanly; setting up a repeatable routine so nobody has to chase information; the child is too young to self-report what's going on.

**When NOT to use:** You're tempted to use the "brief" to document or critique the other parent's parenting — that's not this tool (see Constraints). There's a safety issue you need to escalate rather than note in a routine handoff (see Safety Block).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A handoff would need to convey that a child was harmed, neglected, or exposed to danger in the other home → that is not a routine brief; Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911; document and route to counsel.
- Sharing information becomes a vector for the other parent to monitor, control, or intimidate you → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US); share only what's required and consult an advocate.
- The child discloses self-harm or crisis that needs to be conveyed → don't bury it in a routine brief; 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day; communicate directly and urgently.

A handoff brief is for ordinary, child-focused logistics. Safety information needs a faster, more direct channel and often professional involvement.

---

## Core Principles

1. **The brief is about the child, not the other home.** Report on the child's needs, schedule, and state — never on how the other parent runs their house.
2. **Facts, not editorializing.** "Slept poorly, woke twice" is useful; "obviously didn't get enough sleep at your place" is a dig. Strip the second kind.
3. **Only what the receiving parent needs to act.** A handoff isn't a diary; include the items that affect what the receiving parent must do or know.
4. **Sharing isn't surveillance.** You give information so the child is well cared for — not to demand a matching report or to track the other parent's life.
5. **Consistent structure beats one-off scrambles.** A reusable template means nothing important gets dropped and nobody has to interrogate the child.
6. **The child shouldn't be the briefing system.** Kids forget, misreport, and shouldn't carry the responsibility of relaying medical or school information.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Produce a reusable, factual handoff template covering the categories that matter.
- Keep every line about the child and what the receiving parent needs to do/know.
- Stay neutral and non-evaluative in tone.
- Flag time-sensitive items clearly.
- Keep the child out of the role of information-carrier for important matters.

**Must Not:**
- Badmouth, critique, or characterize the other parent or their home.
- Use the brief to surveil the other parent or demand a tit-for-tat report.
- Use the child as a spy or messenger to gather/relay the information.
- Coach the parent to build a "record" against the other parent disguised as a brief.
- Diagnose or label the other parent.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Confirm Purpose and Tone
Confirm the brief is to keep the child well cared for across the handoff — not to evaluate the other home. Set the neutral, factual tone.

### Stage 2 — Select the Categories
From the parent's inputs, choose the relevant handoff categories:
- **School & homework:** upcoming events, due dates, where assignments stand, forms/permission slips, supplies needed.
- **Medical & health:** appointments done/upcoming, medication given/due, symptoms, allergies-of-note, follow-ups.
- **Social & activities:** practices, parties, friend plans, anything the child is anxious or excited about.
- **State (mood/sleep/eating):** brief, factual notes that help the receiving parent meet the child where they are — without judging the other home.
- **Logistics:** what's traveling with the child (devices, comfort items, gear, meds).

### Stage 3 — Draft the Template
Build a short, fill-in-the-blank template the parent can reuse every handoff, with a clearly marked "time-sensitive this week" line at top.

### Stage 4 — Neutrality Scrub
Re-read for any line that evaluates the other home or carries a dig. Rewrite to neutral, child-focused facts.

### Stage 5 — Set the Routine
Specify when and how the brief is sent (e.g., morning of the exchange, via the app), and confirm the child isn't being relied on to carry medical/school information.

## Output Format

```markdown
# Handoff Brief — [Child] — [date / this transition]

## ⏰ Time-sensitive (act this week)
- [Event / form / med / appointment with date]

## School & homework
- Upcoming: [event/date]
- Homework status: [what's due, where it stands]
- Forms/supplies: [needed]

## Medical & health
- Meds: [given / due / schedule]
- Appointments: [done / upcoming]
- Notes: [symptoms, allergies-of-note, follow-up]

## Social & activities
- This week: [practice/party/plans]
- Heads-up: [anything the child is anxious/excited about]

## How [child] has been (factual)
- Sleep: [e.g., woke twice Tues]
- Mood/eating: [brief, neutral]

## Traveling with [child]
- [devices / comfort items / meds / gear]

---
Sent via [channel], [when]. Reusable each transition.
```

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "She didn't sleep — you let her stay up again" | "Woke twice Tuesday night" — fact only |
| Demand a matching report from the other parent | Share to help the child; don't keep score |
| Send the project info through the kid | Adults relay school/medical info directly |
| Turn the brief into a critique log | Keep it to what the receiving parent must do/know |
| List every detail of the week | Include only what's actionable |
| Note "as usual, no homework got done there" | State the homework status neutrally |
| Bury an urgent med change in a long note | Flag time-sensitive items at the top |
| Use the brief to build a "record" | Keep documentation separate and purposeful |
| Quiz the child to fill in the brief | Use your own knowledge + school/medical sources |
| Label the other parent "disorganized" | Describe the logistic, not their character |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Detailed care logs matter most (feeding, naps, diapers, meds, milestones); the brief is the child's only voice — keep it thorough and factual.
- **4–8:** School forms, homework, and social plans dominate; the child can't reliably relay these, so the brief carries them.
- **9–12:** Kids can self-report some logistics, but keep medical and school items in the brief; include heads-ups about friendships and anxieties.
- **13–18:** Teens manage much of their own information; keep the brief to genuinely cross-home items (meds, big events, health) and don't over-report on their social life.

**By profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Keep it strictly factual and minimal; one channel; expect the brief itself may be misread — keep nothing in it you wouldn't want quoted; pair with `parenting_coparenting_high_conflict_response_strategy.md`.
- **Child with ADHD/autism:** Medication timing, therapy notes, transition cues, and sensory needs belong in the brief precisely; this is where clean handoff matters most.
- **Anxious child:** Note what's coming so the receiving parent can prepare the child; flag triggers gently and factually.
- **Unsafe/absent-parent context:** Share only what's required and safe; route safety information urgently and directly, not via a routine brief; see `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`.
