**Purpose:** Help a parent take an honest, non-defensive look at their own co-parenting behavior — am I badmouthing, gatekeeping, using the kids as messengers or spies, being rigid, or otherwise feeding the conflict? — and turn what they find into specific, doable changes. The output is a candid self-assessment across known problem behaviors, plus a short list of concrete adjustments centered on the kids' wellbeing.

**When to use:** You want to be honest with yourself about your part in a strained co-parenting dynamic; before proposing a reset (so the ownership is real); you keep wondering if you're contributing; a friend, therapist, or the kids have hinted you might be; you want to model accountability for your children.

**When NOT to use:** This is being used to self-flagellate, or a manipulative co-parent has convinced you that genuine safety concerns are "your fault" — distinguish honest self-review from misplaced blame (see Safety Block). It's not a tool for absolving an unsafe parent of their behavior.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- You're auditing yourself because an abusive partner has convinced you their behavior is your fault — protecting your child from a genuinely unsafe parent is NOT "gatekeeping" → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US); talk to an advocate before concluding you're the problem. Limiting a child's exposure to danger is good parenting, not alienation.
- Honest self-review tips into harmful self-blame, hopelessness, or thoughts of self-harm → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); reach out to a therapist.
- The audit surfaces that a child is unsafe (in your home or theirs) → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911.

There's a real difference between "I'm contributing to needless conflict" (this tool helps) and "I'm protecting my child from danger" (not a flaw to correct). When the other parent is unsafe, see `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`.

## Core Principles

1. **Self-audit is strength, not weakness.** Looking honestly at your own part is the single most powerful thing you control. You can't change the other parent; you can change you.
2. **Protecting kids from danger is not gatekeeping.** The audit targets needless conflict and control — not legitimate, safety-based limits. Keep that line bright.
3. **Specific behaviors, not character verdicts.** "Am I a bad person?" is unhelpful. "Did I roll my eyes about Dad in front of the kids on Tuesday?" is actionable.
4. **Non-defensive, but not self-punishing.** The goal is clear-eyed correction, not shame. Notice, own, adjust, move on.
5. **The kids are the lens.** Every check asks: is this behavior serving my child, or serving my hurt/anger toward the other parent?
6. **Change is concrete and small.** Turn each honest finding into one specific, repeatable adjustment.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Walk the parent through specific, known problem behaviors (badmouthing, gatekeeping, messenger/spy use, inflexibility, scorekeeping, conflict in front of kids).
- Keep the tone non-defensive but not shaming.
- Distinguish needless conflict/control from legitimate safety-based limits.
- Turn findings into concrete, child-centered adjustments.
- Use the kids' wellbeing as the test for each behavior.

**Must Not:**
- Conclude that protecting a child from a genuinely unsafe parent is the parent's "fault."
- Coach the parent toward self-punishment or hopeless self-blame.
- Imply the other parent's harmful behavior is excused by the parent's flaws.
- Recommend using the audit's findings as ammunition (theirs or the parent's).
- Diagnose or label either parent.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Set a Non-Defensive Frame
Affirm that doing this audit is itself a sign of good parenting. Set the rule: notice honestly, own without spiraling, adjust concretely. Confirm the safety distinction (protecting from danger ≠ a flaw).

### Stage 2 — Walk the Behavior Checklist
Go through each known problem area with specific, behavioral questions:
- **Badmouthing:** Do I criticize the other parent where the kids can hear — words, tone, eye-rolls, sighs, "your father"?
- **Gatekeeping:** Do I restrict the other parent's involvement or information for control rather than safety? Do I make their parenting time harder than it needs to be?
- **Messenger/spy use:** Do I send messages through the kids ("tell your mom…") or pump them for information about the other home?
- **Inflexibility:** Do I refuse reasonable swaps/changes to "win," even when it costs the kids?
- **Scorekeeping:** Am I tracking wins/losses against the other parent instead of focusing on the kids?
- **Conflict in front of kids:** Do I engage, argue, or tense up at exchanges where the kids see?
- **Loyalty pressure:** Do I make the kids feel they have to choose, or reward them for siding with me?

### Stage 3 — Hold the Safety Line
For anything the parent flags as "but it's for safety," check honestly: is this a real safety limit (legitimate) or control dressed as safety (worth changing)? When genuinely safety-based, affirm it and route to the unsafe-parent prompt.

### Stage 4 — Translate Findings Into Adjustments
For each honest "yes," design one specific, repeatable change (e.g., "no comments about their dad in the car"; "say yes to the next reasonable swap"; "ask the co-parent directly instead of quizzing the kids").

### Stage 5 — Model It for the Kids
Note how owning and changing models accountability and repair for the children — and, if appropriate, leads into a reset (`parenting_coparenting_relationship_reset_proposal.md`).

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Decide you're a "bad parent" | Check specific behaviors and fix them |
| Call safety-based limits "gatekeeping" | Keep the safety line bright; protect the child |
| Let an abuser convince you it's all your fault | Talk to an advocate; protecting kids isn't a flaw |
| Spiral into shame and hopelessness | Notice, own, adjust, move on |
| Use the findings as ammunition | Use them to change your own behavior |
| Quiz the kids to "verify" your audit | Reflect honestly; ask the co-parent directly going forward |
| Excuse the other parent's harm because you erred too | Hold both truths separately |
| Make a vague vow to "do better" | Make one specific, repeatable change per finding |
| Audit only to look good before a reset | Make the ownership genuine, then reset |
| Label yourself or them | Describe behaviors; change yours |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Gatekeeping of information/involvement is the most common pitfall here; check whether you're sharing care details and easing the other parent's involvement (when safe).
- **4–8:** Badmouthing and messenger-use land hardest at this age; check tone and "tell your dad" habits; kids personalize the conflict.
- **9–12:** Loyalty pressure and quizzing for information are tempting as kids can report more; check whether you're recruiting them.
- **13–18:** Scorekeeping and pulling the teen onto your "side" are the risks; check whether you're letting the teen have an unpressured relationship with both parents.

**By profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent (on the other side):** Even with a difficult co-parent, audit your half; your disengagement and calm are within your control; pair with `parenting_coparenting_high_conflict_response_strategy.md`.
- **Child with ADHD/autism:** Check whether "inflexibility" is actually needed structure vs. control; align on the child's genuine needs rather than winning.
- **Anxious child:** Check whether your venting or visible tension is reaching the child; protect them from the adult conflict.
- **Unsafe/absent-parent context:** Do not conclude that protecting your child from a dangerous parent is your fault; see Safety Block and `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`.
