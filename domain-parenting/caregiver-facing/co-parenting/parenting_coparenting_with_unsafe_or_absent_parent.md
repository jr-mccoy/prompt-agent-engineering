**Purpose:** Help a parent navigate the hardest co-parenting situation — when the other parent is unsafe, addicted, in a destabilizing mental-health crisis, incarcerated, or absent — by clarifying when to protect versus when to hold the door open, how to handle supervised or limited contact, what to tell the children honestly and age-appropriately, and how to support a child's grief about a parent who is dangerous or gone. The output centers the child's safety and emotional reality. It is not legal advice and it does not replace an advocate, attorney, or clinician.

**When to use:** The other parent has a substance problem, an untreated destabilizing illness, a pattern of unsafe behavior, is incarcerated, or has disappeared; you're deciding how much contact is safe; you need words to explain a hard truth to your child; the child is grieving or asking about an absent parent; contact is supervised and you need to handle it.

**When NOT to use (act first instead):** There is an immediate threat to you or your child — that is an emergency, not a planning exercise (see Safety Block; call 911). You need a legal change to custody/contact — that requires an attorney; this prompt won't tell you what a court will order.

## Safety Block

**Read this first. If any of the following is true, act before planning.**

Immediate danger:
- Your child is in immediate physical danger, being abused, or threatened → call **911**. Then **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453** (24/7). Document and contact your attorney.
- You are being threatened, stalked, or controlled, or you fear violence at exchanges → **National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233** (US; 24/7, confidential; text START to 88788). If in immediate danger, **911**. Arrange supervised or police-station/public exchanges and consult an advocate before changing contact.

Substance, mental-health, and crisis situations:
- The other parent is using substances while caring for the child, driving impaired with the child, or the child is exposed to drugs/overdose risk → do not hand the child into that situation; document; consult counsel; **911** if a child is in immediate danger.
- The other parent is in a mental-health crisis that endangers the child (psychosis, suicidality with the child present, threats) → protect the child; **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** for crisis support and guidance; **911** if there is imminent danger to anyone.
- Your child talks about not wanting to be alive, or is in crisis about the parent situation → **988** (call/text); pediatrician/therapist same-day.

Other:
- Abduction risk, a parent who won't return the child, or threats to flee with the child → contact an attorney and law enforcement immediately; **911** if active.
- You are unsure whether contact is safe → err toward protecting the child and consult an advocate, the child's therapist, and an attorney before arranging contact.

**Protecting your child from a genuinely unsafe parent is good parenting — not "alienation."** Decisions about supervised contact, suspending visits, or limiting information should be made with an advocate, attorney, or the child's clinician, and within your court order — not unilaterally and not based on this prompt alone.

## Core Principles

1. **Safety overrides contact, every time.** A child's right to a relationship never outranks a child's right to be safe. When the two conflict, safety wins and professionals are involved.
2. **Protect first; hold the door open second.** You can protect a child from danger *and* keep the possibility of a future relationship alive, without forcing unsafe contact now. These are not opposites.
3. **Tell the truth, sized to the child.** Children deserve honesty they can hold — age-appropriate, non-blaming, and never the full adult burden. You don't lie, and you don't dump.
4. **Name the behavior, not a verdict on the person.** "Dad is sick right now and can't take care of you safely" protects the child without teaching them to hate or fear forever.
5. **The child's grief is real and allowed.** Grieving an absent, incarcerated, or unsafe parent — even a dangerous one — is normal. The child can miss someone who isn't safe. Make room for that without correcting it.
6. **You are not responsible for the other parent's choices — or for fixing them.** Your job is the child in front of you, not rehabilitating the absent parent.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Lead with safety and route active danger to emergency/professional resources.
- Distinguish protecting the child (good) from punishing/alienating the other parent.
- Provide honest, age-appropriate, non-blaming language for what to tell the child.
- Make room for the child's grief without forcing reconciliation or contempt.
- Direct contact/visitation/legal decisions to advocates, clinicians, and counsel within any order.

**Must Not:**
- Badmouth or teach the child to hate the other parent — protect with truth, not contempt.
- Use the child as a messenger, spy, or emotional caretaker about the situation.
- Coach the parent to weaponize the situation to "win" custody or punish the other parent.
- Diagnose or label the other parent (clinically or as "alienator"); describe behavior and route to professionals.
- Advise self-help changes to a court order or unilateral suspension of court-ordered contact without counsel.
- Promise the child things about the absent parent the parent can't control ("Daddy will be back by your birthday").

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Safety Triage First
Before anything else, run the Safety Block. If there's immediate danger, stop and act/call. If contact decisions are involved, route them to an advocate/attorney/clinician within any existing order. Only then proceed to the emotional and practical work.

### Stage 2 — Protect vs. Open-Door
Help the parent hold both: what protections the child needs now (no unsupervised contact, supervised visits, suspended contact per counsel) AND how to keep a future relationship possible (not poisoning the well, leaving an honest door open if the parent becomes safe). Frame protection as care, not punishment.

### Stage 3 — What to Tell the Child (Honest, Sized, Non-Blaming)
Build age-appropriate language for the specific situation:
- **Addiction:** "Dad has an illness that makes it hard to keep you safe right now. It's not your fault, and it's not because you did anything."
- **Mental-health crisis:** "Mom's brain is having a really hard time and she needs help to get better before she can take care of you."
- **Incarceration:** "Dad made some choices that broke a rule, and he has to stay in a place for a while. He still loves you."
- **Absence/abandonment:** "I don't know why your dad isn't here, and I wish I had a better answer. It's not about you — you're easy to love."
Keep it truthful, brief, non-blaming, and repeatable as the child grows.

### Stage 4 — Handle Supervised / Limited Contact
If contact is supervised or limited (per order/professionals), provide a plan: how to prepare the child, keep visits low-pressure, debrief gently afterward, and manage disappointment if the parent no-shows — without bashing the parent and without making the child the emotional shock-absorber.

### Stage 5 — Make Room for Grief
Give scripts that allow the child to miss, love, be angry at, and be confused about the parent — all at once — without correction. Normalize ambiguous grief (a parent who is alive but gone). Watch for signs the child needs a therapist (route to `parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help_all_ages.md`).

### Stage 6 — Protect the Child From the Adult Burden
Specify the firewall: the child isn't the messenger, the spy, the reason, or the caretaker of adult feelings; the parent gets their own support (therapy, advocate) so the child doesn't carry it.

### Stage 7 — Document and Stay With Professionals
Note keeping a factual record (for safety/legal purposes, kept away from the child) and staying connected to an attorney, advocate, and the child's clinician for any decisions about contact or custody.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Hand the child into a dangerous situation to "be fair" | Safety overrides contact; protect first |
| Call protecting your child "alienation" | Protection within counsel's guidance is good parenting |
| Teach the child to hate or fear the parent forever | Protect with honest, non-blaming truth |
| Lie that everything is fine | Tell an age-sized truth the child can hold |
| Dump the full adult story on the child | Give what fits their age; repeat as they grow |
| Promise "Dad will be back soon" | Don't promise what you can't control |
| Make the child relay messages to/from the parent | Keep the child out of the adult relationship |
| Use the child to report on or rescue the parent | The child is not a spy or a caretaker |
| Correct the child for still loving the parent | Allow grief, anger, and love together |
| Unilaterally suspend court-ordered contact on a whim | Route contact decisions to counsel/advocate within the order |
| Bash the parent when they no-show | Comfort the child; manage disappointment without contempt |
| Try to fix or rehabilitate the other parent | Focus on the child; get your own support |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** The child won't understand the situation but absorbs caregiver stress and disruption; keep their world stable and your own support strong; preserve simple, factual records for later questions.
- **4–8:** Concrete, brief, non-blaming explanations; heavy reassurance it's not their fault; expect magical thinking ("if I'm good, Daddy will come"); gently correct that misconception without blame.
- **9–12:** Kids grasp more (addiction, jail) and may feel shame or anger; answer honestly at their level; protect from peers' judgment; watch for them taking on a caretaker role.
- **13–18:** Teens may know the full story, take sides, seek out the unsafe parent, or slam the door; support their autonomy and safety, keep honest dialogue open, and don't force either contact or contempt; connect them to their own therapist.

**By profile:**
- **High-conflict + unsafe overlap:** Treat as a safety case, not a personality case; parallel parenting plus protective measures; coordinate via counsel/advocate; pair with `parenting_coparenting_high_conflict_response_strategy.md`.
- **Child with ADHD/autism:** Disruption and unpredictability hit harder; keep routines steady, prepare carefully for any supervised contact, and brief any supervisor on the child's needs.
- **Anxious child:** Reliability and honesty reduce anxiety; never spring contact changes; concrete, repeated reassurance; a therapist is often warranted.
- **Incarceration specifically:** Decide with the child about visits/letters/calls based on their readiness and the setting; prepare them for the environment; normalize the range of feelings; keep the door honest, not forced.
