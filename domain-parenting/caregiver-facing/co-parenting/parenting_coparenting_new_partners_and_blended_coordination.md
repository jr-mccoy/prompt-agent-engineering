**Purpose:** Help a parent navigate the introduction and integration of a new partner (theirs or the co-parent's) in a way that protects the child and keeps co-parenting workable — defining the step/bonus parent's role and limits, clarifying who handles discipline and decisions, finding a neutral way to communicate about new partners with the co-parent, and supporting the child through the adjustment. The output is a role map, boundary script, communication plan, and child-support plan.

**When to use:** You're about to introduce a new partner to the kids; the co-parent has a new partner and you need to figure out how to handle it; a step-parent and child are clashing over authority; you and your partner disagree on the partner's role; the child is struggling with a new adult in either home.

**When NOT to use:** A new partner poses a genuine safety risk to the child (history of abuse, substance danger) — that's a safety/legal matter, not a coordination one (see Safety Block). You need to know what your order says about introducing partners → legal advice; check with counsel.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A new partner (yours or the co-parent's) has a history that endangers the child — abuse, violence, untreated substance problems, predatory behavior → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911; consult an attorney about protecting the child. This is not a "boundaries" conversation.
- A new partner is being used to intimidate, monitor, or control you, or the co-parent's reaction to your partner involves threats → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US).
- The child discloses fear of or harm by a new adult in either home → believe it, protect the child, document, and route to CPS/counsel; 911 if immediate.
- The transition triggers a mental-health crisis in the child → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

Coordinating new partners assumes the new adults are safe. If safety is in question, safety comes first, through professionals — not through household rules.

## Core Principles

1. **The child sets the pace, not the adults' romance.** Introductions and integration go at the child's speed; rushing a new partner into a parental role harms adjustment.
2. **A step/bonus parent is an additional caring adult, not a replacement.** They support the parent's authority; they don't compete with or override the other biological parent.
3. **Discipline and big decisions stay with the parents — at first, and often for a long time.** The new partner builds relationship before assuming any authority, and major decisions remain with the legal parents.
4. **Your co-parent's new partner is the co-parent's business — within safety limits.** You don't get a veto over a safe adult in the other home; you do get to protect your child from danger.
5. **Communicate about new partners as logistics and safety, not jealousy.** "Who is supervising the kids" is a fair question; "I don't like that you're dating" is not a co-parenting matter.
6. **The child's loyalty must be protected.** No child should feel they're betraying one parent by liking a step-parent, or vice versa.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Define the step/bonus parent's role and explicit limits (discipline, decisions).
- Set the introduction/integration pace to the child's readiness.
- Provide neutral language for communicating about new partners with the co-parent.
- Center the child's adjustment and loyalty protection.
- Distinguish safe-adult boundary questions from controlling-the-co-parent's-life.

**Must Not:**
- Badmouth the co-parent or their new partner to or near the kids.
- Use the child as a messenger or spy about the other home's new partner.
- Coach the parent to weaponize a new partner against the co-parent, or to police the co-parent's safe relationships.
- Diagnose or label the co-parent or any new partner.
- Pressure the child to accept or reject a new adult on the adults' timeline.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Validate and Name the Situation
Acknowledge that new partners are one of the hardest co-parenting transitions. Identify which situation applies (your partner, theirs, or a clash) since the playbook differs.

### Stage 2 — Map the Step/Bonus Parent's Role
Define, with the parent (and ideally their partner), the role and limits:
- **Relationship first:** fun, support, presence — before any authority.
- **Discipline:** initially the biological parent handles it; the partner supports the parent's rules rather than imposing their own. Authority grows only as relationship and time do.
- **Decisions:** major decisions stay with the legal parents; the partner advises privately, not in front of the child.
- **Naming:** let the child choose what to call the partner; never force "Mom/Dad."

### Stage 3 — Set the Introduction Pace
If not yet introduced, plan a gradual, low-pressure introduction keyed to the child's age and readiness, not the relationship's milestones. Avoid introducing a string of partners.

### Stage 4 — Communicating With the Co-Parent
Draft neutral language for the genuinely co-parenting parts (who's caring for the kids, advance notice of a cohabiting partner) and separate out the parts that aren't the co-parent's call (a safe adult's mere existence). Route phrasing to `parenting_coparenting_message_composer_biff.md`. For the co-parent's new partner, the fair questions are about the child's safety and care, not about the relationship.

### Stage 5 — Support the Child's Adjustment
Provide scripts that protect loyalty: permission to like the step-parent without betraying the other parent; permission to take time; no pressure to call anyone "Mom/Dad"; acknowledgment of grief about the original family. Address sibling/step-sibling dynamics if relevant.

### Stage 6 — Align the Two Adults in Your Home
Help the parent and their partner get on the same page privately so the child sees a united, calm front — disagreements about the partner's role are worked out away from the child.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Hand the new partner full discipline authority on day one | Build relationship first; authority grows slowly |
| Force the child to call a partner "Mom/Dad" | Let the child choose the name and the pace |
| Veto the co-parent's safe new relationship | Limit questions to the child's safety and care |
| Quiz the child about the other home's partner | Ask the co-parent directly about care/safety |
| Introduce a series of partners to the kids | Introduce slowly, when a relationship is serious |
| Run down the new partner in front of the child | Protect the child's loyalty to everyone |
| Let the partner make major child decisions | Keep major decisions with the legal parents |
| Use a new partner to provoke the co-parent | Keep coordination about logistics and safety |
| Argue the partner's role in front of the child | Settle it privately; present a calm front |
| Label the co-parent or partner | Describe behavior; protect the child |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Toddlers attach quickly but need stability; introduce a partner gradually as a consistent, gentle presence; keep caregiving primarily with the parent.
- **4–8:** High loyalty sensitivity; lots of reassurance that liking a step-parent doesn't hurt either parent; concrete naming choices; expect testing of the new adult's authority.
- **9–12:** Kids reason about fairness and "replacement" fears; involve them in low-stakes ways; respect their pace; address step-sibling dynamics explicitly.
- **13–18:** Teens may resist a new adult's authority outright; lean on relationship and respect over imposed rules; don't force closeness; let them set the relationship's depth.

**By profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Expect the new partner to become a flashpoint; share only required logistics; keep the partner out of co-parent communication; pair with `parenting_coparenting_high_conflict_response_strategy.md`.
- **Child with ADHD/autism:** New adults and routine changes are destabilizing; introduce very gradually, keep routines steady, and brief the partner on the child's needs and cues.
- **Anxious child:** Predictability and reassurance are key; over-communicate the plan; never spring a cohabitation or marriage on the child.
- **Unsafe/absent-parent context:** If a new partner is the safety concern, see Safety Block and `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`; protect the child through professionals/counsel.
