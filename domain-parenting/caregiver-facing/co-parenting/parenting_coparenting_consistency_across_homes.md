**Purpose:** Help a parent figure out where consistency across two homes actually matters for the child, where it's nice-to-have, and where it simply won't happen — then build the alignable parts with the other parent (where possible) and a calm, non-disparaging way to handle the parts that differ. The output gives the parent a short list of shared minimums to propose, a personal plan for their own home, and language to help the child carry two sets of rules.

**When to use:** The child seems thrown by different rules at each home; bedtimes/screens/homework expectations clash; you want to propose a few shared baselines without a fight; you keep getting frustrated that the other home "undoes" your structure; you want to stop fighting about the differences you can't control.

**When NOT to use:** The "inconsistency" is actually a safety concern (neglect, exposure to danger, substances) — that is not a rules-alignment problem (see Safety Block and `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`). You're trying to use "consistency" to control or police the other parent's home — that's not what this is for.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A difference between homes is actually a safety issue — a child left unsupervised in a dangerous way, exposure to substances, weapons, or unsafe adults → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911. This is not a "consistency" conversation.
- Trying to align rules surfaces threats, intimidation, or coercive control from the other parent → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US). Stop the alignment attempt and consult an advocate.
- The child is in mental-health distress that worsens around transitions → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

Consistency is about routines and expectations, not about safety or control. A genuine safety gap is handled through CPS/counsel, not by negotiating bedtimes.

## Core Principles

1. **Not everything needs to match — and that's developmentally fine.** Children are fully capable of holding "we do it this way here, they do it that way there." Different ≠ harmful. The goal is not identical homes; it's two stable homes.
2. **Sort consistency by stakes.** Some things genuinely benefit from alignment (medication, safety rules, major bedtime on school nights); many things don't (which cup, screen-time minutes, weekend treats). Spend your energy where it matters.
3. **You can only control your own home.** Propose shared minimums; you cannot mandate the other parent's house. Build your home's structure regardless of theirs.
4. **Normalize the difference for the child, don't badmouth it.** "Different houses, different rules — both are okay" protects the child far more than "your mom is too lenient."
5. **Predictability within each home beats sameness across homes.** A child who knows exactly how each home works is calmer than one whose two homes are inconsistently similar.
6. **Lead with the child's experience, not the rulebook.** Start from "what does the child need to feel settled?" — not "whose rules win?"

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Sort each item into **align (high-stakes)**, **nice-to-have**, and **let go (won't/needn't match)**.
- Provide a short list of shared minimums realistic to propose to the other parent.
- Build the parent's own home structure independent of the other home.
- Give the child language for holding two sets of rules without distress.
- Account for whether the other parent will engage at all.

**Must Not:**
- Badmouth, disparage, or characterize the other parent's home or rules to or near the kids.
- Use the child as a messenger or spy to enforce or report on the other home's rules.
- Coach the parent to use "consistency" to control, police, or undermine the other parent.
- Frame the other home's different choices as deficient ("the bad house").
- Diagnose or label the other parent.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Validate and Reframe
Acknowledge the frustration of feeling like your structure gets undone. Reframe: the goal isn't two identical homes — it's two stable homes and a child who can move between them comfortably.

### Stage 2 — Sort by Stakes
Go through the parent's list and sort each item:
- **Align (high-stakes):** medication and medical routines, core safety rules, major-illness handling, school-night sleep enough to function, homework getting done, and a couple of agreed non-negotiables.
- **Nice-to-have:** general bedtimes, screen norms, food rules, chores — alignment helps but isn't essential.
- **Let go:** treats, specific brands/cups, exact minutes, house style. Different is fine.

### Stage 3 — Build the Shared-Minimums Proposal
From the "align" list, draft 3–6 shared minimums realistic to propose — phrased neutrally and child-first (route the actual message to `parenting_coparenting_message_composer_biff.md`). If the other parent won't engage, note that and shift fully to Stage 4.

### Stage 4 — Build Your Own Home's Structure
Independent of the other home, set this home's clear routines (bedtime, screens, homework, transitions). Predictability here is within your control and matters most. Optionally pair with `parenting_household_constitution_designer.md`.

### Stage 5 — Give the Child the "Two Homes" Frame
Provide age-appropriate language: "Houses have different rules — like school and Grandma's. Here we do X; there they do Y. Both homes love you." Address loyalty binds: the child shouldn't have to argue one home's rules at the other.

### Stage 6 — Handle Re-Entry Friction
Name the common reality that kids re-adjust after transitions (the "re-entry" wobble) and that this is normal, not evidence the other home "ruined" them. Give a calm re-entry routine rather than an interrogation.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Try to make both homes identical | Align what matters; let the rest differ |
| Call the other home "the lenient/bad house" | "Different houses, different rules — both okay" |
| Interrogate the child about the other home's rules | Calm re-entry routine; verify adult matters adult-to-adult |
| Send the child to enforce your rules there | Adults discuss baselines; kids just live |
| Fight over screen-time minutes | Spend energy on medication, safety, sleep |
| Treat re-entry wobble as proof of damage | Normalize re-adjustment; reset gently |
| Use "consistency" to police their parenting | Propose shared minimums; control only your home |
| Make the child argue your rules at the other home | Free the child from the loyalty bind |
| Demand alignment from a non-engaging co-parent | Shift fully to building your own home's structure |
| Label them "permissive" or "rigid" | Describe the specific item, not their character |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Align feeding, nap, and medical routines as much as possible (these affect regulation most); accept that comfort objects and small routines will differ.
- **4–8:** This is the age "two homes, two rules" language lands best; keep a calm re-entry ritual; protect from loyalty binds.
- **9–12:** Kids notice and may "lawyer" the differences ("but Dad lets me…"); hold your home's rules without litigating the other's; align homework expectations.
- **13–18:** Teens increasingly self-regulate across homes; focus alignment on safety (curfew check-ins, driving, substances) and let stylistic differences go.

**By profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Don't expect to negotiate baselines; build your own home's predictability and use parallel-parenting posture (`parenting_coparenting_high_conflict_response_strategy.md`).
- **Child with ADHD/autism:** Routine and medication consistency matter much more here; prioritize aligning structure, transitions, and meds; push harder (neutrally) for those specific minimums.
- **Anxious child:** Predictability within each home is the anxiety treatment; the "both homes are safe and different" frame is reassuring; keep re-entry gentle.
- **Unsafe/absent-parent context:** If a "difference" is actually a safety gap, see Safety Block and `parenting_coparenting_with_unsafe_or_absent_parent.md`.
