**Purpose:** Give a parent ready, honest, age-sized answers to the questions children ask before, during, and after a separation — so they're not caught flat-footed in the car, at bedtime, or mid-meltdown. The output is a personalized answer bank: sample language for each recurring question, tuned to the child's age, that tells the truth without blaming the other parent, making false promises, or dumping adult detail.

**When to use:** Right after telling the kids and for the months that follow, when the questions keep coming; when a specific question keeps catching you off guard; when you and the co-parent want consistent answers so the child hears the same thing in both homes; when a child asks something loaded ("Did Daddy do something bad?") and you need words that are honest but protective.

**When NOT to use:** The question reveals a safety issue (a child describing something frightening or harmful — see Safety Block); you want help deciding *whether* to divorce or legal/custody answers (wrong tool — see custody section).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and shift pathways if a child's question reveals harm:
- The child asks something that discloses abuse or describes being hurt ("Why does Daddy hit?") → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911. Don't smooth it over with a reassuring script.
- The child expresses they don't want to be alive, or asks questions about death/self-harm in a worrying way → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.
- A question reveals domestic violence in the home → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US).

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles

1. **Answer the feeling, then the question.** Most hard questions are feelings wearing a question's clothes. "Will you get back together?" is usually "I'm scared and sad." Validate first ("I know you really wish that"), then answer honestly.
2. **True, kind, and small.** Tell the truth, never a lie — but in the smallest honest dose the child's age can hold. You can be honest without being complete.
3. **No blame, ever — even when there's a 'bad guy.'** Children are made of both parents; running one down damages the child's sense of self. Even when one parent behaved badly, you can be honest about behavior at the child's level without character assassination.
4. **No false promises.** Don't promise reconciliation, or that "nothing will change," or that you'll "never be sad." False comfort breaks trust when reality arrives.
5. **The reasons are the adults' business.** "Why" gets a real but child-sized answer ("we couldn't be happy living together"), not the affair, the money, or the betrayal. Adult detail is a burden, not honesty.
6. **It's okay not to have the answer.** "I don't know yet, and I'll tell you as soon as I do" is honest and steadying. You don't have to perform certainty you don't have.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Validate the feeling before answering the question.
- Give age-tiered sample wording for each question.
- Tell the truth at the child's level — no lies, no false hope.
- Keep adult reasons private; answer "why" in child-sized terms.
- Offer an honest "I don't know yet" for genuinely open questions.

**Must Not:**
- Badmouth, blame, or characterize the other parent ("because your dad cheated").
- Make the child a messenger ("ask your mother") or interrogate them via the answer.
- Promise reconciliation or that nothing will change.
- Overshare adult detail (affairs, finances, mental health, legal strategy).
- Make the child responsible for an adult's feelings or for keeping a secret.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Gather the Questions and the Truths
List the questions the child is actually asking (or likely to). For each, note what's true and shareable at this child's level, and what stays private. Mark anything not yet decided.

### Stage 2 — Set the Two Filters
For every answer, run it through: *(a) Is it true?* (no lies) and *(b) Is it kind and small?* (no blame, no adult detail, no false promise). If an answer fails either filter, reshape it.

### Stage 3 — Build the Answer Bank (tiered)
For each question, draft validate-then-answer sample language at the child's tier. Below are starting points the parent adapts to their truth:

**"Why are you getting divorced?"**
- 4–8: "Sometimes grown-ups who used to be happy together can't be happy living together anymore, even when they try hard. It's a grown-up reason, and it's not about you."
- 9–12: "We tried for a long time, and we couldn't fix the part of being married that wasn't working. The details are grown-up stuff between us, but I promise it's nothing you did."
- 13–18: "We're not going to get into the private reasons — that's between us — but it wasn't sudden, and we both think it's the right call even though it's hard."

**"Is it my fault?"**
- All ages: "No. Absolutely not. Nothing you did, said, or thought caused this, and nothing you can do can change it. This is a grown-up decision and it's our job, not yours." (Repeat as often as asked.)

**"Will you get back together?"**
- "I can hear how much you wish that, and that makes total sense. But no — we're not getting back together. We'll both always be your parents and we'll both always love you, and that part never changes." (No "maybe.")

**"Do you still love each other?"**
- "We care about each other and we'll always be your parents together. The married kind of love changed, and that's why we're living apart. But the love we both have for you is not going anywhere."

**"Who will I live with?"**
- If known: "You'll have a home with me and a home with [other parent]. Here's roughly how it'll work…" If not: "We're still working that out, and we'll tell you the plan as soon as we know. You won't have to choose — that's our job, not yours."

**"Did Daddy/Mommy do something bad?"**
- Without harm: "This isn't about anyone being a bad person. Grown-ups can be good parents and still not be able to stay married."
- With real harm, age-appropriately honest, no character assassination: "Some grown-up things happened that aren't okay, and that's part of why we can't live together. You don't have to carry that — it's the grown-ups' to handle. You're allowed to love [parent] and it's okay that this is confusing."

**"Where will I sleep / what about my pet / my room / my friends?"**
- Answer concretely with what's known; "still figuring out" with a promise to update for what isn't.

### Stage 4 — Plan the Repeats and the Curveballs
Note which questions will recur (especially "is it my fault" and "will you get back together") and that you'll answer patiently each time. Prepare for the curveball asked at a bad moment — it's okay to say "That's a really good question. Can we talk about it tonight when we have time?" and then actually do it.

### Stage 5 — Coordinate (if possible)
Where you and the co-parent can agree on consistent answers (especially "why" and "will you get back together"), do — the child is steadier hearing one story in both homes.

```markdown
# My Answer Bank — [Child name, age]

## Ground Rules I'm Keeping
- Validate feeling first, then answer.
- True + kind + small. No blame, no false promises, no adult detail.

## The Questions
### "Why?"
- My answer: "..."
### "Is it my fault?"
- My answer: "..." (repeat patiently every time)
### "Will you get back together?"
- My answer: "..." (honest no; no "maybe")
### "Do you still love each other?"
- My answer: "..."
### "Who will I live with?"
- My answer: "..." [or honest "still working it out"]
### "Did [parent] do something bad?"
- My answer: "..." (no character attack)
### [Other recurring question]
- My answer: "..."

## For the Curveball at a Bad Moment
- Holding line: "Great question — let's really talk tonight." (then do it)

## Coordinated with co-parent?
- [Which answers we've agreed to keep consistent]
```

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Because your dad cheated on me" | "It's a grown-up reason between us, and not your fault" |
| "Maybe we'll get back together someday" | "No — and we'll both always love you" (honest, kind) |
| "Nothing is going to change" | Name what's known; "still figuring out" for the rest |
| "Ask your mother why" | Answer it yourself; don't bounce the child |
| "Your father is a bad person" | "Good parents can still not be able to stay married" |
| Lie to make the moment easier | True, kind, and small — never a lie |
| Demand the child stop asking | Answer "is it my fault" patiently, every time |
| Answer a hard one mid-rush, badly | "Great question — tonight, properly" (then deliver) |
| Make the child keep your secret | Adults hold adult business; no secrets to carry |
| Dump the legal/financial fight on them | Keep adult logistics out of the child's answer |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** They don't ask "why"; they ask through behavior. The "answer" is routine, calm, and short repeated phrases ("two homes, both love you").
- **4–8:** Expect literal, repeated questions and self-blame; over-deliver the "not your fault" answer; keep it concrete and brief.
- **9–12:** They want fairness and a real "why"; give honest, blame-free reasons and acknowledge it's unfair; protect them from the "pick a side" pull.
- **13–18:** They may probe for adult detail or test your honesty; you can acknowledge there are private reasons without disclosing them, and respect their maturity without making them a confidant.

**By profile:**
- **Autistic child:** Literal answers; avoid metaphor ("we grew apart" confuses); a written version of key answers helps; expect the same question repeatedly as processing, not defiance.
- **ADHD child:** Short answers; repeat across many small moments rather than one big talk.
- **Anxious child:** Anchor answers in certainty and continuity; resist the urge to over-reassure with promises you can't keep; the honest, steady answer calms more than the rosy one.
- **High-conflict / real-harm situation:** Keep answers scrupulously non-blaming even when it's hard; "behaviors, not character" is the line; never recruit the child against the other parent. If harm is involved, see Safety Block and route accordingly.
