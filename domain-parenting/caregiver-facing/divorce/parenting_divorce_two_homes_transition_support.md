**Purpose:** Help a parent ease the emotional experience of a child living across two homes — not the legal schedule (that lives in the custody set), but the felt reality: the gut-drop at an exchange, the "I miss Daddy" sob at bedtime, the prickly reentry when a child comes back wired, and the long project of making *both* places feel like home. The output is a personalized transition-support plan with rituals, scripts, and reentry strategies.

**When to use:** When two homes are starting or already in place and the child struggles with going back and forth; when exchanges are tearful, clingy, or volatile; when a child says they miss the other parent and you don't know what to say; when one home feels like "home" and the other like "visiting"; when reentry after time at the other parent's is consistently rocky.

**When NOT to use:** You need to build or change the custody schedule itself (route to the custody set); the difficulty is actually about safety at one home (see Safety Block); the friction is driven by adult conflict at exchanges (pair with the shield-from-conflict prompt — the fix is upstream).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A child resists or fears going to one home in a way that suggests harm, not just sadness — describes being hurt, frightened, or unsafe there → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911. Do not "ritual" a child past a genuine safety signal.
- Exchanges involve threats, violence, or a protective-order situation → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US); design exchanges for safety with an advocate/counsel.
- A child becomes hopeless or talks about not wanting to be alive around transitions → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles

1. **Transitions are mini-grief, not misbehavior.** Each move between homes re-touches the loss. The clinginess, tears, or attitude at an exchange is the cost of caring about both parents — meet it with comfort, not correction.
2. **Predictability is the painkiller.** Children settle when they know what's coming. Consistent routines, a known schedule the child can see, and the same comforting things in both homes shrink the anxiety of the back-and-forth.
3. **"I miss the other parent" is a feeling to honor, not a wound to you.** When a child misses your co-parent, the supportive move is to make space for it ("Of course you miss Mom — you can call her") — not to take it personally, compete, or guilt them.
4. **Both homes count as home.** A child does better when each place has their stuff, their space, and their welcome — not a guest room and a duffel bag. You only control your home; make it unmistakably theirs.
5. **Reentry friction is normal and time-limited.** Kids often need a buffer after an exchange — they may be dysregulated, missing the other home, or readjusting. A low-demand landing reduces the blow-up.
6. **Protect transitions from conflict.** The single biggest thing you control is keeping the handoff calm and brief. A child should never have to absorb tension, messages, or interrogation at the doorway.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Frame transition distress as grief/adjustment, not defiance.
- Build concrete, repeatable transition rituals.
- Give scripts for the "I miss the other parent" moment that support the bond.
- Provide a reentry buffer plan.
- Address making each home feel like the child's own.

**Must Not:**
- Coach the parent to compete with, undermine, or badmouth the other home.
- Interrogate the child about the other parent's home or use them as an informant.
- Guilt the child for missing or enjoying the other parent.
- Make the child a messenger at exchanges.
- Promise the schedule will change, or relitigate logistics in front of the child.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Read the Current Transition Pattern
Describe what's actually happening at exchanges and reentry, for each child. Separate three things: anticipatory distress (before), exchange-moment distress (at the doorway), and reentry distress (after). Each has a different fix.

### Stage 2 — Build a Departure Ritual (leaving you)
Design a short, predictable goodbye: a phrase ("See you in three sleeps — I love you to the moon"), a small ritual (a special handshake, a note tucked in the bag), an honest, upbeat tone (no tears-on-the-parent, no "I'll miss you so much I can't stand it," which burdens the child). Keep it brief — lingering prolongs the pain.

### Stage 3 — Build an Arrival Ritual (coming to you)
Design a low-key welcome that doesn't demand the child be instantly happy and grateful: a snack, quiet time, a familiar routine, no grilling about the other home. Let them decompress before connection.

### Stage 4 — Script the "I Miss the Other Parent" Moment
Prepare warm responses that hold the bond: "Of course you miss Dad. You love him and he loves you. Want to call him and say goodnight?" Plan a contact bridge (a goodnight call, a photo, a countdown calendar). Never compete or guilt.

### Stage 5 — Plan the Reentry Buffer
Design the first hours back: low demands, no big questions or chores immediately, extra connection, patience with the crankiness that often means "I'm readjusting." Name a re-grounding routine (favorite dinner, bath, story) that signals "you're home and settled."

### Stage 6 — Make This Home Theirs
Audit your home for belonging: their own bed/space, some clothes and toys that live here (so they're not always packing), photos including the other parent if the child wants them, a say in their space. Aim for "my other home," not "where I visit."

### Stage 7 — Keep the Handoff Clean
Set rules for yourself at exchanges: brief, friendly, no messages passed through the child, no interrogation, no tension. If the co-parent brings conflict, you keep your side calm and route the issue to adult channels (pair with the shield prompt).

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Did you have fun? What did you DO over there?" (grilling) | Low-key welcome; let them decompress first |
| "I missed you SO much, it was awful here" | Upbeat, brief goodbye; don't burden them with your grief |
| "You don't need to call her, you just got here" | "Of course — let's call Mom to say goodnight" |
| Treat reentry crankiness as a discipline problem | Buffer it; it's readjustment, not defiance |
| Keep their stuff in a duffel they repack each time | Give them belongings and space that live here |
| Compete to be the "fun house" / "real home" | Make this home theirs; let both homes be home |
| Pass a message or money through the child | Adults handle adult logistics directly |
| Linger and cry at the doorway | Quick, warm goodbye; lingering prolongs the pain |
| Quiz them to find out about the ex's house | None of that — observe, don't interrogate |
| Promise the schedule will change to fix it | Support the feeling; route schedule issues to adults |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Frequent, shorter transitions are easier than long separations; a transitional object and identical routines in both homes do the heavy lifting; expect fussing — soothe with consistency.
- **4–8:** Concrete countdowns ("two more sleeps"), a transition object that travels, and a visible calendar; expect tears and clinginess; over-reassure that both parents come back.
- **9–12:** Give some control (packing their own bag, choosing what stays in each home); watch for the "helper" who hides their own missing to protect a parent.
- **13–18:** Respect their growing need for stability of friends, devices, and stuff across homes; flexibility matters; involve them in logistics without making them mediate adult issues.

**By profile:**
- **Autistic child:** Transitions are uniquely hard; use visual schedules, advance previews, identical routines, and the same sensory items in both homes; build in extra decompression at reentry; minimize surprises.
- **ADHD child:** Externalize the schedule (visible calendar, packing checklist); expect transition-time dysregulation; keep handoffs brief and predictable.
- **Anxious child:** Predictability and contact bridges (a goodnight call, a photo) reduce anticipatory worry; avoid over-reassurance loops; a countdown gives the worry a container.
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Make exchanges as brief and neutral as possible; never pass messages through the child; if the doorway is volatile, consider a neutral exchange point and pair with the shield/exchange-protocol prompts.
