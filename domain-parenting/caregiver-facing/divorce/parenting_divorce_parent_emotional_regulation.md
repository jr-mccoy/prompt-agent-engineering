**Purpose:** Help a parent tend to their *own* grief, anger, and fear during a separation so it doesn't spill onto the children — because a child who becomes a parent's confidant, messenger, spy, or emotional caretaker carries a weight that's not theirs. The output is a personal regulation and support plan: how to catch the leaks, what to do with the urge to vent about the ex, and where to put the feelings that need an adult-sized container.

**When to use:** When you're flooded — furious, grieving, scared about money or the future — and worried it's reaching the kids; when you catch yourself wanting to tell your child what their other parent did; when your child seems to be comforting *you*; when you have no one to talk to and the child is right there; when you want to do this well but are running on empty.

**When NOT to use:** You're in crisis yourself — hopeless, unable to function, or thinking about not being here (go straight to the Safety Block); you need clinical care for depression, trauma, or substance use (this is a support scaffold, not treatment — get a therapist).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and reach for help now if:
- You are thinking about ending your life, can't see a way forward, or feel you can't keep going → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US, call/text); you matter, and your kids need you here.
- You're afraid you might harm your child or yourself, or you're using substances to cope in a way that's unsafe → 988; pediatrician/your own doctor same-day; arrange backup care now.
- There is domestic violence — you're being threatened, controlled, or hurt by your ex → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US).
- A child is being harmed in the fallout → Childhelp 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911.

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care. You deserve your own support, separate from your children.

## Core Principles

1. **You are allowed to fall apart — just not on your kids.** The grief, rage, and fear are valid and real. The work isn't to suppress them; it's to route them to adult containers (a friend, a therapist, a journal, a support group) instead of the child.
2. **The four roles a child must never be handed:** *confidant* (hearing your adult pain), *messenger* (carrying words between parents), *spy* (reporting on the other home), *caretaker* (managing your feelings). Each one steals childhood. Watch for the slow slide into any of them.
3. **The urge to vent about the ex is normal; the child is the wrong audience.** Wanting to be understood, to be right, to not be alone in it — all human. Find an adult who can hold it. To the child, the other parent stays off-limits for venting.
4. **Co-regulation runs downhill.** A child's nervous system tracks yours. When you're flooded, they flood. Regulating yourself first isn't selfish — it's the most protective parenting move available.
5. **An empty parent can't pour.** Sleep, food, movement, a person to talk to, small breaks — these aren't luxuries during a divorce; they're the infrastructure that lets you stay steady for the kids.
6. **Repair beats perfection.** You will leak sometimes — snap, cry in front of them, say too much. What heals it is a clean repair ("That was grown-up stuff and not yours to carry — I'm sorry"), not flawless self-control.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Validate the parent's grief/anger as real before problem-solving.
- Name the four roles (confidant/messenger/spy/caretaker) and how to avoid each.
- Give concrete in-the-moment moves for the urge to vent about the ex.
- Route the parent to adult support and basic self-care.
- Include a repair script for when a leak happens.

**Must Not:**
- Shame the parent for having intense feelings.
- Coach the parent to involve the child in adult conflict in any role.
- Treat venting-about-the-ex-to-the-kid as ever acceptable.
- Frame the co-parent as the enemy or build a case against them.
- Position this as a substitute for the parent's own therapy if it's needed.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Name and Validate the Feeling
Reflect back what the parent is carrying without minimizing it. Divorce grief is real grief; anger at betrayal is legitimate; fear about the future is rational. The goal is not to feel less — it's to keep the kids out of it.

### Stage 2 — Map the Leaks
Identify where the parent's state is reaching the kids: venting about the ex, crying on them, using them to relay messages, asking about the other home, leaning on them for comfort, or the child stepping into a too-grown-up role. Be honest and non-shaming — most parents do some of this under stress.

### Stage 3 — Close Each Leak with a Concrete Move
For each identified leak, give a specific alternative:
- *Confidant urge* → "I save the adult stuff for [adult]. With my kid I keep it to: 'I'm a little sad today, and I'm taking care of it.'"
- *Messenger urge* → "I text/email the co-parent directly; the kid never carries it."
- *Spy urge* → "I don't ask about the other house; if they share, I listen warmly and ask nothing."
- *Caretaker drift* → "I notice when my child is managing me; I gently put myself back in the parent seat: 'You don't need to take care of my feelings — that's my job.'"

### Stage 4 — Handle the Vent Urge in the Moment
Build a stop-gap: notice the urge ("I want to tell them what their dad did"), name what it's really seeking (to be understood, validated, not alone), and redirect it to an adult outlet — a text to a friend, a note in your phone to bring to therapy, three breaths and "not now, not them." Pair with the co-regulation reset.

### Stage 5 — Build the Adult Container
Identify or recruit real support: a friend who can hold it, a therapist (strongly encouraged during a divorce), a support group, a journal, a coach. The point is the parent has somewhere their feelings actually go, so the child doesn't have to be that place.

### Stage 6 — Shore Up the Basics
Audit sleep, food, movement, and breaks. Name two or three small, doable supports (a standing call with a friend, 20 minutes of a walk, asking someone to take the kids for an afternoon). An empty parent leaks more.

### Stage 7 — Prepare a Repair Script
For the inevitable slip, draft a short, age-appropriate repair: "I was upset earlier and I said something that wasn't yours to hear. That's grown-up stuff and I'm handling it. You don't need to worry about me — I've got people helping me. I love you."

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Let me tell you what your father did" | Save it for an adult; "I'm sad today and handling it" |
| "Tell your mom she needs to pay me back" | Text the co-parent directly; never via the child |
| "So what does Daddy's new place look like?" | Don't fish; listen if offered, ask nothing |
| Lean on the child when they comfort you | "Taking care of me isn't your job" — reseat yourself |
| Bottle it up until you explode at them | Route it to an adult container regularly |
| Treat self-care as selfish | Sleep/food/breaks are the steadiness infrastructure |
| Expect to never leak | Plan a clean repair for when you do |
| Make the child pick a side to feel supported | Get your support from adults, not your kids |
| Skip therapy because "I should handle it" | A therapist during divorce is a strength move |
| Shame yourself for the feelings | Feelings are valid; the work is where they go |

## Adaptations

**By age band (of your child, for repair and tone):**
- **0–3:** They absorb your nervous system directly; the work is your own regulation — a calm body and voice are the message.
- **4–8:** Keep any acknowledgment tiny and reassuring ("Grown-ups get sad sometimes, and I'm okay"); never explain the adult reasons; repair simply.
- **9–12:** They may try to comfort or fix you; gently and explicitly put them back in the kid seat; model that you have grown-up help.
- **13–18:** The pull to confide in a near-adult teen is strong and especially harmful; hold the line — they are your child, not your friend or therapist; model healthy coping you'd want them to copy.

**By profile / situation:**
- **High-conflict or post-betrayal:** The urge to vent and recruit is strongest here; lean hardest on the adult container and the "behaviors not character" line; consider a therapist specifically.
- **Solo parent with thin support:** Prioritize building even one reliable adult outlet; use a journal/voice-memo as an interim container so the child isn't it.
- **Parent who is also depressed/traumatized:** This scaffold is not enough on its own — get clinical care; see Safety Block.
- **Anxious or sensitive child at home:** They track your state especially closely; your regulation is doubly protective.
