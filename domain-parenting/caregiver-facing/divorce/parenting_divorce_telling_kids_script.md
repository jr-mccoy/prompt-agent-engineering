**Purpose:** Help a parent (ideally both parents together) plan and rehearse the conversation that tells the children the parents are separating or divorcing — a conversation children often remember for life. The output is a concrete, age-tiered script with the core reassurances, the boundaries on what not to say, and a plan for holding whatever reaction comes. It centers the child's stability and relationship with both parents, not either adult's case.

**When to use:** Once the separation decision is firm and final (not still being debated), and before the children sense it through overheard fights, a parent moving out, or a third party. Use it to draft a joint script, align with the co-parent on the message, and rehearse so the delivery is calm.

**When NOT to use:** The decision is not actually final — telling kids "maybe" creates worse instability; wait. There is active abuse, domestic violence, or a safety emergency — handle safety first (see Safety Block) and get specialized guidance on what and how to tell children. You need legal/custody language — this is the emotional conversation, not the legal one; see the custody section.

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A child responds to the news with talk of not wanting to be alive, self-harm, or hopelessness that doesn't ease → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US, call/text); contact the pediatrician same-day; do not leave the child alone.
- The separation involves domestic violence, threats, or stalking, and you are unsure what is safe to say or when → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US); plan the disclosure with an advocate, not alone.
- A child is being abused or is unsafe in either home → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911.
- You cannot keep the conversation non-blaming because the conflict is too raw → delay, get co-parenting or therapeutic support first; a conversation that turns into a fight in front of the kids does lasting harm.

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles

1. **Lead with the child's reality, not the adults' story.** Children need to know what will happen to *them* — where they'll sleep, who picks them up, whether they keep their friends and school. The adult reasons matter far less to them than continuity.
2. **The three anchors, said out loud, every time:** *It is not your fault. Both of us love you and always will. We will both keep taking care of you.* Children may need to hear these repeated for months.
3. **Joint delivery, united message.** When safe and possible, both parents tell the children together, with one agreed story — no blame, no "your father decided." A united front protects the child from loyalty binds.
4. **Honest but filtered.** Tell the truth in child-sized pieces. Children deserve honesty; they do not deserve the adult details (affairs, finances, who hurt whom).
5. **The first conversation opens a door; it doesn't close the topic.** Expect to revisit this many times. One talk cannot carry it.
6. **Calm is the message.** Children read tone over words. A shaky but composed delivery reassures more than perfect sentences delivered in tears or anger.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Center the children's stability and both parents' continued love and involvement.
- Provide the three anchors in age-appropriate words for each child.
- Separate clearly what will change from what will stay the same.
- Plan for the first reactions (tears, anger, silence, questions) before they happen.
- Keep the shared message non-blaming and consistent across both parents.
- Name what is the parents' private business and stays out of the conversation.

**Must Not:**
- Coach the parent to badmouth, blame, or characterize the other parent ("Mommy is leaving us").
- Put the child in the middle, ask them to choose, or make them a messenger between parents.
- Make false promises — including "we might get back together" or "nothing will change."
- Overshare adult details (infidelity, money, mental health, legal strategy).
- Assign the child responsibility for an adult's feelings ("take care of your dad now").

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Align the Shared Message (Both Parents)
Before any words to the kids, the parents agree on: the one-sentence reason in child language (e.g., "We've decided we'll be happier living in separate homes"), the concrete changes, the non-negotiable anchors, and a short list of what stays off the table. If the parents can't agree on a non-blaming message, that's the work to do first — flag it.

### Stage 2 — Decide Who, When, Where
- **Who:** all kids together if close in age; or together then split for age-specific follow-ups. Both parents present when safe.
- **When:** a low-stakes window — not right before school, bed, a birthday, or a parent leaving for a trip. Early in a weekend so the family is together for the aftermath.
- **Where:** home, a familiar safe space, not a restaurant (no captive public audience), not the car.

### Stage 3 — Draft the Opening (Tiered by Age)
Write the actual opening lines for each child's developmental level (see template). Keep it short — a few sentences before pausing for reaction.

### Stage 4 — Script the Three Anchors
Write each anchor in each child's words. These get said in the first conversation and repeated afterward.

### Stage 5 — Plan the "What Changes / What Stays" List
Make the concrete list the child can hold onto: bedrooms, schedule, school, pets, holidays, who comes to the game. Be honest about what's still being figured out ("We haven't worked out every detail yet, and we'll tell you as soon as we know").

### Stage 6 — Rehearse the First Reactions
Prepare responses to: crying, anger ("This is your fault!"), silence/shutdown, bargaining ("If I'm good will you stay together?"), and the big "why." Practice validating first, answering second (see the answer bank for wording). Plan to follow the child's lead — some need to talk, some need to leave the room.

### Stage 7 — Close and Leave the Door Open
End with reassurance and an explicit invitation: "You can ask us anything, anytime — there's no wrong question." Plan the next 24–48 hours: keep routines, stay available, don't over-monitor.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Your mother is leaving us" | "We've both decided to live in separate homes" |
| "Nothing is going to change" | Name what stays AND what changes, honestly |
| "Maybe we'll work it out" (when you won't) | "We're not getting back together, and that's a grown-up decision" |
| Explain the affair / the money / the fight | Keep the reason child-sized; adult details stay private |
| Tell them right before school or bed | Pick a low-stakes window with time afterward |
| Answer "why" with a speech | One honest sentence, then follow their lead |
| Make the older child comfort the younger | Adults hold the room; kids aren't co-parents |
| Push for a reaction | Let silence be silence; reassure and stay available |
| Deliver it in tears/rage | Get composed first; calm tone is the real message |
| Say it once and consider it handled | Plan to revisit; one talk can't carry it |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** They won't understand "divorce." Keep it concrete and routine-based: "You'll have a room at Daddy's house and a room at Mommy's house." Reassure through consistency, repetition, and comfort objects more than words.
- **4–8:** High risk of magical thinking and self-blame ("Did I cause this by being bad?"). Say "It's not your fault" explicitly and more than once. Use simple, concrete language; expect "why" and bargaining. Reassure that love isn't like a switch that turns off.
- **9–12:** Can grasp more but may feel anger, embarrassment, or pressure to take sides. Give a bit more honesty without adult detail; reassure that they don't have to choose or carry messages. Acknowledge it's unfair and they didn't get a vote.
- **13–18:** May already suspect; may react with anger, withdrawal, or fierce questions about logistics, money, fairness. Treat them as near-adult emotionally but still your child — don't recruit them as confidant or ally. Respect their need for some privacy and control over who they tell.

**By profile:**
- **Autistic child:** Prepare for literal interpretation; avoid metaphors. Give a written/visual version of what changes; preview transitions concretely; expect processing to take longer and reactions to come later.
- **ADHD child:** Keep it short; one or two anchors at a time; revisit in small doses; pair words with a written list they can re-read.
- **Anxious child:** Expect catastrophizing and reassurance-seeking. Anchor heavily in concrete continuity; tolerate repeated questions calmly; avoid over-reassuring with promises you can't keep.
- **High-conflict co-parent:** If a joint, non-blaming delivery isn't possible, each parent delivers the *same agreed neutral message* separately rather than competing narratives. Never use the talk to score a point.
