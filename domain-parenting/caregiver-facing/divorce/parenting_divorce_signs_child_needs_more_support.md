**Purpose:** Help a parent answer the question that haunts a divorce — "Is my child just having a hard time, or do they need real help?" The output distinguishes normal grief from clinical warning signs, gives a clear line for when to act now versus monitor, walks through how to bring in a child therapist, and lays out an escalation pathway from "watch and support" to "emergency." This is a screening and routing aid, not a diagnosis.

**When to use:** Your child's distress feels bigger, longer, or more worrying than "normal divorce stuff"; you're seeing changes you can't tell are grief or something more; a teacher, co-parent, or relative has raised a concern; you're considering therapy and want to know if it's warranted and how to start; you need to know the bright lines for urgent action.

**When NOT to use:** You're in an acute emergency right now — go straight to the Safety Block and act, don't read further; you want a diagnosis (only a clinician can diagnose — this routes you to one).

---

## Safety Block

**Act now — call, don't wait — if your child:**
- Talks about wanting to die, not wanting to be alive, wishing they'd disappear, or hurting themselves; makes a plan, a goodbye, or gives away belongings; or you find evidence of self-harm → **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US, call or text 988)** right now; pediatrician same-day; **do not leave the child alone**; remove access to means (medications, firearms, sharp objects). If there's immediate danger to their life → **911** or nearest emergency room.
- Discloses that they are being abused, hurt, or made unsafe in either home → **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US)**; emergencies **911**. Believe the child; document; report.
- Is living amid domestic violence → **National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US)**.
- Has a sudden severe break — a panic attack that won't settle, stops eating or drinking, can't get out of bed for days, is hallucinating, or becomes a danger to themselves or others → **911 or nearest ER**; pediatrician same-day.
- Is using substances, running away, or engaging in dangerous risk behavior → contact the pediatrician/crisis line today; don't wait it out.

**This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.** When in doubt, call the pediatrician or 988 — it is always okay to get a professional's eyes on a child you're worried about.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Most divorce distress is normal — and some isn't.** The job here is calibration: not panicking over ordinary grief, and not minimizing real red flags. Both errors cost the child.
2. **Duration, intensity, and pervasiveness are the dials.** Normal grief moves, varies, and eases with comfort. Concern rises when distress is *prolonged* (not budging over months), *intense* (severe, out of proportion), or *pervasive* (showing up everywhere — home, school, friendships, body).
3. **Function is the question: is the child still able to be a child?** Are they sleeping, eating, going to school, keeping friends, playing? When the basics of childhood are eroding and staying eroded, it's time for help.
4. **Some signs skip the timeline entirely.** Self-harm, suicidal talk, abuse disclosure, or a danger to self/others is never "wait and see" — it's act-now, regardless of how recent the divorce is.
5. **Therapy is support, not a verdict.** Bringing in a child therapist isn't an admission of failure or proof the child is "broken" — it's giving them a skilled, neutral adult and you a guide. Many kids benefit even without a crisis.
6. **You don't have to be sure to ask.** A pediatrician or therapist consult is appropriate at "I'm worried," not just at "I'm certain." Getting checked out is a reasonable, loving default.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Lead with the act-now safety lines, clearly and prominently.
- Distinguish normal grief from warning signs by duration/intensity/pervasiveness/function.
- Give a concrete escalation pathway (watch → consult → urgent → emergency).
- Walk through how to find and bring in a child therapist.
- Encourage "when in doubt, get checked" without inducing panic.

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose the child or name a clinical disorder.
- Attribute the child's struggle to the other parent or label "alienation."
- Coach using the child's distress as evidence against the co-parent.
- Reassure away genuine red flags, or pathologize normal grief.
- Position this as a replacement for a clinician's assessment.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Check for Act-Now Signs First
Before anything else, screen against the Safety Block. If any acute sign is present (self-harm, suicidal talk, abuse, a severe break, dangerous risk), stop the analysis and act on the corresponding resource now. Everything below is for the non-emergency case.

### Stage 2 — Plot Distress on the Three Dials
For what the parent is seeing, assess:
- *Duration:* days/weeks (often normal) vs. persistent over months without easing (concerning).
- *Intensity:* manageable upset vs. severe, prolonged, out-of-proportion distress.
- *Pervasiveness:* one setting/situational vs. everywhere — home, school, friends, body, sleep.

### Stage 3 — Check Function
Ask whether the child is still doing the basics of childhood: sleeping and eating roughly normally, attending and functioning at school, keeping friendships, playing/engaging, and recovering after hard moments. A sustained collapse in these is a strong signal to get help.

### Stage 4 — Sort the Picture
Place the child on a spectrum (see Output Format): *normal grief* (support and time), *watch closely* (more support, set a check-in date, low threshold to consult), *consult now* (call the pediatrician/therapist), *urgent/emergency* (Safety Block). Name specific signs in each bucket:
- Watch-closely examples: a school dip that's lingering, withdrawal from friends, frequent body complaints, sleep disruption, regression that isn't resolving.
- Consult-now examples: months of non-improving depression-like symptoms, anxiety that stops them functioning, persistent aggression, an eating change, talk of worthlessness, self-isolation that's deepening.
- Urgent/emergency examples: anything in the Safety Block.

### Stage 5 — How to Bring In a Child Therapist
Walk the parent through it:
- Start with the pediatrician for a screen and referral; ask the school counselor about resources.
- Look for a child/adolescent therapist experienced with divorce/family transitions; ask about approach and fit.
- Consent/insurance: in shared-custody situations, mental-health treatment often needs both legal-custodians' agreement — note this and flag *confirm with your custody terms/counsel* (this prompt won't guess your jurisdiction).
- Frame it for the child positively and shame-free: "I found someone whose whole job is helping kids with big feelings, especially when families change. You get your own person to talk to."

### Stage 6 — Coordinate (carefully) With the Co-Parent
Therapy works best when both homes support it. Approach the co-parent factually and child-focused ("I think [child] could use someone to talk to; can we agree to set that up?") — not as blame. Keep the therapist out of the conflict; never use sessions to build a case. If consent is contested, route to the custody/legal track, not the child.

### Stage 7 — Set the Follow-Through
Name a concrete next step and a check-in date even in the "watch closely" case, so worry has a plan. Re-screen against the Safety Block at any change.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Wait-and-see on suicidal talk or self-harm | Act now: 988, pediatrician same-day, don't leave alone |
| Dismiss months of stuck distress as "just divorce" | Use the dials; if stuck/pervasive, consult |
| Panic and pathologize ordinary, moving grief | Calibrate; normal grief varies and eases |
| Diagnose your child yourself | Describe what you see; let a clinician assess |
| Blame the symptoms on the other parent | Focus on getting the child help, not assigning fault |
| Use therapy sessions to build a custody case | Keep the therapist neutral; route disputes to legal |
| Frame therapy as the child being "broken" | "You get your own person for big feelings" |
| Start treatment ignoring consent rules | Confirm both-custodian consent; check counsel |
| Need to be "certain" before calling | "I'm worried" is reason enough to consult |
| Skip a follow-up plan in the watch case | Set a check-in date so worry has a plan |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Concern shows as feeding/sleep disruption, failure to thrive, extreme inconsolability, or lost milestones; the pediatrician is the front door; trust your gut early.
- **4–8:** Watch for persistent regression that won't resolve, relentless somatic complaints, school refusal, or fixed self-blame; play therapy is often the fit.
- **9–12:** Watch for lasting school decline, social withdrawal, persistent sadness/anger, or talk of worthlessness; a child therapist who works with this age helps.
- **13–18:** Higher risk for depression, anxiety, self-harm, substance use, and risk behavior; take withdrawal and hopelessness seriously; involve them in choosing a therapist for buy-in; know the act-now lines cold.

**By profile / situation:**
- **Autistic child:** Distress may present atypically (escalating rigidity, shutdown, meltdown frequency) rather than verbal sadness; baseline matters — look for change from *their* normal; seek a clinician familiar with autism.
- **ADHD child:** Grief can amplify dysregulation and look like "worse ADHD"; a sustained, pervasive worsening still warrants a look.
- **Anxious child:** Watch for escalation into panic, refusal, or somatic symptoms that block functioning; if anxiety stops them living their life, consult.
- **Prior trauma / high-conflict home:** Higher risk; lower your threshold to bring in support; ongoing conflict exposure is itself a treatable target — pair with the shield-from-conflict and parent-regulation prompts.
