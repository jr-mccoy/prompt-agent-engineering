**Purpose:** Help a parent understand what they're seeing in their child after a separation — to read a tantrum, a clingy phase, a sullen teenager, or a too-perfect "I'm fine" child as the grief responses they usually are, to know the rough arc of adjustment, and to tell ordinary hard from genuinely concerning. The output is a calm, by-age map the parent can hold, plus a clear line for when to seek more help.

**When to use:** In the weeks and months after telling the children; when a child's behavior changes and you're unsure if it's "normal divorce stuff" or something more; when siblings of different ages are reacting very differently and you need to make sense of both; when a relative or co-parent is alarmed and you want a developmental frame.

**When NOT to use:** You're seeing acute warning signs right now — self-harm, talk of not wanting to live, abuse disclosure (go straight to the Safety Block and `parenting_divorce_signs_child_needs_more_support.md`); you want a diagnosis (this guide describes patterns, it does not diagnose — that's a clinician's job).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and act now — do not wait to "see if it passes" — if a child:
- Talks about not wanting to be alive, wishing they were dead, or hurting themselves; or you find evidence of self-harm → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US, call/text); pediatrician same-day; do not leave the child alone.
- Discloses abuse, or you suspect a child is being hurt in either home → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911.
- Is in a home where domestic violence is occurring → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US).
- Shows a sudden, severe change — stops eating, can't get out of bed, has a panic attack that won't settle, becomes a danger to themselves or others → 911 or nearest ER; pediatrician same-day.

This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles

1. **Most reactions are grief, not misbehavior or pathology.** Divorce is a loss. Children grieve in behavior more than in words — through play, sleep, appetite, mood, body complaints, and conduct. Reading it as grief changes how you respond.
2. **The age of the child shapes the reaction more than the temperament.** A 4-year-old and a 14-year-old can experience the same separation completely differently. Knowing the developmental lens prevents over- and under-reacting.
3. **Regression is communication, not backsliding.** A potty-trained child wetting again, an older child wanting to co-sleep, a teen acting younger — these are bids for security under stress, usually temporary, and met best with patience, not pressure.
4. **There is a rough timeline, but no schedule.** Many children show the hardest stretch in the first months and gradually steady over a year or more, often with flare-ups at transitions, holidays, and milestones. "Not over it yet" at six months is usually normal.
5. **The 'too-fine' child also needs attention.** A child who shows nothing, becomes a tiny adult, or works overtime to keep everyone happy can be struggling underneath. Quiet is not always coping.
6. **Your steadiness is the variable you control.** Children adjust best when at least one parent stays regulated, keeps routines, and protects them from conflict. The research throughline isn't "divorce harms kids" — it's that *conflict and instability* harm kids.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Map expected reactions to each child's developmental stage.
- Distinguish normal grief from signs that warrant more support.
- Frame regression and acting-out as communication.
- Give a realistic adjustment timeline with flare-up points named.
- Flag the "too-fine" presentation as worth watching.

**Must Not:**
- Diagnose the child or label a clinical condition.
- Attribute the child's reaction to the other parent or call it "alienation."
- Coach the parent to use the child's reaction as evidence against the co-parent.
- Make the child a confidant or interrogate them about the other home to "assess" them.
- Pathologize normal grief or, conversely, dismiss real warning signs.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Establish Baseline and Timeline
Note what this child was like before the separation and how long it's been. Reactions are read against the child's own baseline, and against time-since-event (early months look different from a year on).

### Stage 2 — Locate the Child's Developmental Lens
Identify each child's stage and the typical reaction pattern (below). Match what the parent is seeing to the stage-typical picture before concluding anything.

**0–3 (infants/toddlers):** No concept of divorce. React to disruption in routine and caregiver stress: more crying, clinginess, sleep and feeding changes, regression in milestones. Soothe through consistency, predictable caregivers, calm tone.

**4–8 (early childhood):** Magical thinking → self-blame ("I caused it"). Common: tantrums, clinginess, regression (bedwetting, baby talk, separation anxiety), worry about being abandoned, somatic complaints (tummy aches), reunion fantasies, asking the same questions repeatedly. Needs concrete reassurance, repeated, plus routine.

**9–12 (middle childhood):** Better understanding, stronger feelings. Common: sadness, anger, loyalty conflicts, embarrassment, feeling pressure to fix it or take sides, school dip, somatic complaints, becoming "the helper" or the rule-follower. Needs honesty without adult detail, permission not to choose, and protection from the middle.

**13–18 (adolescence):** Cognitively adult, emotionally still developing. Common: anger (often at the parent seen as "at fault"), withdrawal, acting-out, testing limits, premature independence, anxiety about money/college, blurred lines (a parent leaning on them). Some relief if the home was high-conflict. Needs respect, boundaries, and not to be made a confidant or ally.

### Stage 3 — Interpret the Specific Behaviors
Translate what the parent is seeing into likely meaning: regression = security-seeking; anger = grief with nowhere to go; clinginess = fear of further loss; "I'm fine"/caretaking = self-protection or over-responsibility; school dip = cognitive load of stress. Name it without diagnosing it.

### Stage 4 — Place It on the Timeline
Locate the reaction in time: acute (first weeks — intense, raw), adjustment (first months — uneven, flaring at transitions), stabilizing (toward a year — steadier with grief that resurfaces at milestones). Name predictable flare points: exchanges, the other parent's absence, holidays, birthdays, a new partner, anniversaries.

### Stage 5 — Sort Normal-Hard from Needs-More
Use the comparison (see Output Format). Normal grief moves, varies, and responds to comfort. Concerning patterns are *persistent, worsening, pervasive across settings, or dangerous.* When in doubt, route to `parenting_divorce_signs_child_needs_more_support.md`.

### Stage 6 — Match the Response to the Reading
For each child, give the parent two or three concrete moves: what to keep doing (routine, availability, validation), what to add (extra connection at transitions, a feelings vocabulary, a check-in ritual), and what to watch.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "He's acting out because of his mother" | Read behavior as grief; keep the co-parent out of it |
| Call a tantrum a "disorder" | Name it as stage-typical grief unless signs say otherwise |
| Punish regression (bedwetting, clinginess) | Meet it with patience; it's a bid for security |
| Assume the quiet "fine" kid is coping | Gently check in; quiet can hide struggle |
| Expect them to be "over it" in weeks | Hold a months-to-a-year arc with flare-ups |
| Interrogate the child to assess them | Observe, stay available, let them come to you |
| Dismiss real warning signs as "just divorce" | Use the needs-more line; route to the signs prompt |
| Use the child's distress as evidence vs. the ex | Focus on supporting the child, not building a case |
| Treat all siblings' reactions as identical | Read each child by their own age and baseline |
| Decide it's clinical on your own | Describe patterns; let a clinician diagnose |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Watch sleep, feeding, and milestone regression; the intervention is adult calm and routine, not talking.
- **4–8:** Expect self-blame and repeated questions; over-deliver "not your fault" and concrete reassurance; regression is normal and temporary.
- **9–12:** Watch for loyalty binds and the "helper/fixer" role; protect them from the middle; give honesty without adult detail.
- **13–18:** Distinguish healthy independence from withdrawal-as-distress; respect privacy; never recruit them as confidant; watch risk behaviors.

**By profile:**
- **Autistic child:** Disruption and transition changes hit hard; expect reactions to show as routine-rigidity, meltdowns, or shutdown rather than verbal grief; preview changes visually; allow longer processing.
- **ADHD child:** Stress amplifies dysregulation, impulsivity, and emotional swings; behavior may look like "worse ADHD" — it's often grief loading onto an already-busy system.
- **Anxious child:** Expect somatic complaints, reassurance-seeking, and catastrophizing; anchor in predictability; avoid over-promising.
- **High-conflict situation:** The strongest predictor of poor adjustment is ongoing conflict, not the divorce itself; if reactions are severe, look hard at the child's exposure to conflict and route to the shield-from-conflict prompt.
