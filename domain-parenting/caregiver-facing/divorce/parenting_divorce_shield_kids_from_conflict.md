**Purpose:** Help a parent protect their children from the single biggest predictor of harm in divorce — ongoing adult conflict the child is caught inside. The output is a personalized plan to dissolve loyalty binds, hold a no-disparagement line even under provocation, stop interrogating the child about the other home, and respond steadily when the other parent badmouths you to the kids.

**When to use:** When the child is being put (or putting themselves) in the middle; when you catch yourself wanting to run the other parent down or fish for information; when the co-parent is badmouthing you and you don't know how to respond without making it worse; when the child seems torn, secretive, or burdened by loyalty; whenever you want to lower the conflict the child is exposed to.

**When NOT to use:** The "conflict" is actually abuse or a safety threat to the child (see Safety Block — that's not conflict to neutralize, it's danger to act on); you want to document or litigate the co-parent's behavior (route to custody/legal and a communication log, not this emotional-support tool).

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- The other parent is harming, threatening, or endangering the child (not merely badmouthing) → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911. Don't "stay neutral" about genuine danger.
- There is domestic violence directed at you or the child → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US); safety planning comes before any "keep it civil" strategy.
- The child is showing signs they can't cope with the conflict — hopelessness, talk of not wanting to live, self-harm → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

Note: This prompt does not diagnose, label, or coach toward proving "parental alienation." Persistent, harmful badmouthing is real and worth addressing through documentation and professionals — but the label is a clinical/legal determination, not something to assert or weaponize. This prompt is educational support, not a substitute for clinical care.

## Core Principles

1. **Conflict, not divorce, is what harms children.** Kids do well across two homes when they're kept out of the fight. The most protective thing you can do is lower the conflict your child is exposed to — even when the other parent won't.
2. **A child is made of both parents.** Disparaging the other parent doesn't land as "Mom is bad" — it lands as "half of me is bad." No matter how justified your anger, the badmouthing wounds the child, not the ex.
3. **Loyalty binds are silent and crushing.** When a child feels they must choose, defend one parent to the other, or hide their love for one, they carry an impossible weight. The fix is permission: "You can love both of us, fully, out loud."
4. **You only control your side — and that's enough to matter.** You can't make the other parent stop. You *can* be the calm, non-disparaging, non-interrogating home. One such home measurably protects the child.
5. **Don't fight fire with fire.** When the co-parent badmouths you, retaliating drags the child deeper. The protective move is to not counter-attack, to reassure the child of your love, and to avoid defending yourself by running the other parent down.
6. **Curiosity isn't license to interrogate.** Wanting to know about the other home is natural; pumping the child for information makes them a spy and a battleground. Listen if they share; ask nothing that's really about you.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Center the child's freedom to love both parents.
- Give a concrete no-disparagement practice the parent can hold under provocation.
- Provide a no-interrogation listening stance.
- Script a steady response to the other parent badmouthing you.
- Name loyalty binds and how to release them.

**Must Not:**
- Coach disparaging, undermining, or "counter-badmouthing" the other parent.
- Encourage interrogating the child or using them as a spy or messenger.
- Diagnose or label "parental alienation."
- Coach building a case against the co-parent through the child.
- Confuse a genuine safety threat with ordinary conflict to "neutralize."

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Find Where the Child Is in the Middle
Map the specific ways this child gets caught: carrying messages, choosing whose event to attend, defending one parent to the other, keeping secrets between homes, comforting a parent, or reporting on the other house. Name each one — you can only release what you can see.

### Stage 2 — Take the No-Disparagement Pledge (with teeth)
Commit to a concrete rule: in front of or within earshot of the child, the other parent is spoken of neutrally or not at all. Build replacement lines for the moments you'd usually vent:
- Instead of "Your mother is so irresponsible" → "You and Mom have your own time; that's between you two and her."
- Instead of trashing a decision → "Different houses do things differently, and that's okay."
Route the real venting to an adult (pair with the regulation prompt).

### Stage 3 — Release the Loyalty Bind
Give the child explicit permission, repeatedly: "You can love Mom and Dad both, all the way, and that makes me happy, not sad. You never have to choose. You never have to take sides. Loving one of us is never being disloyal to the other." Stop any behavior that implies they must choose (sulking when they're excited to see the other parent, competing for "favorite").

### Stage 4 — Adopt the No-Interrogation Stance
Practice listening without fishing: if the child shares about the other home, respond warmly and neutrally ("Sounds like you had fun") and ask nothing investigative. Drop the questions that are really about you ("Is anyone new there?" "What did she say about me?"). If you need information, get it from the co-parent directly.

### Stage 5 — Script the Response to Being Badmouthed
When the child reports the other parent said something bad about you, resist defending yourself by attacking back. Use a calm, bond-protecting script: "I'm sorry you heard that — it sounds like it felt yucky. Grown-ups sometimes say things when they're upset that they wish they hadn't. What I know is that I love you, and I'm not going to say bad things about your mom. You don't have to figure out who's right." Reassure; don't recruit.

### Stage 6 — Decide What's Conflict vs. What's Harm
Sort ordinary high-conflict (badmouthing, rigidity, sniping) — handled with the above plus documentation and professionals — from genuine harm or danger (Safety Block). Don't neutralize danger; don't escalate ordinary conflict.

### Stage 7 — When to Bring in Help
If the child is persistently distressed, if badmouthing is relentless, or if you're struggling to hold the line, route to a child therapist, a co-parenting counselor, a parenting coordinator, or (for documentation) the custody/legal track — not to recruiting the child.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Your dad is so selfish, isn't he?" | "That's between you and Dad" — neutral or nothing |
| Counter-badmouth when they badmouth you | "I won't say bad things about Mom; I love you" |
| "What did your mother say about me?" | Ask nothing investigative; listen if offered |
| Sulk when they're excited to see the other parent | "I'm so glad you have a great time with Dad" |
| Make the child carry "tell your father..." | Message the co-parent directly |
| Quiz the child to gather evidence | Get information from adults; protect the child |
| Label the other parent an "alienator" to the child | Drop the label; reassure the child of your love |
| Defend yourself by attacking the ex | Reassure the child without recruiting them |
| Treat real danger as mere "conflict" | Act on safety; see Safety Block |
| Recruit the child onto your "side" | There are no sides for the child to be on |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** They absorb tension through tone; the work is keeping handoffs and your voice calm — not words.
- **4–8:** Magical thinking means they take loyalty pressure to heart; over-give explicit permission to love both; keep it concrete.
- **9–12:** Prime age for loyalty binds and the "fixer" role; name the bind out loud and release it; watch for secret-keeping.
- **13–18:** May form strong opinions and want to "side" with one parent; resist enlisting them even when they offer; let them love both without rewarding allegiance to you.

**By profile / situation:**
- **Autistic child:** Loyalty binds and mixed messages confuse deeply; be explicit, consistent, and literal: "You can love both. Both is allowed."
- **Anxious child:** Conflict exposure spikes anxiety; predictability and reassurance of both bonds calm; shield them especially hard.
- **High-conflict co-parent:** You can't control their disparagement; double down on your own calm home, document patterns separately (not via the child), and bring in a co-parenting professional.
- **Persistent, harmful badmouthing:** Keep reassuring the child and holding your line; route documentation and concern to a therapist and the legal/custody track — without making the child your witness.
