# When a Child (6–12) Talks About Dying, Suicide, or Hurting Themselves

**Objective:** Help a caregiver respond safely and calmly in the first minutes, hours, and
days after a school-age child talks about wanting to die or hurts themselves — so the child
is kept safe, the home is made safer, a professional assesses the child promptly, and the
child learns that talking about hard feelings brings help, not trouble.

**When to Use:**
- A child aged 6–12 says "I want to die," "I wish I was never born," "I'm going to kill
  myself," or "everyone would be better off without me" — calmly or in a rage.
- The child writes or draws about dying, searches how to die, gives belongings away, or
  has hurt themselves on purpose (head-hitting, scratching, cutting, swallowing pills).
- A teacher, counsellor, or friend's parent reports something the child said.
- **Not this prompt if** the young person is 13–18 and the issue is non-suicidal
  self-injury — use `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-13-18/parenting_teen_self_harm_signal_response.md`
  (and `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-13-18/parenting_teen_mental_health_signal_check.md`
  for broader teen mood concerns).
  Clinicians building the safety plan with the family use
  `domain-psychology/populations/child-adolescent/psychology_pediatric_safety_planning_with_caregiver.md`.
  Talk of wanting to be with someone who died → also use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/transitions-events/parenting_child_grief_after_death.md`
  after safety is addressed. **This prompt is the caregiver's first response for a
  6–12-year-old.**

## Safety Block — Act Now

- **The child has tried to hurt or kill themselves, has swallowed pills or anything
  harmful, is injured, or you cannot keep them safe right now** → call 911 (US) or your
  local emergency number. For a possible overdose or poisoning with no immediate danger
  signs, call Poison Control 1-800-222-1222 (US) while arranging care. Do not wait to see
  if symptoms appear.
- **The child has a plan, a method, a time, or access to a method, or says they intend to
  act** → do not leave them alone, even in the bathroom; call 988 (US Suicide & Crisis
  Lifeline, call or text; can connect to a mobile crisis team) or go to the emergency
  department now.
- **Any other talk of wanting to die or of suicide** → take it seriously at every age.
  Contact the pediatrician or the child's mental-health clinician the same day for an
  assessment; call 988 for guidance if you are unsure.
- **Firearms in the home** → move them out of the home today (a trusted relative, a gun
  shop or law-enforcement storage where available). If that is truly impossible, lock them
  unloaded with ammunition locked separately and keys or codes the child cannot get.
- **Outside the US:** Canada 9-8-8 (call or text) and Kids Help Phone 1-800-668-6868
  (text 686868); UK Samaritans 116 123 and Childline 0800 1111; Australia Kids Helpline
  1800 55 1800 and Lifeline 13 11 14; elsewhere, findahelpline.com. Emergencies: 911,
  999, 112, or 000 as local.

**This guidance supports a caregiver's response; it does not assess risk or replace a
clinician.** Only a trained professional can assess suicide risk.

## Inputs / Context

1. **What happened:** exact words or actions, when, to whom, the situation (calm,
   during a meltdown, after a conflict, after bullying, after a loss).
2. **Child:** age, any diagnoses (ADHD, autism, anxiety, depression), current treatment,
   recent changes in sleep, eating, play, school, friendships.
3. **History:** previous statements or self-harm, family history of suicide or mental
   illness, recent losses, bullying (in person or online), abuse or trauma.
4. **Home:** firearms, medications (prescription and over-the-counter), alcohol, ropes
   and cords, sharp objects, the child's device access.
5. **Supports:** pediatrician, therapist, school counsellor, other caregivers; ADHD or
   impulsivity makes means safety even more important.

## Method

1. **Stay calm and stay with the child (NE-07).** Lower your voice, sit at their level.
   "Thank you for telling me. I'm really glad you did. I'm going to help." Don't show
   shock or anger, don't lecture, and don't promise to keep it secret.
2. **Ask directly — it does not put the idea in their head.** Research shows asking about
   suicide does not increase risk. Use simple words:
   - "Are you thinking about killing yourself / making yourself dead?"
   - "Have you thought about how you would do it?"
   - "Have you ever tried to hurt yourself or make yourself dead before?"
   - "Do you feel like you might do something to hurt yourself now?"
   Write down the answers in the child's words.
3. **Decide the next step against the triggers (AG-19, DP-05).**
   - **Now (minutes):** attempt, injury, ingestion, plan, intent, or you cannot keep them
     safe → 911 / emergency department / 988.
   - **Today:** any suicidal statement without the above → same-day call to the
     pediatrician or clinician; ask for an assessment today or tomorrow; 988 for advice.
   - **Statements in a rage** ("I'll kill myself if you take my tablet") → still ask the
     questions once the child is calm, and still arrange the assessment. Many children who
     later act first said it in anger.
4. **Secure means the same day (DP-04).** Must-nots: no firearm accessible in the home;
   no medications unlocked (lock all, including acetaminophen/paracetamol, ibuprofen,
   vitamins with iron, and sleep aids — dispose of old ones); no alcohol accessible; put
   away cords, belts, and ropes the child has mentioned or searched; secure sharp objects.
   Ask other homes the child visits about firearms and medications.
5. **Supervise closely until assessed.** An adult nearby at all times, including
   overnight (door open, sleeping in the same room if needed). Keep devices in shared
   spaces and check search history for method searches; this is safety, not punishment.
6. **Get the assessment.** Bring your notes. Ask: How serious is the risk? What is the
   plan for the next 72 hours? Who do we call at night? Will you help us write a safety
   plan? What follow-up is arranged? (QA-04: let the professional judge risk; do not try
   to rate it yourself.)
7. **Inform the school, with the clinician's input.** The counsellor or designated
   safeguarding lead needs to know so they can watch, support, and tell you if anything
   happens at school. Ask how they will handle it discreetly.
8. **Look after the child's whole day.** Keep routines, sleep, food, and gentle
   connection; spend some daily one-to-one time; avoid harsh consequences for the
   behaviour that came before the statement.
9. **Follow up.** Keep the safety plan (written with the clinician) where the child and
   adults can see it; keep means secured for the long term, not just for a week; watch
   for warning signs after a hospital visit, the first days back at school, and
   anniversaries of losses.

## Output Format

```
# Safety Response — [child, age]   Date/time: [..]

## What was said or done (child's words)
## Answers to the direct questions (thoughts, plan, past attempt, intent now)
## Decision: NOW (911/ED/988) | TODAY (same-day assessment) — and why
## Means secured (item → where, done time)
## Supervision until assessed (who, where, overnight plan)
## Assessment: who, when, questions to ask
## School notification (who, what, when)
## Ongoing: safety plan location, follow-up appointments, warning signs to watch
## Crisis numbers on the fridge and in every adult's phone
```

## Verification

- [ ] The direct questions were asked and answers recorded in the child's words.
- [ ] The decision is matched to explicit NOW/TODAY triggers.
- [ ] Firearms and all medications are secured the same day with times recorded.
- [ ] Continuous supervision is arranged until a professional has assessed the child.
- [ ] An assessment is booked or under way; the plan does not rate risk itself.
- [ ] Crisis numbers for the family's country are listed.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "Too young to mean it" | Dismissing a 7-year-old's statement | Children under 12 do die by suicide; always ask and assess. |
| "Just attention-seeking" | Ignoring repeated statements | A child who needs attention this way needs help; assess. |
| Avoiding the word | "You're not thinking of doing anything silly?" | Ask plainly; vague questions get vague answers. |
| Promising secrecy | "I won't tell anyone" | Promise help, not secrecy. |
| Means as an afterthought | Pills still in the bathroom cabinet | Secure means the same day, for the long term. |
| Parent rating the risk | "She seems fine now, so we'll skip the doctor" | Only a professional assesses risk; calm can follow a decision to act. |
| Punishing the behaviour first | Grounding for the meltdown that included the statement | Safety and connection first. |

## Adaptations

- **Autistic child:** may say things very literally or flatly; take them at their
  word; may need visual supports to answer the questions.
- **Bullying or online harassment involved:** report it to the school the same day and
  preserve evidence; see `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-9-12/parenting_bullying_target_response_protocol.md`.
- **LGBTQ+ child:** affirm them; family acceptance protects. Trevor Project (US) offers
  youth crisis support (1-866-488-7386, text START to 678-678).
- **Two homes:** both homes secure means and follow the same plan.
- **Caregiver in shock:** 988 supports caregivers too.

## Direct questions (8:40 pm, on the sofa)

## Techniques Used

- **AG-19 Time-Critical Response Protocol** — actions sorted into now, today, and the following days.
- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — attempt, plan, intent, or inability to keep safe trigger emergency care; any statement triggers same-day assessment.
- **DP-04 Must-Not Constraints** — firearms, medications, secrecy promises, and leaving the child alone are ruled out.
- **NE-07 Emotional Validation First** — calm thanks and presence precede questions and decisions.
- **QA-04 Uncertainty Acknowledgment** — the caregiver records and acts; risk assessment is left to a professional.
