# School Refusal in Young Children (Ages 5–8)

**Objective:** Get a young child back to school steadily and with less distress — after
checking for illness, bullying, or harm — by understanding what the refusal does for the
child, building a predictable morning and handoff, reducing the home accommodations that
keep avoidance going, and involving a clinician when the pattern meets clear thresholds.

**When to Use:**
- A 5–8-year-old cries, clings, hides, or has tantrums on school mornings, or begs to
  stay home.
- Stomachaches, headaches, or nausea appear on school days and fade at home or on
  weekends.
- The child has started missing days, arriving late, or being collected early.
- Drop-off has become a 20-minute negotiation at the classroom door.
- **Not this prompt if** the child is 9–12 — use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-9-12/parenting_school_refusal_decoder_tween.md`;
  a teenager → `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-13-18/parenting_teen_school_disengagement_decoder.md`.
  Under-3 childcare separation → `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-0-3/parenting_separation_anxiety_protocol_0_3.md`.
  A first-week adjustment at a **new** school → `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/transitions-events/parenting_new_school_transition_plan.md`.
  A clinician treating the anxiety uses
  `domain-psychology/populations/child-adolescent/psychology_pediatric_anxiety_protocol_session_plan.md`.

## Safety Block — Check These First

- **The child discloses being hurt, touched, threatened, or bullied** by a child or adult
  at school or on the bus → this is a safety issue, not anxiety; do not push attendance
  until it is addressed. Contact the principal/safeguarding lead; see
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/safety-risk/parenting_body_safety_consent_lessons.md`.
- **Talk of wanting to die or hurting themselves** → see
  `parenting_child_suicide_talk_response_6_12.md` (988 in the US).
- **Physical symptoms with fever, vomiting, weight loss, blood in stool, night waking with
  pain, or symptoms on weekends too** → pediatrician; do not assume anxiety.
- **Sudden onset after a frightening event** (lockdown, accident, witnessing violence)
  → ask the pediatrician about a trauma-informed assessment.
- **The child panics all day at school**, stops eating or toileting at school, or
  distress is spreading to other places → clinician now, not after the trial.

**This plan supports a return to school; it does not diagnose an anxiety disorder.**

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, grade, temperament, recent changes (new sibling, move, separation,
   illness, death), sleep, any diagnosis.
2. **Pattern:** when it began, which days, what the child says, symptoms, what happens
   at the door, how the child is once in class (ask the teacher).
3. **Attendance:** days missed, late arrivals, early pickups in the last month.
4. **What adults do now:** let the child stay home, stay in class, drive instead of
   bus, phone calls during the day, extra screen time at home.
5. **School partner:** who could meet the child at the door.
6. **Caregiver situation:** work flexibility, the caregiver's own anxiety, co-parent.

## Method

1. **Clear medical and safety causes (DS-01).** One pediatrician visit for recurring
   symptoms; ask the teacher and child about bullying, toileting fears, and anything that
   frightens them. Then agree a **well-enough rule** with the doctor: no fever, no
   vomiting, no diarrhoea → school.
2. **Identify the dominant function (DP-06).** School-refusal research groups motives into
   four functions:
   - **Avoiding distress** caused by school in general (dread, vague worry).
   - **Escaping a specific situation** (a loud lunchroom, reading aloud, a peer, PE).
   - **Staying close to a caregiver** (worry something will happen to the parent;
     common at this age).
   - **Rewards of staying home** (screens, one-to-one time, snacks).
   Name the main one; there may be a second.
3. **Validate the feeling, keep the expectation (NE-07).** "You feel scared about
   school. That feeling is real. And you're going, and I know you can handle it." Avoid
   long reassurance loops; answer a repeated worry once, then point to the plan.
4. **Design the morning (ST-02).** Wake early enough to avoid rushing; same steps every
   day on a picture schedule; worry talk is allowed at a set time (breakfast), not on
   the doorstep. Screens off on school mornings.
5. **Design the handoff.** Short goodbye ritual (under a minute: hug, phrase, wave);
   never sneak away; a named adult meets the child (teacher, aide, counsellor) with a job
   or a calm activity; a transitional object if helpful; pickup time stated and kept.
6. **Reduce accommodations.** Caregiver accommodations (letting the child stay home,
   staying in class, constant reassurance, daytime calls) are kind but keep the fear
   going. Parent-focused anxiety programs such as SPACE teach parents to replace them
   with supportive statements. Remove one accommodation at a time and say so to the
   child in advance.
7. **Make home on a school day boring.** If the child stays home ill, it looks like a
   school day: bed or schoolwork, no screens, no outings.
8. **Graded return if days have been missed.** Steps such as: arrive for the first hour
   → until lunch → full day; or enter with a parent to the gate → to the classroom door
   → bus. Move up after 3 calm-enough days at a step.
9. **Escalation gate (DP-05).** Clinician referral when the triggers below are met.

## Output Format

```
# School Return Plan — [child, age, grade]   Start: [date]

## Safety and medical check (done / outcome)
## Well-enough rule (agreed with doctor)
## Main function (and second, if any) with evidence
## What we say (validate + expectation scripts)
## Morning schedule (picture steps, times)
## Handoff plan (ritual, named adult, first task, pickup promise)
## Accommodations we are reducing (one at a time, with dates)
## Graded steps (if days missed) and move-up rule
## Tracker: | Day | Got to school? | Time | Door distress 0–10 | Teacher report |
## Get clinical help if…
```

## Verification

- [ ] Medical and safety causes are checked before any push to attend.
- [ ] A dominant function is named with evidence.
- [ ] Goodbye ritual is short and never involves sneaking away.
- [ ] A named school adult is part of the handoff.
- [ ] Accommodations are reduced one at a time with the child told in advance.
- [ ] Escalation triggers and routes are explicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Calling every stomachache anxiety | Missed appendicitis or constipation | One medical check first; weekend symptoms point away from anxiety. |
| Calling it manipulation | "He's just playing us" | Most young refusers are frightened; function, not motive. |
| Missing bullying or harm | Pushing a child back to a bully | Ask directly and ask the teacher before the plan. |
| Rescuing at the door | Parent takes child home after tears | Each rescue teaches that distress ends school; hold the handoff. |
| Endless reassurance | Ten "you'll be fine" per morning | Answer once, then point to the plan. |
| Waiting it out | Weeks of partial days | Missed days make return harder; graded return now. |

**Get clinical help if:** refusal or partial days continue for more than 2 weeks with the
plan in place; the child misses 10% or more of school days (chronic absence); panic,
vomiting from fear, or all-day distress at school; worry spreads to sleep, playdates, or
being apart from the parent anywhere; the caregiver cannot carry out the plan because of
their own distress. Route: pediatrician referral to a child psychologist offering CBT with
exposure and a parent component.

## Adaptations

- **Autistic child:** look hard at the escape function (noise, transitions, unclear
  rules); ask for a quiet start, visual timetable, and warning of changes.
- **ADHD:** mornings need external structure; check whether school demands are the issue.
- **Two homes:** same morning routine and handoff ritual at both; refusal often peaks
  after transitions between homes.
- **Caregiver with anxiety:** a different adult may do drop-off for the first two weeks.
- **After illness or a holiday:** expect a spike; return on a Monday with a full plan.

## Techniques Used

- **DP-06 Dominant Driver Identification** — the main function of the refusal shapes the plan.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — four-function model of school refusal and the parent-accommodation framework.
- **NE-07 Emotional Validation First** — feelings acknowledged before the expectation is restated.
- **ST-02 Structured Sequential Instructions** — fixed morning steps, handoff, and graded return with a move-up rule.
- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — duration, attendance, and distress thresholds trigger clinical referral.
