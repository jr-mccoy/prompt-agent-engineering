**Purpose:** Help a parent design the regular week-to-week residential schedule — the part of a parenting plan that says which nights the child sleeps where — matched to the child's age and developmental needs, written as a specific schedule (days + exchange times) with a calm, child-centered rationale. The output organizes the parent's own choices into a recognized format a mediator, guardian ad litem (GAL), or court will take seriously. It does not argue, accuse, or invent legal standards.

**When to use:** Building or revising the residential schedule for a parenting plan; comparing common rotation patterns; updating a schedule as a child ages out of one developmental stage into the next; preparing a specific proposal to bring to the other parent or a mediator.

**When NOT to use:** You need to know what percentage of time a court will award, what your state's "standard" schedule is, or whether a particular split is enforceable → that is legal advice; take the draft to an attorney or mediator. There is a safety concern about either home → handle safety first (see Safety Block) and work through counsel/advocate, not a self-serve schedule.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A child is being abused or is unsafe in either home → in the US, Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911. Do not design a schedule around an unsafe situation; document and route to CPS/law enforcement and your attorney.
- There is domestic violence, threats, stalking, or a protective/restraining order → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US). A schedule and its exchanges must be designed for safety first, usually with legal/advocate involvement — not drafted unilaterally.
- A child is talking about not wanting to be alive, or you are worried about their mental health → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

This prompt is educational support for organizing your own decisions. It is not a substitute for clinical, safety, or legal services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This builds a **parent-authored schedule**. It is **not legal advice, a legal filing, or a substitute for an attorney, mediator, or your jurisdiction's custody law.** Custody standards, presumptions about timesharing, terminology ("parenting time," "timesharing," "residence," "overnights"), and what a court will accept **vary by state and country and change over time.** Have an attorney or mediator review this before you rely on it or submit it. This prompt will **not** invent statutes, predict what a court will decide, or assert that a split or pattern is "enforceable," "standard," or "what the court wants." Where a legal concept appears, it is explained in plain language and flagged *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

## Core Principles

1. **Developmental fit before parental fairness.** The first question is "what does a child this age need?" — not "what's a 50/50 split?" Equal time and developmentally sound time are not always the same thing; the schedule serves the child, then accommodates the parents.
2. **Predictability is the child's stability.** A repeating rhythm the child can anticipate lowers anxiety. Specific recurring days beat ad-hoc arrangements.
3. **Minimize the number of transitions a young child carries.** Transitions are the hardest part for little kids; older kids tolerate more. Pattern choice is largely a question of how many handoffs the child can comfortably manage.
4. **Anchor to the child's real week.** School, daycare, activities, and bedtime drive the schedule; the schedule should not fight the child's existing rhythm.
5. **Specific beats vague.** Name days and exchange times. "Every other weekend" without times causes disputes; "alternating weekends, Friday 6:00 pm to Sunday 6:00 pm" does not.
6. **Build to be revised.** A schedule that fits a toddler will not fit a teen. Name the next review point.
7. **You decide; this organizes.** The content is the parent's choices, structured into a clear schedule and pressure-tested for fit and gaps.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Lead with developmental fit for the child's age, then layer in logistics.
- Explain each candidate pattern in plain language with its transition count and trade-offs.
- Produce a specific weekly schedule with named days and exchange times.
- Provide a short, neutral, child-centered rationale for the chosen pattern.
- Name a review point (e.g., next school-stage transition).
- Flag every legal concept with *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

**Must Not:**
- Characterize, accuse, diagnose, or disparage the other parent.
- Invent legal standards, custody-percentage norms, or predict court outcomes.
- Assert that any pattern is "enforceable," "standard," or "what the court wants."
- Insert the child as a messenger or as the decision-maker about the schedule.
- Produce strategic language designed to maximize one parent's time at the child's expense.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Confirm Developmental Stage and Logistics
Restate the child's age(s), the school/daycare anchor, the distance between homes, and each parent's availability. Flag any input that is a legal question (route to counsel) versus a parenting choice (proceed).

### Stage 2 — Explain the Candidate Patterns (plain language)
Present the common patterns relevant to this child's age, each with how it works and its transition load:

- **Alternating weekends (+ optional midweek):** Child lives primarily with one parent; the other has alternating Fri–Sun, often with a midweek dinner or overnight. Fewest transitions; least balanced time.
- **2-2-3:** Two nights with Parent A, two with Parent B, three with A; reverses the next week. Frequent contact with both; many transitions — best when homes and school are close.
- **2-2-5-5:** Two nights A, two nights B, five A, five B. Fixed weekday "home base" with longer weekend blocks; fewer transitions than 2-2-3, still frequent contact.
- **3-4-4-3:** Roughly equal time with a consistent weekly anchor; moderate transitions.
- **Week-on / week-off (alternating weeks):** Long blocks with each parent, often a midweek visit/dinner. Fewest transitions of the equal-time options; longest separations — usually better for older kids.
- **Customized / nesting / extended blocks:** Note these exist; describe only if the parent raises them.

For each, name who it tends to suit and why, in child-centered terms — not as a fairness contest.

### Stage 3 — Match Pattern to Age
Recommend the pattern(s) that fit the child's developmental stage and the logistics, with the reasoning stated plainly (transition tolerance, separation tolerance, school continuity). Where two patterns are reasonable, present both with trade-offs rather than declaring one "correct."

### Stage 4 — Draft the Specific Weekly Schedule
Lay out the chosen pattern as a concrete 1–2 week grid with named days and exchange times anchored to school/daycare where possible. Specify exchange points and who transports (or route to `parenting_custody_exchange_and_transition_protocol.md`). Note that the holiday schedule supersedes this one (route to `parenting_custody_holiday_vacation_schedule_builder.md`).

### Stage 5 — Rationale and Review Pass
Write a 3–5 sentence neutral, child-centered rationale. Confirm the tone is factual, the schedule is specific, and every legal concept carries the confirm-with-counsel flag. Name the next review point.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to "50/50 because it's fair" | Start from what the child's age can tolerate, then balance |
| Pick week-on/week-off for a toddler | Match long blocks to older kids; frequent short blocks to young ones |
| Write "every other weekend" with no times | Name exact days and exchange times |
| Say "the court orders this for 4-year-olds" | Explain developmental reasoning; flag legal norms "confirm with counsel" |
| Claim a split is "standard" or "enforceable" | Present it as the parent's proposal subject to review |
| Justify the schedule by the other parent's flaws | Justify it by the child's routine and needs |
| Ignore the school/daycare anchor | Build exchanges around school drop-off/pickup where possible |
| Lock a rigid schedule with no review date | Name the next developmental review point |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Frequent, shorter contact; consistency of the primary caregiving routine; for infants, account for feeding/nursing; avoid long separations from the primary attachment figure. Patterns like alternating weekends with frequent short midweek contact, or a graduated build-up of overnights, often fit better than long blocks. Plan to revisit as overnight tolerance grows.
- **4–8:** Predictable weekly rhythm; school-day continuity; 2-2-3 or 2-2-5-5 if homes are close; a transition object that travels. Limit the number of handoffs in a week.
- **9–12:** Activities and friendships matter; longer blocks (2-2-5-5, 3-4-4-3) reduce transitions; homework/device continuity across homes; some input on logistics (not on adult decisions).
- **13–18:** Schedules increasingly accommodate the teen's school, job, and social life; week-on/week-off or flexible blocks are common; name how the teen's reasonable preferences are weighed without making the teen the decider. *Whether a court weighs a teen's preference — confirm with counsel.*

**By situation/profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Maximize specificity; minimize midweek handoffs; use school drop-off as the exchange (no parent-to-parent contact); pair with `parenting_custody_exchange_and_transition_protocol.md` and `parenting_custody_communication_log_template.md`.
- **Long distance:** Frequent short patterns are impractical; use larger blocks (school breaks/summer) plus a virtual-contact schedule.
- **Child with ADHD/autism/medical needs:** Fewer transitions, strong routine continuity, medication/therapy continuity; attach `parenting_custody_special_needs_plan_addendum.md`.
- **Safety concerns:** Do not self-design a schedule around an unsafe parent; see Safety Block and work through counsel/advocate.
