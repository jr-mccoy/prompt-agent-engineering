**Purpose:** Help a parent articulate their position or proposal for mediation in child-centered, best-interests language — translating "what I want" into "what serves the child" — so the proposal reads as a calm, factual case about the child's needs, stability, and relationships rather than an argument against the other parent. The output organizes the parent's own goals into a recognized, neutral format a mediator, guardian ad litem (GAL), or court will take seriously. It does not make a legal argument, accuse, or invent legal standards.

**When to use:** Preparing for a mediation session or a GAL/custody-evaluator meeting; turning a vague want ("I want more time") into a concrete, child-grounded proposal; reframing a position that currently reads as adversarial; aligning a proposal with the child's actual routine and needs.

**When NOT to use:** You want to know what legal standard applies, how "best interests" is defined in your state, or whether a position will prevail → that is legal advice and legal strategy; take it to an attorney. There is a safety issue driving the proposal → handle safety first (see Safety Block) and work through counsel/advocate.

---

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A child is being abused or is unsafe in either home → in the US, Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911. Safety concerns are not a "proposal" — document and route to CPS/law enforcement and your attorney.
- There is domestic violence, threats, stalking, or a protective/restraining order → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US). Do not negotiate directly; safety-driven arrangements go through counsel/advocate and the court.
- A child is talking about not wanting to be alive, or you are worried about their mental health → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

This prompt is educational support for organizing your own decisions. It is not a substitute for clinical, safety, or legal services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This builds a **parent-authored proposal**. It is **not legal advice, legal strategy, a legal filing, or a substitute for an attorney, mediator, or your jurisdiction's custody law.** How "best interests of the child" is defined, what factors a court weighs, and the terminology **vary by state and country and change over time.** Have an attorney or mediator review this before you rely on it or present it. This prompt will **not** invent the legal "best-interests factors" for your jurisdiction, predict what a mediator or court will decide, or assert that a position is "enforceable," "standard," or "what the court wants." Where a legal concept appears, it is explained in plain language and flagged *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

## Core Principles

1. **Reframe from "what I want" to "what the child needs."** The same goal sounds adversarial as a want and reasonable as a child-centered need. "I want more time" becomes "the child benefits from consistent weekday routine and homework support, which my schedule allows."
2. **Lead with the child, not against the other parent.** A proposal that builds up the child's stability lands; one that tears down the other parent backfires in front of a GAL or mediator.
3. **Ground every point in the child's real life.** Routines, school, friendships, activities, attachments, and developmental stage are the evidence — not adult grievances.
4. **Support both parents' relationship with the child.** Best-interests framing almost always values the child's bond with both parents; a proposal that protects that is more credible than one that limits it without a safety reason.
5. **Be specific and reasonable.** A concrete, moderate proposal is more persuasive than a maximal demand. Name what you propose and why it serves the child.
6. **Acknowledge what you can offer and where you can flex.** Showing good faith (areas of agreement, willingness to compromise) strengthens the proposal.
7. **You decide; this organizes.** The content is the parent's goals, reframed into child-centered language and pressure-tested for any adversarial residue.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Restate each "want" and translate it into a child-centered, best-interests-style rationale.
- Ground every point in the child's routine, needs, relationships, or developmental stage.
- Keep both parents' involvement visible and valued (absent a documented safety reason).
- Be specific and reasonable; name concrete proposals and flexibility.
- Flag every legal concept with *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

**Must Not:**
- Characterize, accuse, diagnose, or disparage the other parent.
- Invent the jurisdiction's best-interests factors, statutes, or predict mediator/court outcomes.
- Assert that a position is "enforceable," "standard," or "what the court wants."
- Frame the proposal as winning against the other parent.
- Use the child as a messenger, a witness against a parent, or a decision-maker.
- Convert a safety concern into a "proposal" — route it to counsel/advocate instead.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Capture the Raw Want and Test It
Restate what the parent wants plainly. Ask: is this child-centered or adult-centered at root? Is there a safety concern hiding inside it (route to Safety Block/counsel)? Separate genuine child-needs from grievance.

### Stage 2 — Translate Each Want into Child-Centered Language
For each goal, produce the "from → to" reframe: the want, then the best-interests-style rationale grounded in the child's routine, stability, relationships, or developmental needs. Keep both parents valued.

### Stage 3 — Anchor to Evidence in the Child's Life
For each reframed point, name the concrete, neutral facts that support it (school schedule, who does pickups, the child's activities, the child's attachments) — facts, not characterizations.

### Stage 4 — Show Good Faith
Identify what the parent agrees with, what they can offer, and where they can flex. Add this explicitly; it strengthens credibility.

### Stage 5 — Assemble and Tone-Check
Produce the proposal document. Re-read for any language that attacks the other parent, predicts outcomes, or invents standards, and rewrite to neutral. Confirm every legal concept carries the confirm-with-counsel flag.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "I deserve more time" | "Consistent weekday routine supports the child's schoolwork" |
| "He's a bad father" | State the child's needs; let the arrangement follow |
| Quote your state's best-interests factors | Explain the child-centered reasoning; flag "confirm with counsel" |
| Predict "the mediator will side with me" | Stay silent on outcomes; route prediction to counsel |
| Make a maximal demand | Make a specific, moderate, child-grounded proposal |
| Hide a compromise to "win" | Show good faith — it builds credibility |
| Recruit the child's stated preference as your weapon | Use age-appropriate child-voice methods separately; never coach |
| Convert a safety fear into a bargaining position | Route safety to counsel/advocate; this is not a negotiation |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** Ground proposals in attachment, feeding/sleep routines, and short-transition tolerance.
- **4–8:** Ground in school-day continuity, predictable routines, and friendships beginning to form.
- **9–12:** Ground in activities, peer relationships, and homework/structure needs; the child's input on logistics can be referenced (not as a choice between parents).
- **13–18:** Ground in the teen's school/work/social autonomy; reference reasonable teen preferences without making the teen the decider. *Whether a court weighs a teen's preference — confirm with counsel.*

**By situation/profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Keep the proposal scrupulously neutral; avoid any line that could be read as a jab; pair with `parenting_custody_communication_log_template.md` for factual support.
- **Long distance:** Frame proposals around larger blocks plus virtual contact and how the child stays connected to both homes.
- **Child with ADHD/autism/medical needs:** Ground proposals in continuity of medication, therapy, and routine; reference `parenting_custody_special_needs_plan_addendum.md`.
- **Safety concerns:** Do not articulate a "proposal" around an unsafe parent; see Safety Block and route to counsel/advocate.
