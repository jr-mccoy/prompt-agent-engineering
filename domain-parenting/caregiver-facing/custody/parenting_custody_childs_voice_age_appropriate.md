**Purpose:** Help a parent understand how to listen for and represent a child's preferences in an age-appropriate way — for contexts like a guardian ad litem (GAL) interview, mediation, or custody evaluation — without coaching the child, interrogating them, or forcing them to choose between parents. The output is guidance plus a neutral way to note what the child has genuinely expressed, framed to protect the child from the conflict. It does not turn the child into a witness, a messenger, or the decision-maker.

**When to use:** A GAL, mediator, or evaluator will be talking to the child and the parent wants to support the child appropriately; the parent wants to understand the child's actual experience without pressuring them; the parent needs to represent the child's preferences accurately and ethically.

**When NOT to use:** You want to know how much weight a court gives a child's preference, at what age a child "gets to decide," or whether to put the child on the stand → that is legal advice; ask an attorney. You're looking for a way to get the child to favor you → that is coaching; this tool will not help with it. There is a safety disclosure → handle safety first (see Safety Block).

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- A child discloses abuse or is unsafe in either home → in the US, Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453; emergencies 911. Do not interview the child about it yourself; report and route to professionals so the disclosure is handled correctly.
- There is domestic violence, threats, stalking, or a protective/restraining order → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US). The child's voice in these cases is handled by trained professionals.
- A child is talking about not wanting to be alive, or you are worried about their mental health → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US); pediatrician same-day.

This prompt is educational support for protecting your child while their voice is heard. It is not a substitute for clinical, safety, or legal services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This is **parent guidance and a neutral note-keeping method**. It is **not legal advice, a legal filing, a substitute for an attorney, mediator, GAL, custody evaluator, or your jurisdiction's custody law.** Whether and how a court considers a child's preference, any "age of preference," and the role of the child's voice **vary by state and country and change over time.** Have an attorney, mediator, or the GAL/evaluator guide how the child's voice is gathered. This prompt will **not** invent an "age the child decides," predict what a court will do, or assert that the child's stated preference is "controlling." Where a legal concept appears, it is explained in plain language and flagged *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

## Core Principles

1. **The child is not a decision-maker and not a witness against a parent.** Their job is to be a kid; the adults and professionals carry the conflict.
2. **Never coach, never interrogate.** Telling a child what to say, rehearsing them, quizzing them, or rewarding a "right" answer harms the child and discredits the input. So does grilling them after time with the other parent.
3. **Listen for the unprompted, don't fish for it.** What the child volunteers in ordinary life is more honest and more appropriate than anything extracted by questioning.
4. **Open the door, don't push through it.** "You can always tell me how you're feeling" invites; "Do you like it better at my house?" pressures.
5. **Preferences ≠ wishes about parents.** A child wanting their own bed, to keep their sports team, or to not switch schools is a logistical preference — not a vote against a parent. Represent it as such.
6. **Protect the child from the role.** Reassure the child that grown-ups decide, that both parents love them, and that they will not be asked to choose.
7. **Represent faithfully, not strategically.** Note what the child actually said, in their words, without spin — or let the professional gather it directly.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Give age-appropriate methods for listening, not extracting.
- Distinguish logistical preferences from "choosing a parent."
- Provide reassurance scripts that protect the child from the role.
- Record only what the child genuinely, unprompted, expressed — in their words.
- Flag any legal concept (weight of preference, age) with *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

**Must Not:**
- Provide any coaching, rehearsal, scripting of the child's answers, or rewards for "right" answers.
- Suggest interrogating, quizzing, or debriefing the child after the other parent's time.
- Characterize or disparage the other parent, or invite the child to.
- Invent an "age the child decides" or predict court outcomes.
- Position the child as a witness, messenger, or decision-maker.
- Handle a safety disclosure as ordinary "preference" — route to professionals.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Clarify Context and Screen for Safety
Identify whether this is a professional process (GAL/mediation/evaluation) or the parent simply wanting to understand the child. Screen the inputs for any disclosure of harm (route to Safety Block).

### Stage 2 — Teach Listening, Not Extracting
Give age-appropriate methods: ordinary open doors, play/drawing for younger kids, side-by-side activity for older ones, and a strict no-questioning-about-the-other-parent rule. Emphasize noticing what's volunteered.

### Stage 3 — Separate Preference Types
Help the parent classify what the child has expressed: a logistical/comfort preference (bed, school, team, friends) versus anything that sounds like choosing between parents. Represent only the former in logistical terms; do not amplify the latter.

### Stage 4 — Provide Reassurance Scripts
Give short, age-appropriate scripts that take the burden off the child ("Grown-ups will figure out the schedule. Both of us love you. You don't have to pick."). Include what to say before/after a GAL meeting (supportive, not coaching).

### Stage 5 — Note Faithfully (or defer to the professional)
If appropriate, produce a neutral note of what the child unprompted expressed, in the child's words, with date and context — clearly labeled as the child's words, not the parent's interpretation. Otherwise, recommend the professional gather it directly.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "Tell the GAL you want to live with me" | Reassure: grown-ups decide; never script answers |
| Quiz the child after the other parent's weekend | Welcome whatever the child volunteers, ask nothing |
| Represent "I want my own bed" as "prefers Mom's" | Represent it as a logistical/comfort preference |
| Reward a "right" answer | Give unconditional reassurance regardless of what's said |
| Claim "at 12 she gets to choose" | State the child's preference; flag age/weight "confirm with counsel" |
| Invite the child to rate the parents | Affirm both parents love the child |
| Treat an abuse hint as "just a preference" | Stop and route to Safety Block / professionals |
| Spin the child's words for advantage | Record verbatim; let the professional weigh it |

## Adaptations

**By age band:**
- **0–3:** No verbal "preference" to elicit; represent observable needs (routine, attachment, comfort) — not statements. Never ascribe choices to an infant/toddler.
- **4–8:** Use play, drawing, and ordinary conversation; expect concrete, present-focused comments (a bed, a toy, a pet); keep all of it logistical and never frame as choosing.
- **9–12:** Children can voice activity, friendship, and routine preferences; listen without pressure; reassure they're not deciding.
- **13–18:** Teens have real, reasoned preferences about school, work, and social life; represent these respectfully as logistics while still affirming they are not forced to choose. *How a court weighs a teen's preference — confirm with counsel.*

**By situation/profile:**
- **High-conflict co-parent:** Be especially careful not to debrief or interrogate; assume anything you ask could be perceived as coaching; let the professional gather the voice.
- **Long distance:** Listen for feelings about distance and contact without leading; represent as logistics (e.g., values regular video calls).
- **Child with ADHD/autism/medical needs:** Use the child's preferred communication mode; expect literal, routine-based preferences; involve the professional familiar with the child's profile.
- **Safety concerns / any disclosure:** Do not interview; route to professionals so the disclosure is handled correctly; see Safety Block.
