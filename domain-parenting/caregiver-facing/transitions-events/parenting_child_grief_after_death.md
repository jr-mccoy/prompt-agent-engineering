# Child Grief After a Death

**Objective:** Produce a grief-support plan for a specific child after a specific
death — what the child can understand, how to involve them in rituals, how to respond
to grief that comes and goes, what to keep steady, how to mark hard dates, and a clear
screen for complicated or traumatic grief with a named route to specialist help.

**When to Use:**
- Someone important to the child has died — parent, sibling, grandparent, friend,
  classmate, teacher — or a pet whose death has hit the child hard.
- The news has been told and the caregiver now needs to support the child over time.
- A child's grief looks different from what the adult expected (playing at the
  funeral, no tears, anger, regression, questions weeks later).
- **Not this prompt if** you still need the words to **tell** a 4–8-year-old the
  news — use `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_hard_topics_age_appropriate_scripts.md`
  first. If the grieving person is the adult over their own pet, use
  `domain-psychology/client-self-use/grief-loss/clientself_pet_loss_script.md`. A
  clinician assessing grief should use
  `domain-psychology/populations/cross-population/psychology_grief_complicated_vs_normal_assessment.md`.
  This prompt is the caregiver's plan for the months after.

## Safety Block

- **Child says they want to die, to "be with" the person, or to stop existing; or
  gives belongings away, makes a plan, or self-harms** → 988 Suicide & Crisis
  Lifeline (US, call/text) now; pediatrician same-day; do not leave them alone;
  lock away medications and firearms. Take this seriously at every age, including
  under 8. Immediate danger → 911.
- **The death was by suicide, homicide, overdose, or violence, or the child
  witnessed it** → arrange a child trauma assessment early (TF-CBT-trained
  clinician); do not wait to see if grief "settles."
- **The surviving parent cannot function** (not eating, not getting out of bed,
  unsafe drinking) → the child's safety net is at risk; involve family, the
  parent's doctor, or 988.
- Outside the US, use your local emergency and crisis numbers.

**This plan supports a grieving child; it does not diagnose.** A child who meets the
"get help" thresholds below needs a bereavement specialist or child mental-health
clinician.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, developmental level, neurodivergence, prior losses, temperament.
2. **The death:** who, relationship, cause (expected, sudden, suicide, violent),
   whether the child witnessed or found the person, date.
3. **What the child knows and the words used so far.**
4. **Rituals ahead:** funeral, memorial, burial, cremation, religious observance.
5. **Household changes caused by the death:** income, moving, a new caregiver,
   loss of a parent's role (secondary losses).
6. **Surviving adults' state** and the family's faith or worldview.

## Method

1. **Map the child's understanding of death (DS-01).** Four concepts —
   irreversibility, non-functionality (the body stops working), universality, and
   causality — are usually grasped by about 5–7. Under 5: expect "when is Grandpa
   coming back?" for weeks; repeat the same concrete answer. 6–9: may fear their own
   or a parent's death; ask detailed body questions. 10–12: understand fully, may hide
   grief to protect adults. Teens: adult understanding; grieve with peers and online;
   may take on adult roles.
2. **Use honest, concrete language (CM-11).** "Died," not "lost" or "went to sleep"
   — euphemisms make a young child afraid of sleep or wait for the lost person to be
   found. For suicide, use truthful age-matched words, because children learn the
   truth later and secrecy costs trust: "Uncle Dev died by suicide. His brain got
   very sick and he made his body stop working."
3. **Offer prepared ritual choices.** Children may attend the funeral if they are
   told beforehand what they will see (the casket, people crying, how long), have a
   designated adult who can leave with them, and are allowed to choose. Offer roles:
   a drawing in the casket, a candle, a reading for teens.
4. **Expect grief that comes and goes (NE-07).** Children "puddle-jump": intense
   sadness, then play minutes later. Play is not a sign they did not care. Answer
   questions when they come, even the fifth time. Regression (sleep, toileting,
   clinginess) is common for weeks.
5. **Hold routines and name secondary losses.** Keep school, bedtime, and
   activities steady. Name what else changed ("Dad used to drive you to soccer — now
   Aunt Rosa will").
6. **Keep connection to the person.** Memory box, photos, telling stories, letters
   to the person, keeping an object. Continuing bonds are healthy.
7. **Tell the school.** Teacher and counsellor get the facts, the words the family
   uses, and the plan for upset moments (a pass to the counsellor's office).
8. **Mark hard dates.** Birthday, death anniversary, Mother's/Father's Day, the
   first holiday. Plan them in advance; ask the child how they want to remember.
9. **Screen for complicated or traumatic grief (DP-05)** at 1, 3, and 6 months
   against the thresholds below; uncertain cases go to a professional (QA-04).

## Output Format

```
# Grief Support Plan — [child, age] after the death of [person, date, cause]

## What [child] can understand now (and expected questions)
## Words we use (and words we avoid)
## Rituals: choices offered, preparation script, designated adult
## Day-to-day: routines kept, secondary losses named
## Responding to grief bursts and regression (scripts)
## Staying connected to [person]
## School plan
## Hard dates (list + plan)
## Check-ins at 1 / 3 / 6 months: normal vs. get help
## Support routes (bereavement programs, clinicians, crisis)
```

## Verification

- [ ] Explanation fits the child's grasp of the four death concepts.
- [ ] No euphemisms; suicide/violent death handled with truthful age-matched words.
- [ ] Funeral participation is a prepared choice with a designated adult.
- [ ] Secondary losses are named and addressed.
- [ ] Hard dates are listed with plans.
- [ ] Complicated/traumatic grief thresholds and specialist routes are explicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Reading play as not grieving | "She doesn't care — she's laughing" | Intermittent grief is typical of children. |
| Reading sadness at 2 months as disorder | Referral for normal tears | Ongoing sadness is expected; the screen is impairment and intensity. |
| Missing grief in a "fine" child | Quiet 11-year-old taking care of mum | Ask directly; protective hiding is common at 10+. |
| Shielding from the funeral by default | Child sent away without a choice | Prepared choice reduces fear and regret. |
| Secrecy about cause | "Heart attack" for a suicide | Age-matched truth; secrets surface and erode trust. |
| Rushing closure | Clearing the room in week 1 | Let the child have a say over belongings. |

## Adaptations

- **Autistic child:** literal language is essential; visual explanation of what
  happens at a funeral; grief may show as rigidity or meltdowns.
- **Death of a parent:** the surviving parent's grief and capacity is the largest
  factor; line up adult support and practical help early.
- **Death of a sibling:** watch for survivor guilt and "replacement" pressure.
- **Faith tradition:** use the family's framework for where the person is, while
  still saying the body has stopped working.
- **Teen:** peer and online memorials matter; a teen may want a role, not shelter.

## Example Output

```
# Grief Support Plan — Leo, 7, after the death of Grandpa Joe
# (heart attack, 12 Oct; Leo was at school)

## Understanding now
Leo knows dead means the body stops; may still ask if Grandpa can feel cold
in the ground. Expect: "Will you die?" Answer: "I plan to live a very long
time, until you're grown up and older. Lots of people take care of you."

## Words
Use: died, body stopped working, buried. Avoid: lost, passed, sleeping.

## Funeral (19 Oct)
Choice offered on 14 Oct after describing it: casket closed, people
crying, 45 minutes, then lunch. Aunt Mei is Leo's adult and can take him
outside at any time. Leo chose to go and put his drawing of the fishing
boat in the casket.

## Day-to-day
School Monday as usual. Secondary loss: Grandpa did Thursday pickup;
Aunt Mei takes Thursdays and keeps the ice-cream stop.

## Grief bursts
At bedtime: "You're missing Grandpa. Let's look at his photo and say one
thing we remember." Bedwetting returned twice: change sheets, no comment.

## Connection
Memory box with Grandpa's fishing hat; story night on Sundays.

## School
Teacher and counsellor told 13 Oct; Leo can visit the counsellor when sad.

## Hard dates
Grandpa's birthday 3 Feb: fishing at his lake. First Thanksgiving: empty
chair is okay; Leo lights a candle.

## Check-ins at 1 / 3 / 6 months — get help if:
- Talks about wanting to die or be with Grandpa → 988 / pediatrician now.
- Intense yearning or preoccupation most days and not improving by
  ~6 months (DSM-5-TR prolonged grief disorder threshold for children is
  6 months), or school/friend functioning drops for >4 weeks.
- Persistent guilt ("I made his heart stop"), fear of leaving the house, or
  nightmares/avoidance suggesting trauma.
Routes: pediatrician referral to a child bereavement therapist; local
hospice bereavement program (often free); Dougy Center and National
Alliance for Children's Grief directories; grief camp next summer.
```

## Techniques Used

- **NE-07 Emotional Validation First** — grief bursts are met with presence before redirection.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — four-concept developmental model of death understanding.
- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — 1/3/6-month gates escalate to specialist care.
- **CM-11 Reasoning-Based Constraint Design** — each language rule carries its reason (euphemisms → fear of sleep; secrecy → lost trust).
- **QA-04 Uncertainty Acknowledgment** — the plan supports, it does not diagnose; uncertain cases route to a clinician.
