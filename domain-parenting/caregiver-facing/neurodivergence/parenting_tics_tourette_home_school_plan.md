# Tics and Tourette's at Home and School

**Objective:** Help the caregiver understand their child's tics accurately, stop the
well-meant responses that make tics worse, make home and school easier for the child,
prepare the school and classmates in a way the child agrees to, and know when tics or
what comes with them need medical attention.

**When to Use:**
- A child has started blinking, grimacing, sniffing, throat-clearing, shrugging, jerking
  their head, or making noises they seem unable to stop.
- A pediatrician or neurologist has diagnosed a tic disorder or Tourette syndrome.
- Tics are drawing comments at school, from relatives, or from classmates.
- Tics are worse at home after school and the caregiver wonders if the child is
  "doing it on purpose."
- **Not this prompt if** repetitive movements are rhythmic, self-soothing, and stop when
  the child is distracted (e.g., hand-flapping, rocking) — those are usually stereotypies;
  use the sensory or ADHD prompts in `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/`.
  If you need to find the right specialist, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/cross-age/parenting_when_to_seek_professional_help_all_ages.md`.
  For writing to the teacher in general, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_teacher_partnership_email_composer.md`.
  This prompt is the caregiver's plan for tics; it does not diagnose or treat them.

## Safety Block — See the Doctor

- **Same-day or urgent:** jerks with loss of awareness, staring spells, or confusion
  (possible seizures); new movements with weakness, headache, vomiting, or a change in
  walking or speech; tics after a head injury; sudden onset of severe tics with abrupt
  OCD, food restriction, or personality change over days (doctors may consider PANS/PANDAS
  — a debated diagnosis that the doctor, not the family, should weigh).
- **Soon:** tics that cause pain or injury (neck jerks, hitting self, biting); tics that
  disrupt sleep, eating, or schoolwork; distress, bullying, or social withdrawal; tics that
  appeared or worsened after starting or changing a medication.
- **Sudden complex tic-like behaviours** (often in teens, often after exposure to tic
  videos online; long, complex movements or words starting all at once) → ask the
  doctor about functional tic-like behaviours, which are managed differently.
- **Talk of self-harm or wanting to die** → 988 (US), and see
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/mental-health-behavior/parenting_child_suicide_talk_response_6_12.md`.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, when tics began, which tics (motor, vocal, simple, complex), how often,
   whether they cause pain.
2. **Pattern:** worse with stress, excitement, tiredness, screens, or after holding them in
   at school? Better when focused (sport, music)?
3. **Diagnosis status:** none yet, provisional/transient tic disorder, chronic tic
   disorder, Tourette syndrome.
4. **Co-occurring concerns:** attention, obsessions or rituals, anxiety, rage attacks,
   learning, sleep.
5. **School and social:** teacher reactions, peer comments, tests, reading aloud.
6. **The child's view:** how they describe the urge; what they want others to know.

## Method

1. **Explain how tics work (DS-01).** Tics are sudden, repeated movements or sounds.
   Short-lived tics are common in childhood (studies estimate up to about one in five
   school-age children have them at some point), and many fade within a year. Tourette
   syndrome is diagnosed when there have been at least two motor tics and at least one
   vocal tic for more than a year, starting before 18. Tics wax and wane, change form,
   are often preceded by an uncomfortable urge (more noticeable from about age 10), and
   can be held in briefly — usually with a rebound later. Swearing tics (coprolalia) are
   uncommon. Tics often peak around 10–12 and lessen in the late teens for many.
2. **Separate tics from look-alikes (QA-12).** Habits (nail-biting), stereotypies
   (rhythmic, self-soothing), compulsions (done to relieve a worry or "just right"
   feeling), allergy sniffing, and seizures. Note which you are seeing and ask the doctor
   when unsure.
3. **Stop the responses that make tics worse.** Don't comment, imitate, ask them to stop,
   or praise them for "not doing it" — attention and pressure increase tension and tics.
   Don't punish tics. Respond to the child, not the tic.
4. **Adjust the home.** Protect sleep; plan downtime after school (when held-in tics
   rebound); a calm, predictable routine; physical activity and absorbing hobbies, which
   often reduce tics. Track stressors rather than tics day-to-day.
5. **Write the school information sheet (NE-20).** One page the teacher can use without
   further explanation: what the child's tics look like, that they are involuntary and
   holding them in is tiring, what helps (ignore the tic, a pass to step out, seat near
   the door, tests in a quiet room with extra time, not reading aloud if vocal tics are
   strong, typing if hand tics affect writing), how to respond if other children react,
   and who to contact.
6. **Prepare a classmate explanation — only with the child's consent.** Options: the
   child explains, the teacher explains with the child present, or the parent visits.
   Short, factual, and kind: "Sometimes my body moves or makes sounds that I can't stop.
   It's called a tic. You can just ignore it — that helps most."
7. **Watch for what often comes along.** About half of children with Tourette syndrome
   also have ADHD, and many have OCD symptoms or anxiety; these often cause more day-to-day
   difficulty than the tics themselves. Note them for the doctor.
8. **Know the treatment landscape for the doctor's visit.** The first-line treatment for
   bothersome tics is a behavioural therapy called CBIT (Comprehensive Behavioral
   Intervention for Tics), delivered by a trained clinician; medication is considered
   when tics remain impairing. Tics that do not bother the child often need no treatment.
9. **Medical gate (DP-05).** Use the Safety Block triggers; otherwise review at a routine
   visit with the log.

## Output Format

```
# Tic Support Plan — [child, age]   Date: [..]

## What [child]'s tics look like (motor / vocal; simple / complex)
## How tics work — family explanation (in plain words for the child too)
## Look-alikes ruled in/out (habit, stereotypy, compulsion, allergy, seizure)
## What we stop doing / what we do instead
## Home adjustments (sleep, after-school downtime, activities)
## School information sheet (one page)
## Classmate explanation (option chosen with the child)
## Co-occurring concerns to raise with the doctor
## Doctor: urgent / soon / routine — triggers and questions
## Log: | Week | Tic intensity (0–10) | Stressors | Sleep | Notes |
```

## Verification

- [ ] Tics are described concretely and separated from look-alikes.
- [ ] The plan includes no commenting, imitating, or asking the child to stop.
- [ ] The school sheet is one page and usable without further explanation.
- [ ] Classmate explanation is agreed with the child.
- [ ] Co-occurring ADHD, OCD, and anxiety are screened for the doctor.
- [ ] Urgent and soon triggers are explicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "Doing it on purpose" | Child punished for sniffing | Tics are involuntary; brief suppression is not control. |
| Reading after-school tics as home stress | "He's fine at school, so it's us" | Held-in tics rebound at home. |
| Calling every tic Tourette's | Panic after 3 weeks of blinking | Many tics are short-lived; diagnosis needs more than a year. |
| Missing seizures | Staring spells called "tics" | Loss of awareness → doctor. |
| Over-focusing on tics | Weekly tic counts dominate family life | Track stress and function, not each tic. |
| Missing the co-occurring condition | Tics managed, ADHD/OCD missed | Ask about attention, rituals, and worry. |

## Adaptations

- **Teen:** involve them in every decision about disclosure; driving, exams, and jobs may
  need accommodation discussions; watch for social media exposure to tic content.
- **Autistic child:** distinguish tics from stereotypies; both can co-occur.
- **Sports and music:** often reduce tics; tell coaches the same one-page facts.
- **Two homes:** share the information sheet and the "don't comment" rule with both homes
  and grandparents.
- **Cultural or faith beliefs** about the cause: tics are a neurological condition, not
  bad behaviour, weakness, or a spiritual problem.

## Example Output

```
# Tic Support Plan — Noah, 9   Date: 3 Oct

## Noah's tics
Eye blinking and shoulder shrug (since age 7); throat-clearing and a
short "hm" sound (since last winter). No pain. Diagnosed with
Tourette syndrome by Dr. Hale, Sept.

## Family explanation (for Noah)
"Your brain sends quick messages to move or make sounds. It's called a
tic. You didn't cause it. Holding them in is hard work, so home is a
place you don't have to."

## Look-alikes
Not seizures (no staring, aware throughout). Some "touching the door
frame twice" — possible compulsion; raise with Dr. Hale.

## Stop / instead
Stop: Grandma saying "stop clearing your throat"; Dad's "you didn't
blink all dinner — great!" Instead: carry on the conversation.

## Home
Quiet 45 minutes after school (Lego, no demands). Swimming 2x/week.
Bed 8:30 pm.

## School sheet (sent 6 Oct to Ms. Diaz and the office)
Noah has Tourette syndrome. Blinking, shrugging, throat-clearing and
"hm" sounds are involuntary. Please don't ask him to stop. Helpful:
seat near the door; quiet room with extra time for tests; a card to
step out for 2 minutes; if classmates comment, say "That's a tic —
we ignore it." Contact: Mum, 555-0142.

## Classmates
Noah chose to explain with Ms. Diaz beside him, 10 Oct, three
sentences he wrote himself.

## Doctor
Soon: ask about the door-frame touching and worry at bedtime.
Urgent if staring spells, pain, or sudden big change.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DS-01 Framework Application** — the clinical picture of tic disorders and Tourette syndrome explained in plain language.
- **QA-12 False Positives Identification** — tics separated from habits, stereotypies, compulsions, allergies, and seizures.
- **NE-20 Third-Party Handoff Package** — a one-page school sheet the teacher can act on without further explanation.
- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — urgent, soon, and routine medical triggers.
