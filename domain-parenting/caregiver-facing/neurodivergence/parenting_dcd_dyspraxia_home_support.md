# Coordination Difficulties (DCD / Dyspraxia) at Home

**Objective:** Help the caregiver decide whether a child's clumsiness needs medical review
or a coordination assessment, then build a home plan that teaches the skills that matter
most to the child one at a time, adapts what can be adapted, and protects confidence — with
a short list of school supports to request.

**When to Use:**
- A child is much clumsier than peers: trips, bumps into things, spills, drops things.
- Self-care skills come late: buttons, zips, shoelaces, cutlery, brushing teeth.
- Handwriting is slow, effortful, and hard to read, or the child avoids drawing and
  writing.
- The child avoids playground games, ball sports, or bikes, or is teased for being
  "uncoordinated."
- A doctor or occupational therapist has mentioned developmental coordination disorder
  (DCD), also called dyspraxia.
- **Not this prompt if** the main issue is sensory sensitivity or sensory seeking
  (noise, textures, crashing into things for input) — use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_sensory_at_home_toolkit.md`.
  For a child under 3 with motor delays, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-0-3/parenting_developmental_red_flags_0_3.md`.
  For school meetings about writing and learning, use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/academics-skills/parenting_learning_concern_school_meeting.md`.

## Safety Block — See the Doctor First If

- **Loss of skills the child already had** (walking, running, writing, speech) → see the
  pediatrician promptly; regression is not DCD.
- **Getting weaker or falling more over weeks to months**, difficulty climbing stairs,
  getting up from the floor using hands on the legs, large calves, or toe-walking that
  is new or worsening → pediatrician soon (neuromuscular causes need ruling out).
- **One side of the body weaker or used much less**, a limp, or a change in walking →
  pediatrician soon.
- **Headaches with vomiting, unsteadiness that is new, or a change in vision** →
  same-day medical care.
- **Joint pain, swelling, or frequent injuries from falls** → pediatrician.
- **The child is bullied or says they hate themselves or want to die** → address now;
  988 (US) for any talk of dying.

**DCD is diagnosed by a doctor, usually with an occupational or physical therapist's
assessment; this plan does not diagnose it.**

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, birth history (prematurity), motor milestones, speech, attention,
   learning, any diagnosis (ADHD, autism, dyslexia, speech difficulty often co-occur).
2. **Daily-life impact:** which tasks are hard (dressing, eating, hygiene, handwriting,
   sport, play) and how long they take compared with siblings or peers.
3. **The child's priorities:** what they most want to be able to do (ride a bike, tie
   shoes, keep up at football).
4. **Assessment status:** pediatrician seen? OT or PT referral? waitlist?
5. **School:** handwriting demands, PE, teacher comments, teasing.
6. **Time and resources** for practice at home.

## Method

1. **Clear the red flags (DP-05).** Any Safety Block sign → doctor before anything else.
   Otherwise, book a pediatric review with examples and a short video of the tasks, and
   ask for an occupational-therapy (and if needed physical-therapy) assessment.
2. **Understand the pattern (DS-01).** DCD affects roughly 5–6% of school-age children:
   learning and carrying out coordinated movements is much harder than expected for age,
   it affects daily life or school, it began in early development, and it is not explained
   by another medical or neurological condition or by intellectual disability. It is not
   laziness or lack of practice. It often co-occurs with ADHD, dyslexia, and speech or
   language difficulties.
3. **Pick the skills that matter to the child.** International clinical recommendations
   favour task-oriented approaches (practising the real task the child wants to do, with
   problem-solving and coaching, such as CO-OP and similar methods) over general exercises
   or process-based programs. Choose 1–2 goals at a time that the child cares about.
4. **Teach step by step (ST-02).** Break each skill into small steps; teach the last step
   first so the child finishes successfully (backward chaining — e.g., you do the bow,
   child pulls the loops tight; next week the child makes the last loop). Use the same
   words every time; let the child talk themselves through ("Goal, plan, do, check").
   Practise little and often (5–10 minutes most days) in the real setting.
5. **Adapt while learning.** Elastic laces or Velcro shoes; clothes without small buttons;
   non-slip mats and cups with lids; pencil grips and slanted boards; extra time in the
   morning routine; a checklist with pictures. Adapting is not giving up — it removes daily
   failure while the skill develops.
6. **Choose confidence-building activities.** Individual or rhythmic activities with
   steady practice (swimming, cycling, martial arts, climbing, dance) often suit better
   than fast team ball games; let the child choose; tell coaches what helps.
7. **Ask school for support.** Reduced copying; extra time for written work and tests;
   keyboarding instruction and typing for longer work by about age 8–10; marking for
   ideas rather than neatness; PE adaptations (bigger or softer balls, partner work,
   graded steps); a buddy for changing for PE; protection from teasing.
8. **Run an eight-week skill trial (DP-11).** Rate each goal weekly (0 = can't, 5 =
   independent and quick); add a child-rated "how hard was it" score.

## Output Format

```
# Coordination Support Plan — [child, age]   Date: [..]

## Medical red flags checked (none / referred)
## What we see (tasks, time taken, examples)
## Assessment: pediatrician / OT / PT — status and questions
## The child's goals (1–2 now, more later)
## Step-by-step teaching plan per goal (backward chaining, words we use)
## Adaptations at home (clothing, tools, routine)
## Activities [child] chose
## School asks
## Eight-week tracker: | Week | Goal 1 (0–5) | Goal 2 (0–5) | Child: how hard (1–5) |
```

## Verification

- [ ] Regression, weakness, and neurological signs are checked first.
- [ ] Assessment is requested with concrete examples.
- [ ] Goals are chosen with the child and limited to 1–2 at a time.
- [ ] Each goal has small steps and a consistent script.
- [ ] Adaptations are in place so daily life is not blocked while learning.
- [ ] School asks cover writing, keyboarding, and PE.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "Just clumsy, will grow out of it" | Waiting years, daily struggle | Many children do not outgrow DCD; assessment helps early. |
| Calling regression DCD | Lost skills put down to clumsiness | Loss of skills → doctor promptly. |
| Generic exercise as treatment | Balance-board drills for shoelaces | Practise the actual task the child wants. |
| Refusing adaptations | "He'll never learn if we buy Velcro" | Adapt now; teach the skill alongside. |
| Labelling as lazy or careless | Punishing messy handwriting | Output reflects motor difficulty, not effort. |
| Missing co-occurring conditions | Handwriting fixed, ADHD unnoticed | Ask about attention, reading, and speech. |

## Adaptations

- **ADHD:** short, lively practice; visual steps; reward the attempt.
- **Autistic child:** predictable routines, visual supports, and attention to sensory
  needs during practice.
- **Teen:** focus on what matters now (driving readiness, cooking, keyboard speed, gym
  confidence); respect privacy.
- **Two homes:** same words and steps in both; share the tracker.
- **Limited time or money:** OT waitlists are long; start with adaptations and one
  practised skill; many OT services publish free home guides.

## Example Output

```
# Coordination Support Plan — Ethan, 7   Date: 3 Oct

## Red flags
None: no loss of skills, no weakness, both sides used equally, no
falls getting worse. Born at 33 weeks.

## What we see
Dressing takes 25 minutes (brother, 5, takes 10). Can't do buttons or
laces. Spills at most meals. Handwriting large, slow, hard to read;
tired after 5 lines. Avoids football at break.

## Assessment
Pediatrician 10 Oct with a 1-minute video of dressing and writing; ask
for OT referral and whether to check attention (teacher mentions
drifting).

## Ethan's goals
1. Ride his bike without stabilisers (his choice).
2. Get dressed for school by himself in 15 minutes.

## Teaching plan
Bike: balance bike-style first (pedals off, scoot and glide), then
pedals on, Dad holding the seat for 3 pushes. Script: "Eyes up, push,
glide." 10 minutes, 4 days a week, in the park.
Dressing: picture checklist; Ethan does the last step of each item
first (pull socks up after Mum starts them); same order every day.

## Adaptations
Velcro school shoes; polo shirts without buttons; elastic-waist trousers;
cup with lid; non-slip placemat.

## Activities
Ethan chose swimming lessons on Saturdays.

## School asks
Reduced copying from the board; type longer work from January; mark
ideas not neatness; PE teacher told about catching (bigger, softer
ball; partner practice).

## Tracker
| Wk 1 | Bike 1 | Dressing 2 | Hard 4 |
| Wk 4 | Bike 3 | Dressing 3 | Hard 3 |
```

## Techniques Used

- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — regression, weakness, and neurological signs go to the doctor before any home plan.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — the DCD criteria and the task-oriented approach recommended in international guidance.
- **ST-02 Structured Sequential Instructions** — skills broken into small steps taught by backward chaining with a consistent script.
- **DP-11 Safe Experiment Design** — an eight-week tracked trial of 1–2 child-chosen goals.
