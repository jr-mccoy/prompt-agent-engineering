# Homework Battles Plan (Ages 5–8)

**Objective:** Turn a nightly homework fight with a young child into a short, predictable
routine — sized to what the teacher actually intends, matched to the real driver of the
resistance, with small choices that belong to the child — and give the caregiver clear
signs for when the struggle points to a learning issue that the school should evaluate.

**When to Use:**
- A 5–8-year-old cries, stalls, or refuses at homework most evenings, and the adult ends
  up nagging, bribing, or doing it for them.
- Homework that "should take 15 minutes" takes an hour, or caregivers disagree on rules.
- The child seems to struggle with one subject (usually reading or writing) far more
  than others.
- **Not this prompt if** the child is 9–12 and the goal is handing the system over to
  them — use `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-9-12/parenting_homework_autonomy_handoff_9_12.md`.
  If the child has ADHD and you need whole-day scaffolds (morning, bedtime,
  transitions), use `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_adhd_executive_function_scaffold.md`.
  If you have already seen the warning signs below and need to prepare for the school
  meeting, go to `parenting_learning_concern_school_meeting.md`. This prompt is the
  home routine for a young child.

## Safety Block

- **Homework fights involve hitting, rough handling, or threats from an adult** → stop
  homework for the week; the relationship and the child's safety come first. If an
  adult is losing control regularly, see
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-4-8/parenting_parent_coregulation_reset.md`.
- **The child says things like "I'm stupid, I want to die," hits themselves, or has
  panic-level distress** → pause homework and use
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/mental-health-behavior/parenting_child_suicide_talk_response_6_12.md`
  for any talk of dying; call the pediatrician for daily panic or stomachaches.
- **The child cannot see the board, squints, or does not respond when called** → vision
  and hearing check with the pediatrician before assuming a behaviour problem.

**This plan organizes home support; it does not diagnose a learning disability.**
Only a qualified evaluator can do that.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Child:** age, grade, temperament, any known diagnosis or concern (ADHD, speech,
   anxiety), sleep and after-school schedule.
2. **The homework:** what comes home, how often, and what the teacher says it should
   take. If unknown, ask the teacher before anything else.
3. **What a bad night looks like:** when it starts, which task, what the child says and
   does, what the adult does, how it ends.
4. **Subject pattern:** is it all homework, or mostly reading, writing, or math?
5. **What has been tried:** rewards, taking away screens, sitting beside them, doing it
   for them.
6. **Household constraints:** work hours, siblings, shared custody across two homes.

## Method

1. **Size the load first (DS-01).** A widely cited guideline (Cooper's "10-minute
   rule," endorsed by the US National PTA) is about 10 minutes per grade per night:
   K–1 about 10 minutes, grade 2 about 20, grade 3 about 30. Research finds little
   academic benefit from homework in the early grades beyond reading practice, so the
   fight is rarely worth the relationship cost. Ask the teacher: "How long should this
   take? What should I do if it takes much longer?"
2. **Name the dominant driver (DP-06).** Pick one primary driver from the bad-night
   log; the plan is built around it.
   - **Fatigue/hunger:** fights start immediately after a long day; better on weekends.
   - **Attention/executive function:** starts are hard, child drifts, loses materials,
     improves sharply with an adult nearby.
   - **Skill gap:** fights cluster on one subject; child guesses, avoids, or says "I
     can't"; easy tasks go fine.
   - **Anxiety/perfectionism:** erasing until the paper tears, tears over one mistake.
   - **Power struggle:** child can do it, does it fine for a grandparent or tutor, and
     the battle is with one adult.
3. **Build a short, predictable routine.** Same place, same start time chosen to suit
   the driver (snack and 30 minutes of play first for fatigue; straight after school for
   a child who cannot restart later). Materials ready before the child sits down. Use a
   visual timer and a "first–then" card ("First spelling, then Lego").
4. **Set a time cap and a stop-and-note rule.** When the cap is reached (the teacher's
   intended time plus 10 minutes), homework stops. The adult writes a one-line note:
   "Worked 25 minutes, stuck on reading the word problems." This turns a fight into
   information for the teacher.
5. **Give real choices (autonomy support).** Which task first, pencil or marker, kitchen
   table or desk, a break now or after three problems. Children supported this way do
   better than those who are controlled; the adult stays nearby rather than next to
   them, and helps with prompts ("What does the first sound say?") rather than answers.
6. **Match the response to the driver.** Skill gap → make it smaller and tell the
   teacher; attention → break into 5-minute chunks with movement breaks; anxiety →
   model mistakes ("I wrote it wrong — I'll cross it out"), praise effort and strategy;
   power struggle → step back and let the natural school consequence happen, or let
   another adult own homework.
7. **Run a two-week trial (DP-11).** Track nightly minutes, start delay, and how the
   evening ended (calm / grumbly / meltdown). Change one variable at a time.
8. **Escalation gate (DP-05).** Any warning sign below, persisting through the trial,
   means a teacher conversation within two weeks and a request for evaluation if the
   school agrees there is a gap — not more pressure at home.

## Output Format

```
# Homework Plan — [child, age, grade]   Trial: [dates]

## Load check
Teacher's intended time: [..]   Actual time now: [..]   Cap: [..]
## Dominant driver: [..] (evidence from the log)
## Routine
Start time, place, snack/break, materials, timer, first–then
## Choices the child owns
## How the adult helps (prompts, not answers)
## Stop-and-note rule (wording of the note)
## Driver-specific adjustments
## Two-week tracker
| Night | Minutes | Start delay | Ending (calm/grumbly/meltdown) | Note |
## Warning signs → talk to teacher / request evaluation
```

## Verification

- [ ] Teacher's intended time is known (or a request to ask is the first step).
- [ ] One dominant driver is named with evidence from the log.
- [ ] Time cap and stop-and-note wording are explicit.
- [ ] At least three real choices are given to the child.
- [ ] Trial tracker has measurable fields and changes one variable at a time.
- [ ] Learning-issue warning signs are listed with the next step.

## False-Positive Prevention

| Misfire | What it looks like | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Calling it laziness or defiance | "He just won't try" | Check skill gap first; children avoid what is hard for them. |
| Reading a skill gap as ADHD | Struggles only with reading tasks | Subject-specific trouble points to a learning skill, not attention. |
| More time as the fix | Two-hour sessions to "finish" | Cap the time; overtime is information for the teacher. |
| Adult doing the work | Neat homework, child learned nothing | Teacher needs to see the real level; send the honest version. |
| Rewards for a skill gap | Sticker chart for reading he can't do | Rewards help motivation, not missing skills. |
| Waiting to "see if it clicks" | Months of tears over reading | Warning signs justify a teacher talk now; early help works best. |

**Warning signs that suggest a learning issue (talk to the teacher):** homework in one
subject regularly takes more than twice the intended time; reading or spelling far
behind classmates; still reversing or guessing at many simple words in late grade 1;
can explain ideas aloud but cannot get them on paper; trouble remembering math facts
despite daily practice; a family history of reading or learning difficulty.

## Adaptations

- **ADHD:** shorter chunks, body breaks, adult nearby; pair with the ADHD scaffold.
- **Two homes:** one shared routine card and one note format so the teacher sees one
  picture; agree the time cap with the co-parent.
- **Multilingual family:** help in the home language is fine.
- **Shift-working caregiver:** a grandparent, sitter, or after-school program can own
  homework; the evening adult only reviews the note.

## Example Output

```
# Homework Plan — Maya, 7, grade 2   Trial: 6–17 Oct

## Load check
Teacher (asked by email 3 Oct): "About 20 minutes: reading log + one
math page." Actual now: 60–75 minutes, 4 nights a week. Cap: 30 min.

## Dominant driver: skill gap in reading
Math page done in 8 minutes without help. Reading log and word
problems trigger tears, guessing from pictures, "I can't read this."
Fine on weekends with Grandma only when Grandma reads aloud.

## Routine
4:15 snack + outdoor play; 4:45 kitchen table; materials in a basket.
Visual timer 10 min, 2-min break, 10 min. First–then card:
"First math, then reading, then bike."

## Choices Maya owns
Math or reading first; pencil colour; which activity after.

## How the adult helps
Prompts: "Look at the first letter. What sound?" Not: "That says
'horse.'"

## Stop-and-note
At 30 minutes: "Maya worked 30 minutes. Math done alone. Reading:
stuck on most two-syllable words; guessed from pictures."

## Driver-specific
Paired reading log (Dad page, Maya page); Dad reads word problems aloud.

## Tracker (first days)
| Mon | 30 | 2 min | calm    | note sent |
| Tue | 30 | 5 min | grumbly | note sent |

## Warning signs present
Reading takes >3x intended time; guessing from pictures in grade 2;
Mum had reading trouble in school. → Email teacher 20 Oct asking
for her reading screening scores and a meeting; prepare with
parenting_learning_concern_school_meeting.md.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DP-06 Dominant Driver Identification** — one named driver from the bad-night log shapes the whole plan.
- **DS-01 Framework Application** — the 10-minute-per-grade guideline and autonomy-support research size the load and set the adult's role.
- **DP-11 Safe Experiment Design** — a two-week, one-variable-at-a-time trial with a tracker.
- **DP-05 Stakes-Based Gate Policy** — persistent learning warning signs move the issue to the school rather than to more home pressure.
