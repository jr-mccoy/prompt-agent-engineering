**Purpose:** Help a caregiver respond to a tween's friendship rupture, exclusion event, or social drama in a way that supports the tween rather than rushing to fix, that builds the tween's own conflict-navigation skills, and that knows when relational dynamics cross into bullying or warrant adult intervention.

**When to use:** Tween upset about a fight with a best friend, group-text exclusion, social-media unfollow drama, third-party gossip, "no one likes me anymore" reports, sudden friend-group shift, after-school crying.

**When NOT to use:** Bullying patterns (use bullying-target prompt), suspected school avoidance from friendship distress (school-refusal prompt), trauma context, suicidal ideation tied to friendship loss (clinical support).

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Suicidal ideation tied to social distress → mental health, today.
- Sustained social isolation lasting weeks with depressive features → clinical support.
- Bullying pattern (repeated, power-imbalanced, intentional harm) → escalate to school + bullying protocol.
- Severe online harassment → safety planning + platform reporting.
- Self-harm signals → mental health.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Validate before solve.** A tween whose caregiver immediately problem-solves feels unheard.
2. **Naming the dynamic is half the work.** "Triangulation," "frenemy," "exclusion," "loyalty bind," "third-party drama" — once a tween has a word for what's happening, they can think about it.
3. **The caregiver's role is coaching, not negotiation.** Caregivers calling other parents (without specific reason) often makes it worse. The tween needs to develop the skill.
4. **Tween friendships shift fast and re-form fast.** Today's catastrophe may be tomorrow's resolution. Don't catastrophize alongside.
5. **Some friend losses are real and lasting.** Don't reassure away grief.
6. **Relational aggression** (rumor, exclusion, social-media manipulation) **looks different from physical bullying but is just as damaging.** Recognize it as legitimate.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Lead with validation, not solving.
- Name the dynamic.
- Distinguish typical friendship friction from bullying.
- Scaffold the tween's own response.
- Know when to escalate.
- Address caregiver's own activation.

**Must Not:**
- Default to "ignore them" or "they're not worth it."
- Default to caregiver-to-caregiver call as first move.
- Solve without listening.
- Catastrophize.
- Frame the other tween as a villain.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Open With Listening

Caregiver opening:
- "Tell me what happened from the start."
- "How are you feeling about it right now?"
- "What's the part that hurt most?"

Caregiver body: face open, phone away, no fixing yet. 5–15 minutes of listening before any response.

Reflect what you heard: "So [Friend] said [X] in front of [others], and that's been going on since [time]. And what's hurting most is [the part]."

### Stage 2 — Validate

Acknowledge the feeling, even if the surface story sounds small.

- "That sounds really painful."
- "Of course you feel left out — you have been."
- "It makes sense you're confused. That's a confusing thing for a friend to do."

Avoid:
- "That's not a big deal."
- "Just ignore them."
- "Kids will be kids."
- "When I was your age..."
- Premature solving.

### Stage 3 — Name the Dynamic

Help the tween name what's happening. Common patterns:

- **Triangulation:** Friend A talks about Friend B to the tween, then about the tween to B.
- **Loyalty bind:** "If you're friends with [X], you can't be friends with [Y]."
- **Exclusion as power:** Group leaves one out to demonstrate group cohesion or power hierarchy.
- **Frenemy:** A friend who is sometimes warm, sometimes cruel. Often charismatic.
- **Drift:** Slow loss of closeness without specific event.
- **Group reorganization:** Friend group expanding or contracting; some lose seats.
- **Online-amplified IRL conflict:** Group chat or social media inflates a small in-person event.
- **Third-party drama:** Rumor, gossip, "she said you said."

Saying: "What you're describing sounds like [X]. Have you heard that word? It's a real pattern. Here's what often happens with it..."

### Stage 4 — Differentiate Typical from Bullying

Typical friendship friction:
- Among equals.
- Specific event or cause.
- Both parties hurt.
- Tween has agency.
- Resolves or shifts.

Bullying:
- Power imbalance.
- Repeated.
- Intentional harm.
- Tween relatively powerless.
- Patterned over time.

If pattern is bullying: see `parenting_bullying_target_response_protocol.md`.

### Stage 5 — Scaffold the Tween's Response

Ask:
- "What do you wish would happen?"
- "What have you already tried?"
- "What feels possible to try?"

Co-design 1–3 possible moves the tween could make. Examples:
- "Have a direct conversation with [friend] when you can be alone."
- "Take a step back from the group chat for a few days."
- "Spend time with [different friend] you've been close to before."
- "Talk to [trusted adult at school] about feeling left out."
- "Wait it out for [time period] and reassess."

Don't pick for the tween. Let them choose.

### Stage 6 — Decide on Caregiver Action

Caregiver-to-caregiver contact:
- Yes, when: bullying with specific incidents; safety concern; online posting that needs takedown; child welfare relevant.
- No, when: friendship friction the tweens can navigate; rumor that the tween wants to handle; parent-call would amplify the drama.

Caregiver-to-school contact:
- Yes, when: pattern at school requiring teacher/counselor awareness; bullying; sustained distress affecting school function.
- No, when: weekend social-media drama not at school; tween's stated preference is to handle without school.

### Stage 7 — Caregiver Self-Regulation

- Check the urge to solve. Whose anxiety is being soothed by solving?
- Avoid sharing your own teen-friendship trauma in a way that overshadows the tween's experience.
- Don't bad-mouth the other tween or other parents. The tween will hear it; the friendship may resolve; you'll have to walk that back.
- Don't let the situation become caregiver-vs-caregiver drama.

### Stage 8 — Recovery Tracking

Over 2–6 weeks:
- Sleep, appetite, mood, school engagement, willingness to attend events.
- New friendship signals.
- Conversation patterns about the situation.

If sustained distress >2–4 weeks despite supportive response: clinical support.

## Adaptations

- **Anxious tween:** Avoidance pattern (refusing school, dropping activities) is high-risk; lean into approach with scaffolding.
- **Autistic tween:** Social-rule decoding may be needed (`parenting_hfa_social_situation_decoder.md`); explicit naming of unwritten rules.
- **ADHD tween:** Rejection sensitivity is real; emotion-regulation work in parallel with social-skill work.
- **2e tween (gifted + ND):** Asymmetric peer match; interest-based community can be a real resource.
- **Recently moved or changed schools:** Friendship-loss grief is real; expect 6–12 months for new attachments.
- **LGBTQ+ tween:** Affinity groups (online or local) can be a resource; existing friendships may shift around identity.
- **Cultural / immigrant context:** First-language friendships matter; bridging cultures.
- **Sibling dynamics affecting friend group:** Consider whether sibling is interfering or magnifying.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Solve before listening | 5–15 min listening, then validate, then name |
| Default to "ignore them" | Acknowledge real harm; help respond |
| Default to caregiver-to-caregiver call | Only when bullying or safety; otherwise scaffold |
| Frame other tween as villain | Will likely have to walk it back when they reconcile |
| Catastrophize ("they'll never recover") | Tween friendships shift fast |
| Reassure away grief | Some losses are real; honor |
| Pick the tween's response for them | Coach options; let them choose |
| Skip the dynamic name | Naming is half the work |
| Confuse drama with bullying | Differentiate by power + repetition + intent |
| Forget caregiver's own activation | Whose anxiety is being soothed? |
| Pathologize a 2-week social rough patch | Track over weeks; most resolve |
| Pull from school over typical friction | School avoidance is its own problem |
