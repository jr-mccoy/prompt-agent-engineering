**Purpose:** Build a response when your tween is the one bullying — identify what kind of bullying, address it without shame-collapse, run real accountability with the target, and address the underlying driver (status, modeling, mental health, peer pressure, conflict skill gap, history of being bullied themselves).

**When to use:** School informed you, target's family contacted you, you witnessed the behavior, you discovered it on devices, your tween confessed, repeated reports across settings.

**When NOT to use:** Defensive reaction without yet investigating. Cases where your tween's behavior is in fact a response to being bullied themselves (a different protocol). Mental health crisis driving the behavior — clinical first.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Targeted aggression, lack of remorse, sustained pattern despite intervention → conduct disorder evaluation.
- Cruel patterns toward animals or younger siblings + sustained → mental health, urgent.
- Bullying pattern post-trauma (abuse, witnessed violence) → trauma-informed clinical response.
- Severe escalation (weapons, sexual coercion, image-based abuse) → safety, possibly law enforcement, mental health.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Most tween bullying is a peer-status move, not a sociopathic pattern.** It's correctable.
2. **Shame collapses accountability.** A tween made to feel like a monster cannot do the relational work of repair.
3. **Bullying often runs in families' modeling.** Adults' speech about other people, mocking, contempt, rage at out-groups gets imitated.
4. **Bullies-who-were-bullied are common.** The pattern flips. Intervention has to address both directions.
5. **The target's experience is the data, not the bully's intent.** "I was just joking" is irrelevant if it landed as harm.
6. **Real accountability includes consequences AND repair.** Not consequences alone; not apology alone.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Treat the report seriously without prejudging guilt.
- Run accountability without shame-collapse.
- Address the driver, not just the behavior.
- Coordinate with school.
- Repair with target appropriately.
- Address caregiver flooding.

**Must Not:**
- Default to "kids will be kids."
- Default to defending tween without investigation.
- Default to harsh punishment that doesn't address cause.
- Force a public apology that retraumatizes the target.
- Skip the underlying driver.
- Frame your tween as a monster.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Investigate, Don't Pre-Judge

When you get the report:
- Listen completely before defending or convicting.
- Ask the school: specific incidents, witnesses, evidence.
- Ask your tween: what happened, multiple times, calm, separate from the announcement.
- Compare accounts.
- Don't share school's account verbatim with tween — that becomes a defense template.
- Don't tell tween "the school is wrong" or "I'll handle them" before investigating.

### Stage 2 — Confront Without Shame

Open with the tween:
- "I've heard something concerning. I want to understand from you."
- "I love you. I will love you no matter what. And I need to understand what happened."
- "Take your time."

Listen. Then respond to the behavior, not the person:
- "What I'm hearing is that you [specific behavior]. That's not okay. We're going to address it."

Avoid:
- "You're a bully now."
- "I'm so disappointed in you."
- "How could you do this — what's wrong with you?"
- Public family-shaming.

Shame-collapse leads to: defensiveness, denial, lying, and an inability to do repair.

### Stage 3 — Identify the Driver

Common drivers in tween bullying:

- **Status:** Bullying as social currency in the group. Bully gets popularity points.
- **Retaliation:** "She did it first / she said something about me first."
- **Bystander cascade:** Group bullied; tween joined in.
- **Modeling:** Caregiver or older sibling speaks contemptuously about others; tween imitates.
- **Conflict skill gap:** Doesn't have words; uses cruelty.
- **Bullied-becoming-bully:** Past target now flips dynamic on a perceived weaker peer.
- **Mental health:** Depression, anxiety, ADHD impulsivity, autism social-rule misread.
- **Group identity:** "We're the [group]; they're the [other group]."
- **Online disinhibition:** Behavior the tween wouldn't do in person.
- **Trauma:** Aggression as nervous-system response.

State the most likely driver explicitly and address it.

### Stage 4 — Consequences + Repair

**Consequences:**
- Match severity of behavior. Not draconian; not negligible.
- Loss of access to the channel of harm (phone, app, specific class group).
- Lost privileges restored when conditions met.

**Repair:**
- Target's needs come first. Some targets do not want apology; respect that.
- If repair welcomed: tween writes apology that names specific harm, takes responsibility without "I'm sorry you felt," states what they will do differently. Caregiver reviews.
- For online harm: remove the post, if possible publicly correct.
- For physical / property harm: replace, restitute.
- For relational / exclusion: not "go be friends." Often: a sustained pattern of decency without re-friending.

Apology + behavior change > apology alone.

### Stage 5 — Address the Driver

- **Status driver:** Conversations about what kind of person the tween wants to be; values clarification; modeling other status routes.
- **Modeling driver:** Audit family speech. The tween is mirroring something. Adults change first.
- **Conflict skill gap:** Teach: how to disagree, walk away, name a boundary, ask for space.
- **Bullied-becoming-bully:** Address both wounds; therapist can help.
- **Mental health:** Pediatric and mental health pathway.
- **Online disinhibition:** Tighter device structure; specific apps removed.
- **Trauma:** Trauma-informed therapy.

### Stage 6 — School Coordination

- Engage with the school's discipline process; don't fight it reflexively.
- Ask: "What's the plan for our tween's behavior change? What support is offered? What's the consequence we agree on?"
- Document.
- If school recommends counseling, follow through.
- Do not let your tween become "the bullying kid" — but also don't deny if the pattern is real.

### Stage 7 — Caregiver Self-Audit

Hard questions:
- Do you speak contemptuously about others (other parents, teachers, in-laws, ex-spouses, public figures, coworkers)?
- Is humor in your home about cutting people down?
- Do you mock vulnerability — your own or others'?
- Have you modeled exclusion, gossip, or social manipulation?
- Has your tween been targeted in ways you minimized?

Family-level change is usually part of the protocol.

### Stage 8 — Tracking and Follow-Up

Over 4–8 weeks:
- New patterns observed.
- Communication with school.
- Communication with target's family if relationship is salvageable.
- Therapy progress.
- Tween's emotional state.

If pattern persists 8+ weeks despite consistent intervention: escalation to clinical, including conduct disorder evaluation if other signs.

## Adaptations

- **Autistic tween:** Sometimes the "bullying" is social-rule misread (mocking that the tween didn't recognize as cruel) — doesn't excuse, but changes intervention. Direct teaching of unwritten rules.
- **ADHD tween:** Impulsivity-driven cruelty looks different from sustained pattern; address impulse + repair.
- **Trauma history:** Therapeutic response, not just behavioral.
- **Tween was bullied first:** Address both sides.
- **Co-parents differing:** Align on minimum agreed accountability before tween conversation; don't fragment.
- **Stepfamily:** Step-parent's role in accountability defined; biological parent leads.
- **Family modeling driver:** Adult work primary; tween work derivative.
- **Online specifically:** Device structure tightened; specific platform restrictions.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to "kids will be kids" | Take seriously; investigate |
| Defend tween without investigation | Listen to school + tween; compare |
| Shame-collapse the tween | Address behavior; preserve self |
| Force public apology that retraumatizes target | Target leads repair shape |
| Punish without addressing driver | Driver-specific intervention |
| Skip caregiver self-audit | Modeling is often part of it |
| Ignore school recommendations reflexively | Engage; don't reject |
| Treat one incident as identity | Address the behavior; preserve the person |
| Force re-friending after exclusion | Sustained decency, not forced friendship |
| Treat online harm as less real | Equal weight |
| Skip clinical pathway for mental-health driver | Mental health pathway central if relevant |
| Forget the autism / ADHD / trauma confound | Differentiate impulsivity / misread / trauma response |
