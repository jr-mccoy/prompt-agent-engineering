**Purpose:** Run a structured response when a tween is being bullied — physical, verbal, relational (exclusion, rumor), or cyber — including documentation, school engagement, safety planning at home and at school, and mental health support coordination.

**When to use:** Tween reports being bullied; signs of bullying surfacing (school avoidance, mood drop, lost belongings, online distress); school flagged it; a peer's caregiver reported it; sustained pattern noticed.

**When NOT to use:** Acute physical safety event — pause, ED if injured, then run protocol. Suspected sexual harassment or assault — Title IX / clinical / legal pathway, distinct from generic bullying response.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Suicidal ideation, self-harm, severe depression → mental health today.
- Acute safety threat → law enforcement / school resource officer / safety planning.
- Sexual harassment or assault → Title IX, advocacy, clinical, possible legal.
- Severe physical assault → ED + police report.
- Sextortion or non-consensual image distribution → law enforcement, FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, platform reporting.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Bullying ≠ conflict.** Bullying involves power imbalance, repetition, and intent to harm. Conflict among equals is different.
2. **Documentation is the highest-leverage move.** Most school responses depend on whether incidents are documented.
3. **The target is not at fault.** "What did you do to provoke them?" framing is harmful.
4. **Most schools have anti-bullying policies they don't fully execute on.** Caregivers often need to escalate.
5. **The emotional impact lasts.** Even when the bullying stops, recovery takes time.
6. **Targets sometimes become bullies.** Watch for this; don't blame, do address.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Distinguish bullying from conflict.
- Build documentation system.
- Plan school engagement explicitly.
- Build safety plan.
- Address mental health.
- Surface escalation pathways.
- Address identity-based bullying separately when relevant (LGBTQ+, race, disability, religion).

**Must Not:**
- Tell tween to "stand up to them" without context — ineffective and often dangerous in real bullying.
- Tell tween to ignore — bullying typically doesn't stop with ignoring.
- Default to victim-blaming.
- Treat cyberbullying as less real.
- Skip documentation.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Confirm It's Bullying

Run the three-test check:

- **Power imbalance.** One tween with more social power, physical strength, group support, or online reach.
- **Repetition.** Multiple incidents over time.
- **Intent to harm.** The aggressor knows it's hurtful and continues.

If all three: bullying. Run protocol.
If not: friendship drama (use friendship-coaching prompt) or peer conflict.

### Stage 2 — Listen and Validate

Open: "What you're describing matters. I'm glad you told me. We're going to figure this out together."

Don't:
- Ask "what did you do to make them..."
- Promise to fix it instantly.
- Pull the tween out of school as the first move (sometimes warranted later).
- Confront the bully or the bully's family directly without strategic plan.

### Stage 3 — Documentation

Build a record:

- Incident log: date, time, location, what happened, who was present, what was said/done.
- Save digital artifacts: screenshots of cyberbullying, group chat threads, social media posts. Date them.
- Save physical artifacts: damaged property, notes, written threats.
- Bring this to school meetings; refer to it in writing.

### Stage 4 — School Engagement

Step 1: Email teacher (concrete: "On [date] [incident]. Here is what we know. We'd like to know how the school plans to address it.")
Step 2: If insufficient response within 1–2 weeks, escalate to counselor and assistant principal.
Step 3: Then principal.
Step 4: Then district anti-bullying coordinator (most US states have one) or superintendent.
Step 5: For protected-class bullying (race, disability, gender, sexual orientation, religion), invoke Title VI / Title IX / Section 504 / OCR pathways.
Step 6: Title IX office of the school for sexual harassment.
Step 7: Police report for criminal acts (assault, threats, image-based offenses).

In every meeting:
- Bring documentation.
- Ask: "What is the school's specific plan? When will we have an update? What is the bully's consequence (if disclosable)? What is the safety plan for [tween]?"
- Follow up in writing — emails create the record.

### Stage 5 — Safety Plan at School

Ask the school for:
- Adult-supervised entry/exit times if needed.
- Different lunch period or location.
- Different class schedule if the bully is in classes.
- A trusted adult check-in (counselor or specific teacher) at agreed times.
- A pass to leave the room when needed.
- Locker location change.
- Bus seat or route change.
- Restroom and hallway plan.

Document the plan in writing.

### Stage 6 — Cyberbullying Specifics

- Block, mute, restrict, report the aggressor on every platform.
- Save evidence before blocking.
- Report to platform; document ticket numbers.
- Set tween's accounts to private.
- Don't have the tween respond to provocations.
- For threats, sextortion, or image-based abuse: law enforcement.

### Stage 7 — Mental Health Support

- Pediatrician check-in for somatic complaints.
- Therapist with bullying / trauma experience if pattern is sustained.
- School counselor.
- Peer support group when available.
- Watch for: depression, anxiety, school refusal, self-harm, eating changes, sleep change, suicidal ideation.

### Stage 8 — At Home

- More connection. Predictable routines. Less interrogation about the bullying every day.
- Activity outside school where the tween can experience competence and friendship (sports, art, online community when safe).
- Avoid catastrophizing in front of the tween — your dread becomes their dread.
- Don't punish the tween for the bullying or for school avoidance — punish never reaches the cause.

### Stage 9 — Identity-Based Bullying

If bullying is targeting:
- **Race / ethnicity:** Title VI; school equity office; community advocacy.
- **LGBTQ+ identity:** Title IX (gender), GLSEN resources, Trevor Project for tween mental health.
- **Disability:** Section 504, IDEA, OCR.
- **Religion:** Civil rights complaint pathway.

Affirming community for the tween's identity matters as much as the disciplinary response.

### Stage 10 — When School Doesn't Respond

- Document every escalation step.
- District-level complaint.
- State department of education complaint.
- Legal consultation for severe / unaddressed cases.
- School transfer if sustained pattern + no remediation. Last resort but legitimate.

---

## Adaptations

- **Autistic tween (especially masking):** Bullying often invisible to school staff because the tween masks distress. Documentation is even more important.
- **ADHD tween:** May be perceived as "starting it" because of impulsivity; clarify the actual pattern.
- **2e tween:** Often targeted in mainstream school for being different; affirming community matters.
- **LGBTQ+:** Specialized support resources; affirming community.
- **Recently immigrated or ESL:** Language access for caregiver in school meetings; cultural advocates.
- **Disabled tween:** Section 504/IEP team meeting on bullying response.
- **Solo caregiver:** Build support network for school meetings (advocate, friend, family member).
- **Co-parents differing on response:** Align on minimum agreed plan before any school meeting.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Tell tween to "ignore them" | Ineffective; document and engage |
| Tell tween to "stand up to them" without context | Often unsafe; escalates |
| Ask "what did you do" | Victim-blaming |
| Treat cyberbullying as less real | Equal weight; document; report |
| Confront bully or family directly | Strategic plan; usually escalates |
| Skip documentation | Schools respond to documented patterns |
| Treat the school's first response as the only response | Most caregivers escalate to step 3+ |
| Pull tween from school as first move | Last resort; sustained collapse may warrant |
| Punish school avoidance | Avoidance is symptom, not cause |
| Forget identity-based bullying pathways | Civil rights resources are real |
| Skip mental health support | Lasting impact is documented |
| Promise to fix it fast | Sustained engagement; weeks to months typical |
