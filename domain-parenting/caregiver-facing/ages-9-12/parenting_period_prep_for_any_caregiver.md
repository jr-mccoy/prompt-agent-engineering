**Purpose:** Equip any caregiver — including those who haven't experienced menstruation themselves — to prepare a tween for menarche, manage the first period, set up product systems, address pain and tracking, and handle emergencies away from home.

**When to use:** Tween approaching age 9–13, breast budding starting, growth spurt happening, single-caregiver household where the caregiver doesn't menstruate, foster placement of a tween whose body needs prep, stepparent or grandparent role.

**When NOT to use:** Suspected medical menstrual concern (severe pain interfering with function, very heavy flow, very irregular pattern post first 2 years, signs of PCOS or endometriosis) — pediatric or adolescent medicine routing.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Severe pain (missing school, doubling over) → pediatric / adolescent gynecology.
- Bleeding heavy enough to soak a pad/tampon every hour for several hours → urgent.
- No period by 15 with secondary sex characteristics, or no breast development by 13 → pediatric.
- Suspected eating disorder + missed periods → urgent.
- Self-harm, severe mood disruption around periods (PMDD signs) → mental health.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Anyone can be the period-prep caregiver.** Knowledge is portable. Comfort builds with practice.
2. **Menarche typically 9–15 years, median ~12.** Earlier (after 8) and later (up to 15) are within range.
3. **Practical preparation matters more than emotional set-up.** A pad in the backpack and a knowledge of where the school nurse keeps supplies prevents most early period crises.
4. **Body autonomy and ease > shame management.** Casual normalcy is the target.
5. **Cycle education is health education.** A tween who tracks their cycle and understands hormonal flux makes better health decisions throughout life.
6. **Pain is not normal at the level of "missing school."** Workable pain is normal; functional impairment is medical.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Caregiver Knowledge Setup

Caregiver who hasn't menstruated, learn:
- Cycle basics (typical 21–35 days, period 3–7 days, first 2 years often irregular).
- Product types and how each works (below).
- Pain options and threshold for medical attention.
- Where the school nurse keeps supplies.
- A plan for when the tween calls from school.

If you're uncomfortable: practice handling pads/tampons before the tween's prep talk. Open a package, look at the products, read the instructions.

### Stage 2 — Product Knowledge

| Product | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Pads (disposable)** | Adhesive, in underwear | First periods; sleep; light to heavy flow |
| **Pads (reusable cloth)** | Wash and reuse | Eco-conscious; some skin sensitivity |
| **Tampons** | Inserted; absorb internally | Active days; swimming; once tween is ready |
| **Menstrual cups** | Silicone, inserted, reusable | Often after age 14+; longer duration |
| **Period underwear (Thinx, Knix, etc.)** | Built-in absorbency | Backup; light days; sleep |
| **Menstrual discs** | Inserted differently than cup | Older adolescents typically |

Recommendation:
- Start with pads. Various sizes (light, regular, super, overnight, panty liner).
- Add period underwear as backup.
- Tampons when tween wants and feels ready (often months to years later). With instructions, slow practice, hand mirror if helpful, no rush.
- Menstrual cup typically older adolescent.

### Stage 3 — Pre-Period Setup

- Stock the bathroom with various pad sizes.
- Place a small kit (2–3 pads, spare underwear, small wipes, plastic bag for soiled clothes) in the tween's backpack.
- Identify where school keeps supplies.
- Identify a trusted adult at school the tween can ask.
- Show the tween how to read a pad package (light, regular, super, etc.).
- Practice opening, applying, removing, disposing.

### Stage 4 — The First Period — At Home

What to expect:
- Could be brown, dark red, bright red, light pink. All normal.
- Light flow first 1–3 days, often.
- Cramps possible.
- Mood shift possible.
- Could be days or 2 weeks long; first periods variable.

What to do:
- Calm, matter-of-fact response.
- Compliment the tween for telling you (no awkward celebration unless they want it).
- Set the tween up with products + pain plan.
- Don't broadcast to extended family without consent.

### Stage 5 — The First Period — Away from Home

Pre-load the response:

- "If you start your period at school: go to the bathroom. Use a pad from your backpack kit. If you don't have one: ask the nurse, or the school office. They have free supplies. They will not make a big deal."
- "If you bleed through clothes: tie a sweater around your waist. Go to the nurse. Call me. I'll bring spare underwear and clothes within [time]."
- "If at a friend's house: tell the friend's parent or your friend. Most parents have supplies. If not, call me."

This pre-load prevents the most common first-period crisis: silent panic in a bathroom stall.

### Stage 6 — Pain Management

For typical period pain:
- Heat (heating pad, warm bath).
- Hydration.
- Movement (walking, gentle yoga).
- Ibuprofen at pediatric-appropriate dose with food (best taken at the very start of cramps, before they peak).
- Sleep priority.

Track: how much pain, how often, what helps. If sustained pain interferes with school, sleep, or activities → pediatric / adolescent gynecology referral. Endometriosis is underdiagnosed; severe period pain is not "just part of being a girl."

### Stage 7 — Cycle Tracking

- App: tween-friendly options (Clue, Flo with privacy controls, paper calendar, simple notebook).
- Track: start date, length, flow, pain, mood.
- Privacy: caregiver doesn't read tracker without permission once tween is using it.
- Use: helps identify patterns, predict, and surface medical concerns over time.

### Stage 8 — Emotional Framing

- "Periods are a body fact. They're not embarrassing. They're not gross. They're not a curse. They're not a celebration unless you want them to be. They're normal."
- "Some cultures celebrate menarche; some are private about it. Either is okay. Up to you."
- "Some people will joke about periods. You can ignore, push back, or laugh — your call."
- "If you have feelings about getting your period — sad, scared, embarrassed, indifferent — those are all real."

### Stage 9 — Cultural and Religious Practices

Many traditions have specific menstruation practices (some communities have privacy norms, some have ritual observances, some have practical exclusions during certain religious observances). Honor the tween's family framework.

If the tween is in a household where the family practice differs from the school environment: pre-load language for handling that gap.

### Stage 10 — Co-Caregivers and Co-Parents

- Both caregivers know the basics.
- Period kit in both households if split custody.
- Stepparent / family-friend / aunt support is welcome if tween wants it; not forced.
- Sibling privacy respected.

## Adaptations

- **Caregiver who hasn't menstruated (any gender):** Equally capable; learn the basics; ask the pediatrician if needed.
- **Foster placement / kinship care:** Tween may have less prep than expected; do the catch-up gently.
- **Autistic tween:** Sensory issues with pads/tampons real; try multiple products; period underwear often easier.
- **Tween with sensory disorder:** Texture, fit, scent matter; pursue trial-and-error.
- **Disabled tween:** Adaptations for use, hygiene; coordinate with care team.
- **Trans / non-binary tween with female-typical body:** Affirming framing; period products may be in any underwear; some tweens prefer "menstruating" over "having a period," etc.
- **Single-male-caregiver of a tween with female-typical body:** Plenty of dads have done this well. School nurse, pediatrician, family friend, online resource, books — all available.
- **Cultural / religious practice:** Embed within tradition; honor.
- **Athletic tween:** Cycle awareness for performance and pain management.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Treat dads / non-menstruating caregivers as inadequate | Equally capable; learn the basics |
| Default to pads only | Multiple product types; tween's choice |
| Skip away-from-home protocol | First-period-at-school is the most common crisis |
| Treat "missing school due to pain" as normal | Threshold for medical |
| Promote one app | Privacy-respecting options; paper okay |
| Read tween's tracker without permission | Privacy is the deal |
| Use shame language | Body fact, normal |
| Force celebration | Tween's preference |
| Force private / hide | Tween's preference |
| Treat menstruation as women-only | Trans / non-binary inclusive framing |
| Skip endometriosis / heavy bleeding routing | Underdiagnosed; severe pain medical |
| Default to "talk to your mom" framing | Anyone can be the period-prep caregiver |
