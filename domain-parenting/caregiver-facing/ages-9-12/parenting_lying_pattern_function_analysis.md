**Purpose:** Help a caregiver decode lying as functional behavior (it does something for the tween), distinguish typical from concerning, address the driver rather than just the lie, and respond in a way that increases — rather than decreases — the probability of future honesty.

**When to use:** Recurring lies, big lies, lies discovered after the fact, lies about specific topics (homework, friends, devices, money), lies that surprise caregiver, post-incident response, when caregiver feels betrayed.

**When NOT to use:** Lies driven by trauma response (specialized clinical pathway). Lies covering for harmful behavior to others (run alongside that issue). Pathological lying with other red flags → mental health.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Pattern combined with cruelty to animals or younger children, fire-setting, severe rule-breaking → conduct evaluation.
- Lies covering self-harm, eating disorder, suicidal ideation → mental health, urgent.
- Lies covering substance use → mental health + safety planning.
- Lies driven by abuse / fear of abuse from another adult → child protection consultation.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Lying is functional behavior, like everything else.** "Why is this lie useful right now?" is the question.
2. **All children lie sometimes.** Even adults do. The question is the pattern, not the existence.
3. **Tweens lie partly to construct an autonomous self.** Privacy is a developmental need; not all withholding is "lying."
4. **Punishment for lying often increases lying.** A tween who fears the response will hide more skillfully next time.
5. **The conditions that produce honesty are predictable.** Calm parents, repair-not-punishment culture, low-stakes practice. Build those.
6. **Some lies cover real harm.** Don't dismiss every lie as developmentally typical without checking.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Function Analysis

Common functions:

- **Avoidance of consequence.** "If I tell, I'll get in trouble."
- **Image management.** "I don't want them to think I'm [stupid/weird/bad]."
- **Autonomy / privacy.** "This is mine, you don't get to know."
- **Peer pressure / loyalty.** "I can't tell you because I promised."
- **Avoidance of caregiver disappointment.** Strong driver in tweens with high attachment + perfectionism.
- **Fear of harm.** From another adult, an older sibling, a peer.
- **Covering harm to others.** Bullying, theft, harm to younger sibling.
- **Covering self-harm.** Eating, cutting, substance use, suicidal thoughts.
- **Mental health symptom.** Depression-driven dissimulation, OCD-driven false confessions.
- **Identity construction.** Telling stories about who you are; "I'm popular," "I have a girlfriend," "I'm cool."
- **Habit.** Lying has worked; system is reinforcing.

State which is most likely. Often multiple.

### Stage 2 — Distinguish Typical from Concerning

Typical:
- Specific topics (e.g., not having done homework, lied about screen time).
- Tween confesses or shows guilt when caught.
- Doesn't cause harm to others.
- Resolves with conversation + adjusted approach.

Concerning:
- Pervasive, multiple topics, no clear functional driver.
- Lies with no apparent guilt.
- Lies covering harm to self or others.
- Sustained pattern unresponsive to consistent response.
- Combined with other red flags.

### Stage 3 — Caregiver Flooding First

Before responding to a lie, regulate yourself:
- "I'm hurt. I need a minute."
- Don't interrogate when flooded.
- Don't escalate the consequence based on hurt.
- Plan the response in the cold, not the hot.

### Stage 4 — The Confrontation Conversation

Open:
- "I know about [specific thing]. I want to understand what happened, not catch you in the lie."
- "I'd rather hear the truth from you than figure it out without you."
- Listen.

Avoid:
- Demanding confession ("Just tell me the truth").
- Walking through a constructed evidence trail (the tween will adapt to the questioning).
- Adding new consequences mid-conversation.

When the tween tells the truth:
- "Thank you for telling me. That took courage."
- Then address the underlying issue.

When the tween doubles down:
- "Okay. I'm going to tell you what I know, and what's going to happen. We can talk again later if you want to add anything."

### Stage 5 — Address the Driver, Not Just the Lie

For each function:

- **Avoidance of consequence:** Reduce the size of the consequence. If telling the truth always = bigger punishment than getting away with it, lying is rational.
- **Image management:** Affirm the tween's worth independent of the issue. "I love you no matter what; we still need to address [thing]."
- **Autonomy / privacy:** Distinguish — what's privacy (their private journal) vs. what's caregiver-need-to-know (where they actually were Friday night). Honor privacy where appropriate.
- **Peer loyalty:** Don't ask the tween to inform on friends without strong reason. Provide a way out: "If a friend is in danger, that's different. I want to know."
- **Caregiver disappointment:** Audit your reactions to past truth-telling. If your face has fallen at every report, the tween will calibrate.
- **Fear of harm:** Investigate immediately. Safety pathway.
- **Covering harm to others:** Bullying / safety pathway.
- **Covering self-harm:** Mental health pathway.
- **Mental health driver:** Treat the underlying.
- **Identity construction:** Reflect, don't shame. "It sounds like you wanted [Y] to be true. That makes sense. Here's what's actually true."
- **Habit:** Build new conditions; reduce reinforcement of lying.

### Stage 6 — Build Conditions for Honesty

- Repair > punishment. Tween who tells the truth gets a calmer response than one who lies.
- Calm parents. The tween reads your face for the cost of telling.
- Predictable consequences (not dramatic ones).
- Honesty modeling. Your own truth-telling matters.
- Privacy boundaries respected.
- Praise honesty when it happens, even small things.

### Stage 7 — Consequences That Address the Cause

Useful consequences for lying:
- Lost autonomy that's logically connected ("I can't trust you alone with the device for [X] period; we'll rebuild").
- Repair work where harm was done to others.
- Closer caregiver involvement on the topic of the lie.

Unhelpful:
- Long groundings unrelated to the issue.
- Public shaming.
- Withdrawing love or attention.
- Yelling.

### Stage 8 — Trust Rebuilding

After a meaningful lie:
- "Trust is something we're going to rebuild. Here's how."
- Specific small things tween will do; specific small things caregiver will do.
- Time horizon (4–8 weeks).
- Repair, not surveillance.

### Stage 9 — Caregiver Self-Audit

Hard questions:
- Do you lie in front of the tween? (To get out of plans, to a coworker, in traffic, about money?)
- Does telling you the truth result in harsh consequences?
- Do you punish the messenger when they bring you truth?
- Do you respect your tween's emerging privacy?
- Do you make it possible to admit a mistake?

Family-level honesty conditions matter.

## Adaptations

- **Anxious tween:** Lying often driven by image management and avoidance of disappointment; calibrate response gentleness.
- **Perfectionist tween:** Lies about homework, performance, friendships; address perfectionism directly.
- **ADHD tween:** Impulsive lies (saying yes when no, etc.); address impulsivity + repair without harsh consequences.
- **Autistic tween:** Lies often less common as a strategy (executive theory of mind); when present, often function-driven rather than calculated.
- **Depressed tween:** Lies covering withdrawal, mood, food, school; mental health pathway.
- **Trauma history:** Lying as protection from past harm; trauma-informed.
- **Co-parents differing:** Align on response; tween will play differences.
- **Stepfamily:** Build clear honesty conditions; avoid bio-parent vs. step-parent dynamics.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Frame tween as "a liar" (identity) | Address the behavior; preserve the person |
| Default to interrogation | Listening + naming what you know |
| Default to harsh consequences | Calibrated; logically connected |
| Skip the function | Function reveals intervention |
| Confuse privacy with lying | Distinguish |
| Ask tween to inform on friends without strong reason | Provide narrow safety path |
| Punish the truth more than the lie | Rebuilds the lying loop |
| Skip caregiver self-audit | Modeling matters |
| Treat all lies as the same | Differentiate typical / concerning / clinical |
| Forget mental-health-driven lies | Mental health pathway |
| Pretend trust auto-rebuilds | Plan it |
| Public shame | Goes underground |
