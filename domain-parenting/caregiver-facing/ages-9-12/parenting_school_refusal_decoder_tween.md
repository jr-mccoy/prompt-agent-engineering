**Purpose:** Decode why a tween is refusing or avoiding school, distinguishing the four functional categories of school refusal behavior, ruling in/out specific drivers (anxiety, bullying, learning disability, depression, family-related), and building a structured graduated return-to-attendance plan.

**When to use:** Sustained morning resistance, somatic complaints (stomachache, headache) that resolve when allowed to stay home, missed days adding up, attendance officer involvement, post-illness reluctance to return, post-event refusal.

**When NOT to use:** Truancy (different motivation pattern, often externalizing), chronic medical condition genuinely keeping child home, acute family crisis. Severe trauma context warrants trauma-informed clinical pathway.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Suicidal ideation, self-harm, severe depression → mental health, today.
- Bullying or sexual harassment driving avoidance → bullying / Title IX pathway in parallel.
- Severe anxiety with somatic features beyond mild distress → mental health.
- Trauma response → trauma-informed therapy.
- Sustained refusal >2 weeks without re-engagement plan → clinical and school escalation.

---

## Core Principles

1. **School refusal is functional behavior.** It does something for the tween. Identifying the function is the intervention target.
2. **Avoidance reinforces itself.** Each day at home reduces tomorrow's likelihood of attending. Time matters.
3. **The tween's distress is real.** "Just make them go" misses that severe avoidance often has clinical drivers.
4. **Pulling out of school often makes it worse.** Homeschooling decisions made in panic often regress further.
5. **The longer the absence, the harder the re-entry.** Graduated return earlier > forced return after weeks.
6. **School + family + clinical are a triangle.** No leg of the triangle alone solves it.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Functional Analysis

Four functional categories (Kearney's framework):

1. **Avoidance of school-based distress.** Anxiety, sensory overload, fear of teacher, fear of failure, social anxiety. Tween often unable to articulate; somatic.
2. **Escape from social or evaluative situations.** Specific class, presentation, lunch period, bullying, social awkwardness. More articulable.
3. **Attention-seeking from caregivers.** Often younger or more anxious tweens. Home contact = the reward.
4. **Tangible reward.** Home is more interesting (gaming, screens, freedom). More common in older tweens / pre-teens with light parental presence at home.

State which is most likely. Often more than one is in play.

### Stage 2 — Differentiate from Truancy

| Marker | School refusal | Truancy |
|---|---|---|
| Caregiver knows | Yes | Often no |
| Distress | High | Low |
| Tween at home | Yes | Often elsewhere |
| Underlying issue | Anxiety, depression, bullying, LD, social | Behavioral, family, peer-influenced |
| Response | Therapeutic + structured return | Behavioral + family + sometimes legal |

Both warrant intervention; the intervention shape differs.

### Stage 3 — Driver Identification

Specific drivers to rule in/out:

- **Bullying or victimization** at school → safety pathway.
- **Specific subject failure** (math anxiety, reading struggle hidden at home) → academic assessment.
- **Friendship loss / rejection** → friendship-coaching + return.
- **Sensory overload** for autistic / sensory-sensitive tween → accommodations.
- **Anxiety disorder** (separation anxiety, generalized, social, panic) → CBT / SSRI consideration.
- **Depression** → treatment in parallel; school as part of activation.
- **Trauma response** → trauma-informed therapy.
- **Family stress** (divorce, illness, loss, instability) → family-level support.
- **Learning disability or unrecognized ND** → evaluation.

### Stage 4 — Structured Graduated Return

Day 1–3 of plan:
- Tween goes to school for 1 hour.
- Specific arrival point, specific adult to meet.
- Pre-arranged exit if needed.
- Caregiver picks up after the hour.

Day 4–7:
- Half-day attendance.
- Progressive lengthening.
- Anchor activity (lunch with one supportive friend, a teacher's classroom, the library).

Week 2:
- Full day with check-ins.
- Specific scaffolds (early arrival before crowd, pass to leave the room, designated trusted adult).

Week 3+:
- Standard attendance.
- Continued check-ins reducing in frequency.

Adapt pacing per tween. Some need a slower ramp; some need to rip the bandaid.

### Stage 5 — Morning Plan

The morning is the hardest:

- Predictable wake routine.
- Limit decisions (clothes laid out night before, breakfast simple).
- No screens in the morning (delays / amps anxiety).
- Calm, brief check-in: "I know mornings are hard. We're going to do today's plan."
- Don't engage the avoidance in the morning. Address feelings calmly; do the plan.
- Get to the agreed entry point. Hand off.

### Stage 6 — Caregiver Posture

- Validate the distress without negotiating the attendance.
- "I know it's hard. We're going to do today."
- Avoid: "Okay, just one more day." Each one-more-day extends.
- Address anxiety in evening (when nervous system is regulated), not morning.
- Don't overpromise rescue; don't undersell support.

### Stage 7 — School Coordination

Specific asks:
- Trusted adult contact at school.
- Quiet space available.
- Pass to leave the room.
- Modified schedule (start later, leave earlier) for the ramp.
- Attendance counted as present for the hours attended.
- Communication daily during ramp.
- Reentry support after long absence (peer ambassador, teacher heads-up).
- Counselor or social worker involvement.

For accommodations: 504 plan or IEP modification.

### Stage 8 — Clinical Coordination

- Therapist (CBT for anxiety, exposure-based for school refusal specifically; ACT and DBT-A for emotion-regulation).
- Psychiatry consultation if SSRI is on the table.
- School-aware therapist who'll coordinate.
- Family therapy for family-driven cases.

### Stage 9 — When to Pause Pushing

Pause attendance push if:
- Suicidal ideation, self-harm.
- Severe panic with non-recovery.
- Active bullying not yet addressed.
- Acute family crisis.

Resume the plan after the pause, with adjusted ramp.

### Stage 10 — Don't Default to Pulling

Most cases of school refusal do worse out of school. Homeschool, hybrid school, or school change should be:
- Considered after exhaustive supports.
- Not made in panic.
- Made with clinical input.
- Not a substitute for treating the underlying driver.

If pulling: have a structured replacement.

## Adaptations

- **Anxious tween:** Exposure-based intervention; CBT; structured ramp essential.
- **Autistic tween:** Sensory accommodations + social scaffolding; visual schedule.
- **ADHD tween:** Morning routine support; transition warnings.
- **Trauma history:** Trauma-informed school approach; consult specialist.
- **Bullied tween:** Bullying response in parallel.
- **Learning disability:** Evaluation + accommodations.
- **Depressed tween:** Treatment + activation; school often part of recovery.
- **Family stress driver:** Address family situation directly.
- **Custody / co-parents differing:** Align on minimum plan; same in both houses.
- **Cultural context:** Honor family practices; integrate.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to "make them go" | Functional analysis + structured ramp |
| Default to "let them stay home" | Avoidance reinforces |
| Default to homeschool in panic | Last resort; consider after exhaustive supports |
| Negotiate attendance in the morning | Address feelings in evening; do the plan in the morning |
| Confuse refusal with truancy | Different drivers, different responses |
| Skip clinical pathway | Anxiety / depression often present |
| Skip school coordination | School is partner |
| Pull from school without re-entry plan | Each day out increases re-entry difficulty |
| Treat distress as fake | Distress is real; intervention is structured |
| Forget bullying as driver | Always rule in/out |
| Forget unrecognized LD | Evaluate when academic struggle present |
| Push attendance through suicidal ideation | Pause and treat |
