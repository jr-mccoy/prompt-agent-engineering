**Purpose:** Build a defensible first-phone decision: assess whether the tween needs one, choose the right type for the actual need, design a contract, set up technical safeguards, and ramp toward more capability over time.

**When to use:** Caregiver weighing phone for a 9–12-year-old, peer pressure ("everyone has one"), school transition, custody schedule needing direct contact, mobility expansion, or post-incident decision.

**When NOT to use:** Existing acute safety/trauma context; child is already in a problematic tech relationship — that's a stepdown plan, not a first-phone plan.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Existing patterns of aggressive online behavior, exposure to harmful content, sustained sleep disruption around screens, signs of online predation or grooming → handle separately, not as a "first phone" decision.
- Mental health concerns (depression, anxiety, eating disorder, body image) suggest delaying smartphone-with-social-media; literature on adolescent mental health and smartphone/social-media exposure is increasingly negative.

---

## Core Principles

1. **The decision is not "phone yes / phone no." It's a layered choice: need, type, capability, and contract.**
2. **A phone for safety/contact is not the same as a smartphone with internet.** Separate need from desire.
3. **Smartphones with social media correlate with mental health problems for many adolescents** (Haidt 2024 and similar literature). Delay is increasingly recommended where feasible.
4. **Peer normality is a real cost.** "Everyone has one" is not a reason in itself; it is a context to consider.
5. **Contracts work when they describe behavior, not personality.** "Phone is in the kitchen by 9pm" works; "Be responsible" does not.
6. **The capability ramp matters.** Full-feature smartphone day-1 is harder to govern than starting with calls/texts only.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Separate "need" from "desire" explicitly.
- Offer the four-tier device map.
- Provide contract language, not vague principles.
- Address sleep, social-media, and content separately.
- Plan capability expansion over time.
- Address mental health context.

**Must Not:**
- Default to "all tweens get a phone at 12."
- Skip the contract.
- Assume social media must come with the phone.
- Promise no harm.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Need vs. Desire Audit

Ask:
- What is this phone *for*?
- Does the stated need require a smartphone, or could a simpler device meet it?

| Need | Minimal device that meets it |
|---|---|
| Caregiver pickup contact | Dumb phone, kid phone (Gabb, Bark, Pinwheel-style), or shared landline |
| Mobility safety | Dumb phone or kid phone with GPS |
| Group text with friends | Locked smartphone with messaging only |
| Specific app for school | Locked smartphone with single-app access |
| Social media presence | Smartphone with social — but reconsider given mental health literature |
| Gaming on the go | Different decision; consider handheld instead |

If "the need" is "fitting in," that's a different conversation. Honor it but don't pretend it's the same as caregiver contact.

### Stage 2 — Device Tier Selection

**Tier 1 — Dumb phone:** flip phone or basic phone. Calls, texts, no internet, no apps. Cost: low. Best for: contact-only need.

**Tier 2 — Kid phone (Gabb, Pinwheel, Bark, Light Phone, similar):** designed for tweens. Calls/texts to approved contacts, GPS, no app store, often no browser. Cost: moderate. Best for: safety + texting need without internet.

**Tier 3 — Locked smartphone:** standard iPhone or Android with Screen Time / Family Link / Bark / similar parental controls fully configured. App approvals required. Browser locked or filtered. Cost: standard. Best for: tween who needs specific apps but not full internet.

**Tier 4 — Full smartphone:** standard device with light or no controls. Cost: standard. Best for: older tweens or teens with established tech track record.

Recommendation: start one tier lower than impulse. Easier to expand than retract.

### Stage 3 — Contract Components

A workable contract covers:

**Logistics**
- Where the phone lives at night (the kitchen, not the bedroom).
- When it's used (after school, after homework, agreed windows).
- Where it's allowed (not at the dinner table, not in the bathroom, not in school where banned).
- Who pays for it (caregiver / shared / tween earns).

**Communication**
- Caregiver can see the phone any time, no warning.
- Tween knows this is the deal — not a betrayal of trust, but the deal.
- Specific apps allowed and not; new app installs require ask.
- Who tween can text and call.

**Content**
- Strangers don't get to message the tween directly.
- If something weird happens (DM from stranger, scary content, friend posting concerning thing): tell caregiver, no shame, no consequence for telling.
- If tween posts/sends something they regret: caregiver helps.

**Behavior**
- No phone use while another person is talking to them.
- Phone is not a weapon (group chats to exclude, mean comments).
- If used to harm, phone is paused and we talk.

**Tightening**
- If [X] happens, we step back to [Y] for [time].

**Lost / broken**
- Who pays for replacement.
- Replacement timeline.

### Stage 4 — Technical Safeguards

For Tier 3+:

- **iOS:** Screen Time fully configured. Communication Safety on. App Store approvals. Bedtime mode. Web restrictions. Apple parental controls.
- **Android:** Family Link or Google Family. App approvals. Bedtime. Screen time limits.
- **Third-party (Bark, Aura, Qustodio):** monitor messages and content for harm flags without surveilling routine conversation.
- **Wifi-level filtering** (Pi-hole, OpenDNS, router-level) for whole-house defense.

Document the configuration. Recheck quarterly — devices push updates that reset settings.

### Stage 5 — Sleep Protection

The single highest-leverage rule: **phone leaves bedroom at night.**

- Charging station in kitchen or living room.
- Reason given: "Sleep matters more than the phone, even mine."
- Caregivers who don't keep their own phone out of the bedroom will struggle to enforce this; consider modeling.

### Stage 6 — Social Media Separately

Even if the phone is granted, social media is a separate decision:
- Most platforms officially require 13+; many tweens get on younger.
- Mental health literature increasingly negative for adolescent users, especially female-typical.
- Consider delaying social media even after granting phone.
- If granted: see `parenting_social_media_first_account_protocol.md`.

### Stage 7 — Capability Ramp

Plan future expansions:
- 6 months: review.
- 12 months: consider one app addition.
- Each tier transition is earned through observed behavior, not asked for.

### Stage 8 — Caregiver Self-Reflection

- What's the caregiver's own phone relationship?
- Are caregiver rules also caregiver-followed?
- What modeling is happening?

## Adaptations

- **Anxious tween:** Phone access can amplify anxiety (notifications, comparison); tighter limits + mental health support.
- **ADHD tween:** Notifications, app loops, dopamine challenges executive function; tighter limits, specific apps.
- **Autistic tween:** Phone can be a regulator (special interest access); also can be an isolation tool. Calibrate both.
- **Custody schedule:** Phone helps custody contact but creates conflict if rules differ between houses; align with co-parent.
- **Mental health concerns:** Delay social media; consider delay of full smartphone.
- **Body image / eating concerns:** Avoid algorithm-driven feeds (TikTok, Instagram, Snapchat) until stabilized.
- **Older sibling effect:** Younger sibling expects same access at younger age; address explicitly.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Default to "everyone gets a phone at 12" | Need-driven; tier-matched |
| Bundle social media with phone | Separate decisions |
| Recommend full smartphone for first device | Start one tier lower |
| Make contract abstract | Behavioral, observable |
| Skip sleep rule | Phone out of bedroom is highest-leverage |
| Allow phone in bedroom because tween "needs alarm" | Use a $5 alarm clock |
| Promise no harm | Plan for issues; build repair |
| Treat peer pressure as the only consideration | Acknowledge cost of being out of step + cost of premature use |
| Surveil routinely without disclosure | Disclose oversight; that is the deal |
| Tighten in anger after incident | Tighten by pre-agreed criteria |
| Forget caregiver modeling | Phone in caregiver bedroom = harder to enforce |
| Skip mental health context | Mental health concerns argue for delay |
