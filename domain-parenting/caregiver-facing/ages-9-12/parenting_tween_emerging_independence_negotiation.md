**Purpose:** Give caregivers a framework for handing off autonomy in calibrated steps to a 9–12-year-old — across mobility (walking to friend's house, biking to school), time use (alarm, schedule, sleep), money (allowance, decisions), social access (playdates, drop-offs, online), and decisions (clothes, food, hair) — with checkpoints, repair scripts, and tightening protocols when steps fail.

**When to use:** Tween asking for more autonomy, caregiver unsure when/how to grant, recent step-up that worked or didn't, comparison to peers, transition (new school year, post-COVID re-entry), or a tween showing signs of readiness in some domains but not others.

**When NOT to use:** Acute safety concern, major rule violation requiring discipline conversation first, trauma context affecting trust.

---

## Clinical-Signal Triggers

- Sustained risk-taking that escalates → consult.
- Sustained avoidance of any independent task → anxiety screen.
- Trauma history affecting safe-out-of-house dynamics.
- Caregiver overcontrol with significant relational damage.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Autonomy is multi-domain.** A tween can be ready for biking to school but not for managing $50/month, or vice versa. Domain-specific calibration.
2. **Trust grows through small successful experiences.** A tween who has never managed an alarm cannot start with a 7:00 a.m. independent wake; build skills.
3. **Failure is data, not betrayal.** Plan for failure; build in repair.
4. **Tightening is allowed.** "We tried; it didn't work; we step back" is healthy parenting, not punishment, when framed neutrally.
5. **Caregiver anxiety is a signal too.** A caregiver who isn't ready may need support to grant a step the tween is ready for.
6. **Cultural and family variation is huge.** Some communities support 9-year-olds walking siblings to school; others not 12-year-olds. Plans honor family context.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Run a multi-domain readiness check.
- Provide a calibrated step (not full grant or full denial).
- Build in checkpoints.
- Provide repair scripts for when steps fail.
- Provide tightening criteria.
- Honor cultural / family context.

**Must Not:**
- Default to age-only readiness.
- Frame tightening as punishment.
- Demand the tween earn autonomy through perfect compliance.
- Promise the step won't go wrong.

## Instructions

### Stage 1 — Multi-Domain Readiness Check

For each relevant domain:

| Domain | Signs of readiness | Skills to scaffold |
|---|---|---|
| Mobility | Knows route, knows safety rules, can navigate strangers, has phone or check-in plan | Walk together, then walk parallel, then independent with check-in |
| Time | Wakes to alarm, can manage homework start, follows agreed schedule | Use alarm with backup, build routine, build self-correction |
| Money | Counts, makes change, can resist impulse for short period, can save for goal | Allowance with categories, modeled tradeoff conversations |
| Social | Friend selection responsibility, can leave a situation, knows when to call | Practice scenarios, agree on calls and language |
| Decisions | Can hold tradeoff in mind, accepts consequence, can repair after a bad call | Small low-stakes decisions, debrief afterward |
| Household | Follows multi-step task, holds responsibility without daily reminder | Specific role with backup, no surveillance |

### Stage 2 — Calibrate the Step

Three-tier framework:

- **Conservative step:** small expansion, high scaffolding, frequent check-ins.
- **Standard step:** expanded autonomy with agreed-upon boundaries and one major check-in.
- **Stretch step:** notable expansion with explicit handoff conditions.

Pick the level matched to readiness signs + caregiver capacity.

Example for "walk to school" (12-year-old, 0.5-mile route, never done it):
- **Conservative:** Walk together for 1 week. Then caregiver follows 100 yards behind for 1 week. Then independent with morning check-in.
- **Standard:** Skip the parallel-walk. Independent with check-in text on arrival.
- **Stretch:** Independent with no daily check-in but agreed-upon protocols if late.

### Stage 3 — Checkpoints

Build in:
- A specific date/time to evaluate.
- Observable markers ("you arrive on time, you call when [X]").
- One scheduled debrief conversation, not surveillance during the step.

### Stage 4 — Repair Scripts (When Steps Fail)

When the step doesn't go well:

- **Caregiver opening:** "It looks like that didn't go the way we hoped. Let's figure out what happened."
- **Listen:** What went wrong from the tween's view? What was the moment that turned?
- **Co-design:** What would help next time? More scaffolding? Different timing?
- **Decide together:** Hold the step with adjustments? Tighten temporarily? Pause and try later?
- **Frame:** "This isn't a failure. This is how trust grows — by trying things and adjusting."

### Stage 5 — Tightening Without Punishment

If the step fails repeatedly or a major safety incident happens:
- Step back to the prior level.
- Frame: "We're going to take a step back so we can build the skill."
- Set a clear timeline ("In 4 weeks, we'll try again with [X]").
- Avoid: "I told you so," shame, comparing to siblings, punishing related domains.

### Stage 6 — Caregiver Self-Reflection

Tween autonomy collides with caregiver anxiety. Surface:
- What is the caregiver afraid of? (Safety, comparison, loss of role, child's own wellbeing)
- Is the fear realistic for this tween, this domain, this neighborhood?
- Where is the caregiver's anxiety vs. the actual safety analysis?
- Co-parent alignment: are caregivers working from the same readiness assessment?

A caregiver who grants in word but surveils in body conveys "I don't trust you," which produces the behaviors the caregiver fears.

### Stage 7 — Cultural & Family Variation

- "Free range" cultures (much of the world, much of the US pre-1980): 9-year-olds walking errands, watching siblings.
- "Concerted cultivation" / supervised cultures (much of US 2000+): more structured, less independent mobility.
- Religious and immigrant communities have specific readiness markers (shul/temple/mosque attendance alone, market errands, sibling care).
- Honor family context. There's no universal correct calibration.

## Adaptations

- **Anxious tween:** Approach + scaffolded exposure; smaller steps with more practice; avoid step-too-big-then-failure pattern.
- **ADHD tween:** Routine + external scaffolds (alarm, list, calendar); the autonomy is real even with structures.
- **Autistic tween:** Social-mobility separately from physical-mobility; the route may be fine; the social negotiations may need scaffolding.
- **Sibling comparison:** Avoid "your sister did this at 10." Each tween calibrated.
- **Co-parents differing:** Align on the smallest acceptable step both can agree to.
- **Custody schedule:** Same step in both houses where possible.
- **Trauma history:** Slower, more scaffolded; relational repair central.
- **Single caregiver:** Plan for backup adult presence in case tween's step fails badly.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| Treat autonomy as one switch | Multi-domain calibration |
| Default to age | Use readiness signs |
| Frame tightening as punishment | Frame as scaffolding adjustment |
| Demand perfect compliance for autonomy | Build trust through experience, including some failure |
| Ignore caregiver anxiety | Surface and separate from safety analysis |
| Compare siblings | Each tween calibrated |
| Surveil after granting | Surveillance signals "I don't trust"; produces what's feared |
| Promise smooth | Failures expected; repair built in |
| Skip cultural context | Honor family variation |
| Confuse compliance with readiness | Some compliant tweens aren't ready; some independent tweens are |
| Treat anxious tween's hesitation as defiance | Scaffold approach |
| Yank back without conversation after one mistake | Repair, adjust, try again |
