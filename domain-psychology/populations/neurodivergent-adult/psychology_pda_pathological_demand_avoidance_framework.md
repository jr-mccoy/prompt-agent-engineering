# PDA (Pathological/Persistent Demand Avoidance) Framework

## Objective

Provide a structured, PDA-aware framework that:

1. Characterizes the **demand-avoidance profile** — pervasive, anxiety-driven avoidance of everyday demands and expectations (including self-imposed and desirable demands), often using **social strategies** (negotiation, distraction, excuses, role-play, withdrawal, or escalation) to evade demands.
2. Locates PDA conceptually: described in much of the literature as a **profile within the autism spectrum** rather than a standalone DSM-5-TR diagnosis; the framework names this status honestly and notes the terminology debate ("pathological" vs. "persistent" demand avoidance) and the client's language preference.
3. **Differentiates PDA from ODD, ordinary oppositionality, generalized/social anxiety, and trauma-driven control responses**, because the intervention logic diverges sharply.
4. Reframes the clinical model from **compliance/behavioral control toward autonomy-preserving, low-demand, collaborative accommodation** — the demand itself, and the perceived loss of autonomy, is the threat the nervous system is responding to.
5. Screens for **elevated risk** (suicide risk, burnout, school/work refusal collapse) and embeds risk-reassessment hooks.

The framework supports formulation and approach selection; it does not assert a DSM diagnosis where none exists.

## When to Use

- For an autistic (or assessment-pending) adult or older adolescent whose central difficulty is **extreme, pervasive demand avoidance** that conventional behavioral/compliance approaches worsen.
- When standard "consequences and rewards" or directive structures have repeatedly **escalated** distress, refusal, or shutdown.
- When a team is confused about whether the presentation is "oppositional/willful" vs. an anxiety-driven, autonomy-protective nervous-system response.
- To translate a PDA-aware understanding into a **low-demand, collaborative** treatment and accommodation plan.

## When NOT to Use

- As a substitute for autism assessment — use the autism intake module to characterize the broader neurotype; PDA is generally understood as a profile within it.
- For presentations better explained by an untreated treatable condition (e.g., acute trauma, untreated OCD, psychosis) without first running the differential below.
- For acute crisis — stabilize first; demand-avoidance collapse can co-occur with acute suicidality.

## Inputs / Context Required

- **Developmental and autism context:** existing autism identification or assessment status (route to the autism module if unestablished).
- **Demand-avoidance history:** which demands trigger avoidance (everyday, self-care, desirable/preferred, and self-imposed demands), the strategies used, and the autonomy/anxiety pattern.
- **Escalation history:** what happens when demands are pressed; what de-escalates.
- **Prior approaches tried:** behavioral/compliance programs, rewards/consequences, and their effect (worsening is diagnostically informative).
- **Co-occurring conditions:** anxiety, trauma, OCD, depression, ADHD.
- **Language preference:** `[clinician input required: ask whether the client/family prefers "pathological" vs. "persistent" demand avoidance, "PDA," "demand-avoidant profile," or another term; use it consistently and acknowledge the terminology debate]`
- `[clinician input required: current risk profile — suicidality, self-harm, school/work refusal collapse, burnout]`

## Constraints

### Must

- Frame demand avoidance as **anxiety-driven and autonomy-protective**, not willful misbehavior; the nervous system is responding to perceived loss of control, including over self-imposed and desirable demands.
- State PDA's **conceptual status honestly**: widely described as a profile within autism, not a discrete DSM-5-TR category; note the terminology debate and use the client's preferred term.
- Run the **PDA-vs-ODD-vs-anxiety-vs-trauma differential** explicitly (table below); document why PDA best (or does not best) fit.
- Make the **counterintuitive intervention shift explicit**: standard ODD/behavioral compliance approaches (escalating demands, reward/consequence contingencies, firm directives) commonly **worsen** PDA presentations.
- Translate findings into **low-demand, collaborative, autonomy-preserving** strategies (indirect/declarative language, choice and negotiation, reduced demand load, depersonalized demands, novelty/role-play, flexible pacing).
- Screen for **elevated risk** and embed a risk-reassessment hook at formulation and at each plan review.
- Honor language preference; involve the client collaboratively in formulation (PDA work is done *with*, not *on*, the person).
- Flag all `[clinician input required: ...]` gaps; do not fabricate history.

### Must Not

- Do not recommend escalating demands, rigid behavior contracts, or consequence-driven compliance programs as the primary approach for a PDA profile — they typically backfire.
- Do not label the presentation as ODD or "manipulative" without running the differential; social strategies in PDA are anxiety-driven, not antisocial.
- Do not present PDA as an established DSM-5-TR diagnosis.
- Do not treat the demand-avoidant neurotype profile itself as the target to be eliminated; the targets are anxiety, autonomy-supportive environmental fit, and co-occurring conditions.
- Do not omit the risk screen, especially given burnout and refusal-collapse risk.

## Differential Table: PDA vs. ODD vs. Anxiety vs. Trauma-Driven Control

| Feature | PDA profile | ODD | Generalized/Social anxiety | Trauma-driven control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core driver | Anxiety/loss of autonomy over demands themselves | Pattern of angry/defiant, vindictive behavior toward authority | Fear of specific outcomes/evaluation | Threat/safety response, hypervigilance |
| Demands avoided | Everyday, self-care, AND desirable/self-imposed demands | Demands from authority figures; reward-seeking defiance | Specific feared situations | Demands that evoke loss of control/threat cues |
| Strategies | Social: negotiation, distraction, excuse, fantasy/role-play, withdrawal, then escalation | Argument, deliberate annoyance, blame, anger | Avoidance, reassurance-seeking | Freeze/fight/control, dissociation |
| Response to rewards/consequences | Often worsens (experienced as demand/control) | Often responds to contingencies | Neutral-to-helpful with exposure | Can re-trigger threat |
| Autism context | Typically within autism spectrum | Not inherently | Comorbid possible | Comorbid possible |
| Effective approach | Low-demand, collaborative, autonomy-preserving | Structured behavioral (PMT) | Graded exposure / CBT | Trauma-informed, safety-first |

## Instructions

1. **Establish autism context and language.** Confirm autism assessment status (route to the autism module if needed). Record the client/family's preferred terminology and acknowledge the debate. Engage the client collaboratively from the outset.

2. **Triage risk.** Screen for suicidality, self-harm, and refusal/burnout collapse (Columbia C-SSRS where indicated) before formulation.

3. **Map the demand-avoidance profile.** Catalog which demands trigger avoidance — including **desirable and self-imposed** demands (a hallmark) — the social strategies used, and the autonomy/anxiety signature.

4. **Take the escalation and prior-approach history.** Document what presses vs. de-escalates, and the effect of any prior behavioral/compliance programs (worsening supports the PDA formulation).

5. **Run the differential.** Use the table to weigh PDA vs. ODD vs. anxiety vs. trauma-driven control; document the reasoning and any co-occurring conditions.

6. **Make the intervention shift explicit.** State why compliance-based approaches are contraindicated and specify low-demand, collaborative, autonomy-preserving strategies tailored to this person.

7. **Build the formulation and plan**, embedding a risk-reassessment hook and naming non-neurotype targets (anxiety, environmental fit, comorbidity).

8. Run verification.

- [ ] Differential run across PDA, ODD, anxiety, and trauma-driven control with documented reasoning.
