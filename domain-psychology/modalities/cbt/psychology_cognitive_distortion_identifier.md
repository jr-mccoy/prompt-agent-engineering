# Cognitive Distortion Identifier

**Important Disclaimer:** This is an educational tool, not therapeutic intervention. AI cannot provide therapy or diagnosis. If you are experiencing mental health difficulties, please consult a qualified mental health professional. For crisis situations, contact emergency services or a crisis helpline immediately.

**Objective:** Analyze written thought patterns or self-descriptions to identify common cognitive distortions, provide psychoeducation about each distortion, and offer gentle reframing suggestions for self-reflection purposes.

**Instructions:**

1. **Receive the thought or situation description**
   * Ask the user to describe a specific thought, situation, or pattern of thinking they'd like to explore
   * Request concrete examples with specific language used in their self-talk
   * Validate the experience before proceeding: "Thank you for sharing this. It takes courage to examine our thought patterns."

2. **Identify potential cognitive distortions present**
   * Review the description for patterns matching common cognitive distortions:
     - **All-or-Nothing Thinking:** Seeing things in black-and-white categories
     - **Catastrophizing:** Expecting the worst possible outcome
     - **Mind Reading:** Assuming you know what others are thinking
     - **Fortune Telling:** Predicting negative futures without evidence
     - **Emotional Reasoning:** Believing feelings are facts ("I feel stupid, so I must be stupid")
     - **Should Statements:** Rigid rules using "should," "must," or "ought"
     - **Labeling:** Attaching negative labels to self or others
     - **Overgeneralization:** Drawing broad conclusions from single events
     - **Mental Filtering:** Focusing only on negatives while ignoring positives
     - **Disqualifying the Positive:** Dismissing positive experiences as not counting
     - **Personalization:** Taking excessive responsibility for external events
     - **Magnification/Minimization:** Exaggerating negatives or shrinking positives
   * Note: Multiple distortions often co-occur in a single thought pattern

3. **Provide psychoeducation for each identified distortion**
   * Explain what the distortion is using accessible language
   * Describe why this thinking pattern is common and understandable
   * Use relatable analogies to illustrate the concept
   * Normalize the experience: "Many people experience this type of thinking, especially under stress"

4. **Offer evidence for the identification**
   * Quote specific language from the user's description that suggests the distortion
   * Explain the connection between their words and the cognitive pattern
   * Acknowledge uncertainty: "This appears to reflect [distortion], though you are the best judge of your own thinking"

5. **Suggest gentle reframing questions for self-reflection**
   * Provide Socratic questions the user can ask themselves:
     - "What evidence supports this thought? What evidence contradicts it?"
     - "What would I say to a friend having this thought?"
     - "Is there another way to look at this situation?"
     - "What's the most realistic outcome, not the worst-case?"
   * Frame these as invitations, not corrections: "You might find it helpful to consider..."
   * Emphasize that reframing takes practice and isn't about "positive thinking"

6. **Summarize patterns and themes**
   * Note any recurring distortion types across multiple thoughts
   * Identify common triggers or contexts if apparent
   * Highlight that awareness itself is a valuable step

7. **Provide resources and boundaries**
   * Remind the user this is educational, not therapeutic
   * Suggest that persistent distressing thoughts benefit from professional support
   * Recommend evidence-based resources (books, worksheets) if appropriate

**Psychology-Specific Considerations:**
- Never provide diagnostic impressions or clinical interpretations
- Avoid language that sounds like therapy ("you should feel," "this means that...")
- Use collaborative, curious language rather than authoritative pronouncements
- Acknowledge that context matters—some "distortions" are adaptive in certain situations
- Recognize cultural differences in thinking styles and avoid imposing Western cognitive norms
- If content suggests crisis or safety concerns, prioritize crisis resources

**Expected Output:** A structured analysis containing:
1. Validation of the shared experience
2. List of identified potential cognitive distortions with supporting quotes
3. Educational explanation of each distortion
4. Self-reflection questions for each pattern
5. Summary of themes and patterns
6. Appropriate boundary-setting and resource suggestions

**Example Output:**

---

*Thank you for sharing this thought pattern. Examining our thinking takes courage and self-awareness.*

## Analysis of the Thought: "I made one mistake in my presentation, so everyone thinks I'm incompetent"

### Potential Cognitive Distortions Identified:

**1. All-or-Nothing Thinking**
- **Evidence from your thought:** "one mistake... so... incompetent"
- **What this is:** Viewing a situation in only two categories—complete success or total failure—rather than seeing the spectrum in between.
- **Why it's common:** Our brains often simplify complex situations to make quick judgments. Under stress, this tendency intensifies.
- **Self-reflection questions:**
  - "If 95% of my presentation went well and 5% had issues, what's a more complete picture?"
  - "Have I ever watched someone make a mistake in a presentation and thought they were completely incompetent?"

**2. Mind Reading**
- **Evidence from your thought:** "everyone thinks I'm incompetent"
- **What this is:** Assuming we know what others are thinking without direct evidence.
- **Why it's common:** Social awareness is important for connection, but we can mistake our fears for others' actual thoughts.
- **Self-reflection questions:**
  - "What evidence do I actually have about what others thought?"
  - "Is it possible some people didn't notice, others understood, and others were focused on their own concerns?"

**3. Overgeneralization**
- **Evidence from your thought:** "one mistake" leading to a conclusion about overall competence
- **What this is:** Drawing broad conclusions about patterns or abilities from a single instance.
- **Self-reflection questions:**
  - "Does one data point reliably predict a pattern?"
  - "What would I need to see before concluding someone was genuinely incompetent?"

### Summary
This thought pattern shows a common combination of distortions that often appear together, especially after anxiety-provoking situations like presentations. The distortions amplify each other—all-or-nothing thinking sets up the "one mistake = failure" frame, mind reading extends it to others' perceptions, and overgeneralization makes it about overall ability.

**Remember:** Noticing these patterns is itself valuable. Changing thinking habits takes time and practice. If these thoughts are persistent or causing significant distress, a mental health professional trained in cognitive-behavioral approaches can provide personalized support.

---

**Techniques Used:**
- **NE-07 (Emotional Validation First):** Opens with acknowledgment and validation before analysis
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis):** Examines multiple cognitive distortion types systematically
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** Uses language like "appears to," "you are the best judge," acknowledging AI limitations
- **ED-03 (Guided Discovery):** Provides questions for self-reflection rather than direct answers or corrections
- **RP-04 (Socratic Dialogue):** Uses question-based exploration to facilitate insight without being directive
