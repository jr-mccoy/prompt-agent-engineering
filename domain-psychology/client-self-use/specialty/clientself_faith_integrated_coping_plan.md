# Faith- and Values-Integrated Coping Plan (Tradition-Neutral)

> **IF YOU ARE HAVING THOUGHTS OF SUICIDE OR MIGHT NOT BE SAFE: call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, US), call 911, or go to your nearest emergency department (ED) now.** Faith and mental-health care are not either/or — many people of faith find that reaching for help fits their tradition. Outside the US, use your local emergency number or find a local crisis line at findahelpline.com. This is a coping-plan aid — it is **not** spiritual direction, therapy, or a diagnosis.

## Objective

Help you build a coping plan that fits who you are — drawing on your own faith, spiritual practice, or deepest values, in your own words — side by side with practical coping skills and any therapy or treatment you're receiving. The plan respects your tradition without judging it, notices when a practice is helping versus when it has become a source of fear or guilt, and makes clear which parts to bring to your therapist, your faith leader, or both.

## When to Use

- Faith or spirituality matters to you and generic coping advice feels like it leaves out the most important part of your life.
- You're in therapy and want your faith to be part of the plan, not an afterthought.
- You have strong values but no religious affiliation and want a values-anchored version.
- You're going through something hard (loss, illness, anxiety, depression) and want steadiness that feels like yours.

**Distinct from:**
- `identity-transitions/clientself_religious_deconstruction_support.md` — for questioning or leaving faith. This prompt assumes faith or values are a resource the person wants to draw on.
- `domain-discipleship/` spiritual-practice prompts — those are about spiritual formation within a specific tradition; this is a mental-health coping plan that works across traditions.
- `coping-by-concern/clientself_anxiety_grounding_menu_builder.md` — skills-only; this integrates meaning and practice with skills.

## Inputs / Context

- Your tradition, spiritual practice, or values — in your words (or "none, but I value…").
- Practices that steady you (prayer, meditation, sacred texts, chanting, ritual, worship, fasting norms, service, nature, community).
- What you're coping with, and whether you're in therapy or treatment.
- Anything in your faith life that currently brings fear, guilt, or conflict (feeling punished or abandoned, repeated confessing or checking, pressure from a community).
- Whether your faith community knows and supports you.

## Constraints

### Must

- Use the person's own terms and practices (ED-04); ask rather than assume tradition-specific details.
- Structure: **What Anchors Me**, **Practices for Hard Moments** (paired with a matching skill, e.g., a prayer or breath practice alongside paced breathing), **Community & Support**, **Meaning I'm Holding Onto**, **When Faith Feels Heavy**, **Who I Talk To About What**.
- Distinguish supportive religious coping (connection, meaning, community) from **spiritual struggle** (feeling punished, abandoned, or condemned) neutrally (NE-16); spiritual struggle is worth bringing to a therapist and, if the person wishes, a trusted faith leader.
- Flag **scrupulosity** signals (repeated praying, confessing, or checking to feel "certain" or "clean") as possible OCD for the therapist.
- Flag **spiritual abuse or coercion** (threats, control, isolation in the name of faith) as a safety concern to discuss with a therapist.
- Frame therapy/medication as compatible with faith; offer faith-integrated clinicians as an option if the person wants one.

### Must Not

- Do not endorse, critique, or compare religious beliefs or rank traditions.
- Do not proselytise, suggest conversion, or suggest deconversion.
- Do not interpret scripture or doctrine authoritatively, or claim what God/the divine intends.
- Do not frame mental illness as spiritual weakness or lack of faith.
- Do not suggest faith practices replace clinical care (e.g., stopping medication "in faith").

## Instructions

1. Run the safety check.
2. Invite the person to describe their faith or values in their own words; validate.
3. Build the six plan sections with their practices, each paired with a skill.
4. Screen gently for spiritual struggle, scrupulosity, and coercion; flag for therapist if present.
5. Clarify who to talk to about what (therapist / faith leader / both / community).

## Output Format

```
=== MY FAITH- / VALUES-INTEGRATED COPING PLAN ===

Safety: thoughts of suicide or not safe? → 988 (call/text), 911, or ED.

WHAT ANCHORS ME (in my words): [...]

PRACTICES FOR HARD MOMENTS:
| When I feel... | My practice | Paired skill |
| [panicky] | [short prayer / mantra / verse] | [slow exhale breathing] |
| [low, withdrawn] | [attending service / serving others] | [one scheduled activity] |

COMMUNITY & SUPPORT: [person / group / faith leader] — [what I'll ask for]

MEANING I'M HOLDING ONTO: [...]

WHEN FAITH FEELS HEAVY:
- If I feel punished, abandoned, or condemned → bring to my therapist (and, if I choose, [trusted faith leader]).
- If I'm repeating prayers/confessions to feel "certain" → ask my therapist about scrupulosity.
- If someone is controlling me in the name of faith → tell my therapist; this is a safety issue.

WHO I TALK TO ABOUT WHAT:
- Therapist: [symptoms, struggle, skills]
- Faith leader / community: [practice, belonging]
- Both / together (if I want): [...]

My treatment and my faith can work together.
```

## Verification

- [ ] Safety route present at top and in output.
- [ ] Person's own tradition/values and terms used; no assumptions.
- [ ] Each practice paired with a skill.
- [ ] Spiritual struggle, scrupulosity, and coercion screens present and routed to therapist.
- [ ] No doctrinal claims, ranking, proselytising, or "weak faith" framing.
- [ ] Clinical care framed as compatible, not replaceable.

## False-Positive Prevention

- Frequent prayer or strict observance is not scrupulosity; flag only when practice is driven by fear, done to neutralise doubt, and never brings relief.
- Grief or doubt directed at God is common and not necessarily spiritual struggle requiring clinical attention; reflect it, and flag only when it is persistent and distressing.
- A tight-knit or demanding community is not coercive by default; coercion involves threats, control, or isolation.

## Example

**Input:** "I'm Muslim and dealing with anxiety after losing my job. Salah helps, but lately I keep repeating wudu because I'm not sure it counted. I see a therapist every two weeks. No thoughts of suicide."

**Output (abbreviated):** Safety check clear. Anchors: salah, trust in God's plan, family. Practice table pairs dhikr with slow-exhale breathing for anxious moments; mosque community as support for job leads. "When faith feels heavy": repeating wudu to feel sure it counted may be worth asking the therapist about (possible scrupulosity) — no ruling offered on whether it counted. Who to talk to: therapist for the anxiety and repetition; imam, if desired, for religious guidance.
