# Self-Advocacy on an Inpatient Psychiatric Unit

## Objective

Build a short, usable self-advocacy sheet for someone who is currently admitted (or about to be admitted) to an inpatient psychiatric unit. The sheet helps the person ask the right questions, state their goals and preferences clearly, understand their rights and the discharge plan, and ask about medications — all in a collaborative tone that works *with* the treatment team rather than against it.

## When to Use

- Newly admitted and overwhelmed, wanting to know what to ask.
- Rounds happen fast and the person keeps forgetting their questions.
- Wanting to be a partner in the treatment and discharge plan, not just a recipient.
- Wanting to understand voluntary vs. involuntary status, medications being offered, and what discharge depends on.

## Inputs / Context

- Whether the admission is voluntary or involuntary (or unknown — that's a question to ask).
- The person's own goals for this stay ("I want to feel safe enough to go home," "I want my meds sorted out").
- Preferences worth stating (a support person to involve, religious/dietary needs, sensitivities, what helped before).
- Specific worries (a medication side effect, fear of being kept longer, wanting to see a particular doctor).
- Any safety concern present right now.

## Constraints

### Must

- Output sections: **If You're in Crisis Right Now**, **Questions to Ask the Team**, **How to State My Goals and Preferences**, **Understanding My Rights**, **Asking About Medications**, **Understanding the Discharge Plan**, **After Discharge — Handoff**.
- Phrase advocacy collaboratively — partnering with the team, asking rather than demanding, while still being clear about goals and preferences.
- Under rights, prompt the person to *ask the team* about their legal status, their right to be informed about treatment, and any patient-advocate or rights officer the facility has — without stating legal conclusions.
- Generate actual sentences the person can say in rounds, not "you could ask about…".
- Include a post-discharge handoff: who to follow up with and what to bring out (medication list, follow-up appointments, crisis plan).

### Must Not

- Don't advise refusing or stopping medications, or tell the person how to get discharged faster.
- Don't make legal determinations about commitment, holds, or rights — route those to the facility's patient advocate / rights officer or an attorney.
- Don't frame the team as an adversary; staying collaborative is safer and more effective.
- Don't promise any outcome of the stay.

## Instructions

1. Check for an immediate safety concern. On a unit, tell a staff member directly; off-unit, call 988, go to the nearest ED, or call 911.
2. Capture the person's goals for the stay and the preferences worth stating in rounds.
3. Generate the questions, framed collaboratively, as actual sayable sentences.
4. Build the rights section as questions to ask the team / patient advocate, not legal conclusions.
5. Build the medication questions and the discharge-plan questions.
6. Set up the after-discharge handoff.

## Output Format

```
=== MY INPATIENT SELF-ADVOCACY SHEET ===

If You're in Crisis Right Now:
- On the unit: tell a nurse or staff member directly — "I'm not safe right
  now and I need help." That is exactly what they are here for.
- If you ever feel unsafe and can't reach staff after discharge: call or text
  988, go to the nearest ER, or call 911.

Questions to Ask the Team (say these in rounds):
- "What is my legal status here — am I voluntary or on a hold? What does that mean for me?"
- "Who is the doctor in charge of my care, and when do you round so I can be ready?"
- "What is the plan for my treatment this week?"
- "What are you hoping to see before I'm ready to be discharged?"
- "Is there a patient advocate or rights officer I can talk to?"

How to State My Goals and Preferences:
- My goal for this stay: "What I most want out of being here is [____]."
- A support person: "I'd like [name] involved in my care — here's how to reach them."
- What helps me: "When I'm escalating, what helps me is [____]; what makes it worse is [____]."
- Needs: "I have [dietary / religious / sensory / accessibility] needs: [____]."

Understanding My Rights (ask — don't assume):
- "Can someone explain my rights as a patient here, in writing if possible?"
- "If I disagree with part of my treatment, what's the process to raise that?"
- "How do I reach the patient advocate / rights officer?"
- [Note to self: for legal questions about a hold or commitment, ask the patient
  advocate or an attorney — staff can tell me how to reach one.]

Asking About Medications:
- "What is this medication for, and what should I notice if it's working?"
- "What side effects should I watch for, and which ones should I report right away?"
- "Are there alternatives, and what are the tradeoffs?"
- "I had [reaction] to [med] before — can we factor that in?"
  (I won't stop or skip a medication on my own — I'll raise concerns with the team.)

Understanding the Discharge Plan:
- "What specifically needs to happen before I go home?"
- "What does my follow-up look like — appointments, meds, who to call?"
- "Will I leave with a written crisis/safety plan and a medication list?"
- "Who do I call if things get worse after I leave?"

After Discharge — Handoff:
- Follow-up appt(s) booked: [provider, date, time]
- Medication list in hand: ☐
- Written crisis/safety plan in hand: ☐
- My outpatient therapist/prescriber notified: ☐
- "Bring all of this to my next therapist or prescriber appointment."
```

## Verification

- [ ] Immediate-safety guidance leads (tell staff on-unit; 988/ED/911 off-unit).
- [ ] Questions to the team are actual collaborative sentences.
- [ ] Goals and preferences stated as sayable lines.
- [ ] Rights section asks the team / advocate; no legal conclusions.
- [ ] Medication questions included, with no advice to stop/skip meds.
- [ ] Discharge plan and post-discharge handoff covered.
- [ ] Tone is collaborative, not adversarial; no outcome promised.
