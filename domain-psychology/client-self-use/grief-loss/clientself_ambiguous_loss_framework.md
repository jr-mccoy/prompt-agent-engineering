# Ambiguous Loss Framework

## Objective

Help the user make sense of an **ambiguous loss** — grieving someone who is still alive but lost to them in some way: a parent with dementia, a partner in active addiction, an estranged sibling, an incarcerated loved one, someone changed by brain injury. Using Pauline Boss's framework, the output names the type of ambiguity, validates the "frozen" grief, teaches **both-and** thinking, supports meaning-making, and helps the user set a boundary on hope so they can live alongside an unresolvable loss.

## When to Use

- The user is grieving someone who is still here but no longer the person they knew, or no longer in their life.
- The user feels stuck because there's no death, no funeral, no closure — and no permission to grieve.
- The user swings between hope and despair and can't settle.
- Alongside therapy or a support group for caregivers, families of addiction, or estrangement.

## Inputs / Context

- Who the loss involves and the nature of the situation (dementia, addiction, estrangement, incarceration, injury, missing person, other).
- What's still present and what's gone (their body but not their mind; their existence but not their presence).
- What makes it feel unresolvable — the back-and-forth, others' expectations, no ritual for it.
- Whether others recognize this as a loss at all.
- What the user keeps hoping for, and how that hope is affecting them.
- Any current thoughts of not wanting to be here (see escalation).

## Constraints

### Must

- Validate the "frozen" or unrecognized grief first, and name that the lack of closure is the hardest part — not a personal failing (NE-07).
- Name the **type of ambiguity** using Boss's two forms: **psychological absence with physical presence** (e.g., dementia, addiction, brain injury — body here, person gone) and **physical absence with psychological presence** (e.g., estrangement, incarceration, missing person — gone from life, still vividly present in the mind).
- Output sections in order: **What I Want You to Know**, **Naming This Loss**, **Holding Both-And**, **Finding Meaning**, **A Boundary on Hope**, **Where to Get Support**.
- Teach **both-and** explicitly (RT-04): the person is both here and gone; the user can both grieve and stay engaged; both hope and accept the present reality — these are not contradictions to resolve.
- "A Boundary on Hope" helps the user hold realistic hope without organizing their whole life around a recovery/return that may not come — without telling them to give up.
- Include a support handoff: caregiver/family support groups, a therapist familiar with ambiguous loss; and if there are thoughts of self-harm, route to a clinician this week and 988 / emergency services today.

### Must Not

- Don't push the user toward "closure" or "acceptance" as an endpoint — ambiguous loss often has neither.
- Don't tell the user to give up hope, or to keep hoping; help them calibrate it.
- Don't moralize about an estrangement, an addiction, or a decision to step back.
- Don't pathologize the back-and-forth — ambivalence is a normal response to ambiguity.

## Instructions

1. Validate the frozen, unrecognized grief.
2. Name which type of ambiguous loss this is.
3. Teach both-and as it applies to the user's situation.
4. Support meaning-making without forcing a silver lining.
5. Help set a realistic boundary on hope.
6. Hand off to support; lead with escalation if safety surfaces.

## Output Format

```
=== MAKING SENSE OF AN AMBIGUOUS LOSS ===

What I Want You to Know:
[2–3 sentences. Name that grieving someone who is still alive is one of the most disorienting kinds
of loss — there's no funeral, no closure, and often no one recognizing it as grief at all. Being
stuck is not weakness; the situation itself has no resolution, and your mind is doing its best with
an impossible setup.]

Naming This Loss:
This looks like [psychological absence / physical absence]:
- Psychological absence (present in body, lost in mind): [if dementia/addiction/injury — "[name] is
  here, but the person you knew is not fully here."]
- Physical absence (gone from your life, present in your mind): [if estrangement/incarceration/
  missing — "[name] is not in your life, but they're vividly, constantly present in your thoughts."]
Naming it doesn't fix it — but it stops you blaming yourself for not "getting over" something that
has no over.

Holding Both-And:
- [name] is both here and gone. Both can be true.
- You can both grieve them and keep showing up for them (or keep your distance). Both can be right.
- You can both hope things change and accept how things are today. You don't have to choose one.
This is "both-and" thinking. The goal isn't to resolve the contradiction — it's to stop being torn
apart by trying to.

Finding Meaning:
- What does staying connected (or stepping back) protect or honor in you?
- What relationship can you have with the version of [name] that exists now?
- Is there a role, ritual, or boundary that helps you live alongside this — not past it?

A Boundary on Hope:
Hope is allowed. But organizing your whole life around [their recovery / their return / their
remembering you] can keep you frozen. Try: "I hold hope for [X], AND I will live my life as it is
today." Decide what you'll keep doing for yourself regardless of whether [name] changes.

Where to Get Support:
- A support group for [caregivers / families affected by addiction / estrangement] — being with
  people who get it is one of the few things that helps unrecognized grief.
- A therapist familiar with ambiguous loss can help you hold the both-and over time.
- Bring this to your therapist if you have one.

[If any thought of not wanting to be here was shared, this leads the whole output:]
>> You mentioned not wanting to be here. Please reach out today — call or text 988 (US Suicide &
   Crisis Lifeline) or contact local emergency services, and tell your clinician this week. Carrying
   an unresolvable loss is exhausting, and you don't have to hold the heaviest part alone. <<
```

## Verification

- [ ] Frozen/unrecognized grief validated first (NE-07).
- [ ] Loss named by Boss type (psychological vs. physical absence).
- [ ] Both-and taught explicitly and applied to the user's situation (RT-04).
- [ ] Meaning-making offered without forcing a silver lining.
- [ ] Boundary on hope calibrates hope without telling the user to give up or to keep hoping.
- [ ] Support handoff (groups + ambiguous-loss-aware therapist) included.
- [ ] No "closure"/"acceptance" endpoint pushed; ambivalence not pathologized.
- [ ] Crisis escalation leads if SI is present: clinician this week / 988 / emergency services today.
