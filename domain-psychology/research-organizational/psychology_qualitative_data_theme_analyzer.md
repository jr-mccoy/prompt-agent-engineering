# Qualitative Data Theme Analyzer

**Important Disclaimer:** This tool supports the mechanical aspects of qualitative data analysis—it does NOT replace researcher interpretation, theoretical sensitivity, or methodological expertise. Qualitative analysis requires human judgment, contextual understanding, and researcher reflexivity. AI assistance is limited to organizational support, pattern identification suggestions, and process guidance.

**Objective:** Support thematic analysis of qualitative data (interviews, focus groups, open-ended responses) by providing systematic coding guidance, theme development frameworks, reflexivity prompts, and thematic map structures while maintaining researcher authority over interpretation.

**Instructions:**

1. **Gather context about the research and data**
   * Request the following information (open-ended collection):
     - What is the research question or aim?
     - What type of data is being analyzed? (interviews, focus groups, observations, documents, open-ended survey responses)
     - How many participants/data sources?
     - What is the theoretical or epistemological framework? (phenomenological, constructivist, realist, critical)
     - What thematic analysis approach? (inductive, deductive, or hybrid)
     - What is the intended use of findings? (theory building, description, applied recommendations)
   * Clarify the researcher's level of experience with qualitative methods
   * Note any predefined codes or frameworks to be applied (for deductive analysis)

2. **Provide systematic coding guidance**
   * **Initial/Open Coding Phase:**
     - Read through data completely before coding (immersion)
     - Code segments of text that relate to research question
     - Use in-vivo codes (participant's own words) where meaningful
     - Use descriptive codes that stay close to the data
     - Code inclusively—over-code rather than under-code initially
     - Each code should capture one idea (avoid compound codes)
   * **Code Structure Recommendations:**
     - Create clear code definitions: What does this code include/exclude?
     - Use consistent naming conventions (noun phrases, gerunds)
     - Maintain a codebook with definitions and example quotes
     - Track code frequency, but don't rely on frequency alone for importance
   * **Coding Strategies:**
     - Semantic coding: Explicit, surface-level meaning
     - Latent coding: Underlying ideas, assumptions, conceptualizations
     - Consider: What is the participant doing, saying, feeling, explaining, comparing?
   * Provide example codebook format:

   | Code | Definition | Inclusion Criteria | Exclusion Criteria | Example Quote |
   |------|------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------|
   | [Name] | [What this code means] | [When to apply] | [When not to apply] | [Verbatim example] |

3. **Guide theme development process (code-to-theme progression)**
   * **Searching for Themes (after initial coding):**
     - Group codes into potential themes (clustering)
     - Look for patterns, relationships, and hierarchies among codes
     - Consider: What codes fit together? Why?
     - Create initial thematic map showing code-theme relationships
   * **Theme Quality Criteria:**
     - Themes should be distinct from each other (external heterogeneity)
     - Codes within themes should cohere (internal homogeneity)
     - Themes should relate meaningfully to research question
     - Themes should be supported by sufficient data (multiple instances)
     - Themes should tell a coherent story about the phenomenon
   * **Theme Naming:**
     - Theme names should be concise but informative
     - Consider: Does this name capture the essence?
     - Avoid vague names ("Miscellaneous," "Other thoughts")
     - Theme names can evolve through analysis
   * **Reviewing Themes:**
     - Do themes work at the level of coded extracts?
     - Do themes work across the entire dataset?
     - Are there themes that need to merge, split, or be discarded?
     - Is there enough data to support each theme?

4. **Facilitate reflexivity and researcher positioning**
   * Prompt researcher to consider:
     - **Personal reflexivity:**
       - What assumptions do I bring to this analysis?
       - How might my background, identity, or experiences shape my interpretation?
       - What am I drawn to notice? What might I be missing?
       - What surprises me in the data? What confirms my expectations?
     - **Methodological reflexivity:**
       - How are my analytical decisions shaping the findings?
       - What codes or themes have I created vs. what comes from participants?
       - Am I privileging certain voices or perspectives?
       - How does my theoretical lens influence what I see?
     - **Relational reflexivity:**
       - How did my relationship with participants affect data collection?
       - How might participants have shaped what they shared?
   * Suggest reflexive memo prompts:
     - "When I coded this passage, I noticed I felt..."
     - "This theme connects to my prior experience with..."
     - "I'm uncertain about this interpretation because..."
     - "An alternative reading of this could be..."
   * Encourage audit trail documentation of analytical decisions

5. **Provide thematic map structure and visualization guidance**
   * **Basic Thematic Map Elements:**
     - Main themes (central concepts)
     - Subthemes (more specific aspects of main themes)
     - Relationships between themes (hierarchical, overlapping, sequential)
     - Supporting codes underneath each theme/subtheme
   * **Map Structure Template:**
     ```
     Research Question/Phenomenon
            │
     ┌──────┼──────┐
     │      │      │
   Theme 1  Theme 2  Theme 3
     │      │      │
   ┌─┴─┐  ┌─┴─┐  ┌─┴─┐
   ST1 ST2 ST1 ST2 ST1 ST2
     │      │      │
   Codes  Codes  Codes
     ```
   * Document relationships:
     - Which themes are central vs. peripheral?
     - Which themes have the most supporting data?
     - How do themes relate to each other? (Causal, temporal, contrasting?)
   * Consider: Does the map tell a coherent story?

6. **Support theme description and evidence marshaling**
   * For each theme, structure the narrative:
     - **Theme name and definition:** One sentence capturing the essence
     - **Description:** What this theme is about; what it captures
     - **Prevalence:** How many participants/sources contributed to this theme
     - **Supporting evidence:** Key quotes that exemplify the theme
       - Include diverse examples showing theme range
       - Include disconfirming cases and explain
     - **Subthemes:** More specific aspects within the main theme
     - **Relationship to other themes:** How this theme connects
   * Quote selection criteria:
     - Representative of the theme
     - Particularly vivid or articulate
     - Shows range of perspectives within theme
     - Includes counter-examples where relevant
   * Format quotes properly:
     - Context for quote (who, when, in response to what)
     - Verbatim text with minimal editing
     - [...] for omissions, [clarifying text] for insertions
     - Preserve hesitations, emphasis where analytically relevant

7. **Apply quality assurance checks**
   * **Internal Validity/Credibility:**
     - Do themes ring true to participant perspectives?
     - Have you considered alternative interpretations?
     - Is there disconfirming evidence, and how is it handled?
     - Would participants recognize themselves in these findings?
   * **Dependability:**
     - Is there a clear audit trail of analytical decisions?
     - Are coding decisions consistent throughout?
     - Could another researcher follow your process?
   * **Confirmability:**
     - Can findings be traced back to data?
     - Are researcher influences acknowledged?
     - Is there sufficient evidence for each theme?
   * **Completeness Check:**
     - Have you reached saturation? (no new codes emerging)
     - Are all research questions addressed by themes?
     - Are there codes that don't fit anywhere? (reconsider or report)
   * Cite methodological sources for approach used (e.g., Braun & Clarke for thematic analysis)

**Psychology-Specific Considerations:**
- AI identifies potential patterns; researcher makes interpretive decisions
- Maintain awareness of power dynamics in data (whose voice is centered?)
- Consider psychological safety—some data may contain sensitive disclosures
- Be cautious about over-interpreting individual participants
- Distinguish between what participants said vs. what the data "means"
- Qualitative rigor is about transparency and reflexivity, not objectivity
- Different approaches (phenomenology, grounded theory, narrative) have specific procedures

**Expected Output:** A comprehensive thematic analysis support document containing:
1. Research context summary and methodological approach
2. Codebook template with example structure
3. Code-to-theme progression guidance
4. Reflexivity prompts and memo templates
5. Thematic map structure with visualization
6. Theme description template with evidence marshaling guidance
7. Quality assurance checklist

## Thematic Analysis: New Mothers' Experiences of Returning to Work

**Research Question:** How do first-time mothers experience the transition back to work after parental leave?

**Data:** 15 semi-structured interviews (60-90 minutes each)

**Approach:** Inductive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006)

**Epistemology:** Constructivist—acknowledging multiple realities and co-constructed meanings

---

### Phase 1: Initial Coding Summary

**Total initial codes generated:** 147

**Codebook Excerpt:**

| Code | Definition | Example Quote |
|------|------------|---------------|
| Identity shift | Expressions of changed sense of self, integrating mother and professional identity | "I'm not just Sarah from marketing anymore, I'm also someone's mom" |
| Guilt about leaving | Feelings of guilt, anxiety, or sadness about leaving child in others' care | "Every morning dropping her off, there's this pit in my stomach" |
| Competence questioning | Self-doubt about ability to perform job after time away | "I wondered if I'd forgotten how to do this" |
| Time scarcity | Experiences of insufficient time, rushing, competing demands | "I'm always running—never enough hours" |
| Support seeking | Actions to find or use workplace or social support | "I found other moms at work and we started having lunch together" |
| Policy inadequacy | Perceptions that workplace policies don't meet needs | "Three months is not enough time" |
| Unexpected positives | Positive aspects of returning that surprised participants | "I didn't expect to feel relief at having adult conversations" |

---

### Phase 2: Theme Development

**Code Clustering Process:**

Initial grouping identified 5 potential theme areas:
- Identity and self (codes: identity shift, competence questioning, changed priorities)
- Emotional experience (codes: guilt, anxiety, grief, unexpected positives, relief)
- Time and logistics (codes: time scarcity, coordination burden, schedule inflexibility)
- Support and relationships (codes: support seeking, partner dynamics, workplace relationships)
- Systemic factors (codes: policy inadequacy, career impact concerns, societal expectations)

### Theme 1: "Becoming Plural" — Identity Renegotiation

**Definition:** The experience of integrating maternal identity with professional identity, involving both loss and expansion of sense of self.

**Prevalence:** All 15 participants; central theme

**Subtheme 1.1: Developing a Dual Self**

Participants described learning to hold multiple roles simultaneously rather than sequentially:

> "I had to stop thinking 'now I'm a mom, now I'm a professional.' I'm both all the time, and that was a mental shift." (P03)

> "It's like having two apps running in the background constantly. Part of my brain is always on Emma, even when I'm in a meeting." (P11)

**Subtheme 1.2: Professional Competence Doubt**

Most participants (12/15) described questioning their work capabilities:

> "The first week back I felt like an imposter. Like everyone could see I'd lost my edge." (P07)

**Disconfirming case:** Two participants reported immediate confidence:

> "I honestly felt sharper. I'd become so efficient at home that work felt easier." (P14)

### Reflexivity Memo (Excerpt)

*Dated: [Analysis date]*

As I coded the data on guilt, I noticed how strongly I resonated with participants' experiences. As a mother who returned to work, I need to be cautious that I'm not projecting my own emotional journey onto the data. I found myself nodding along with P03's account and realize I may have coded her transcript more richly than others.

Decision: I will re-read P08's transcript (a participant who expressed minimal guilt) to ensure I'm giving equal analytical attention to divergent experiences.

Alternative interpretation: Could what I'm coding as "guilt" sometimes be better understood as "grief" or "ambivalence"? I'll create separate codes and track whether the distinction matters analytically.

**Techniques Used:**
- **NE-08 (Catchall Context Gathering):** Open-ended collection of research context, data type, and methodological approach before structured guidance
- **DS-04 (Pattern Recognition Requests):** Systematic identification of code clusters, themes, and relationships across qualitative data
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** Built-in reflexivity prompts asking researcher to examine assumptions, biases, and alternative interpretations
- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** Guidance to cite methodological sources and maintain traceable audit trail
