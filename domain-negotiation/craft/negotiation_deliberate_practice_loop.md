# Negotiation Practice Loop — Drill One Sub-Skill Against Real Feedback

**Objective:** Negotiation is a domain where experience alone does not reliably produce skill, and the reasons are structural. The feedback is **slow** — months between a move and its consequence. It is **noisy** — outcomes turn substantially on the counterpart's constraints, which you never see. It is **confounded** — a good result from a bad process is indistinguishable from a good result from a good one, unless someone separates them deliberately. And the reps are **rare**: most people conduct a handful of significant negotiations a year, which is far too few for pattern learning. This prompt builds a loop that works around all four. It selects **one sub-skill** narrow enough to drill, finds **high-frequency low-stakes repetitions** that occur weekly rather than annually, defines an **observable that is independent of outcome**, and sets a review cadence.

`domain-learning/learning_deliberate_practice_designer.md` is the domain-general version of this method; this applies it to negotiation's specific feedback problems and does not restate the general theory.

**When to use:**
- You have identified something to improve, via `craft/negotiation_style_self_assessment.md` or feedback.
- You negotiate regularly but have not improved noticeably in a while.
- You want to build a specific capability before a known upcoming negotiation.
- Your reps are too infrequent for learning and you need to manufacture more.

**When NOT to use:**
- You have not identified what to work on — `craft/negotiation_style_self_assessment.md` first.
- The gap is knowledge rather than skill — read and apply the relevant `preparation/` prompt; drilling does not fix not knowing something.
- You want to assess a specific negotiation — `after-the-deal/negotiation_post_negotiation_debrief.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who want negotiating skill to compound.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The target.** What you are trying to improve, as specifically as you can state it.
2. **Where it came from.** Self-assessment, feedback, or a repeated failure.
3. **Your negotiation frequency.** How often significant negotiations actually occur.
4. **Low-stakes opportunities.** Everyday situations with the same structure.
5. **Available feedback sources.** Anyone who observes you negotiating, or a recording.
6. **Time available.** Realistically, per week.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Narrow the target to **one drillable sub-skill**. "Be a better negotiator" cannot be practised; "not responding to a counter-offer in the same conversation" can.
- Find **high-frequency, low-stakes repetitions**. Practising only in real negotiations gives you a handful of reps a year, which is not a practice loop.
- Define an **outcome-independent observable**. You must be able to tell whether you executed the behaviour regardless of whether the negotiation went well.
- Build in **feedback**, since unobserved practice reinforces whatever you already do, including the error.
- Set a **review cadence** measured in reps rather than in time.
- Keep the loop **small enough to actually run**. An elaborate practice plan is an abandoned practice plan.
- Choose a target with **transfer** — a sub-skill that appears in many negotiations rather than one situation.

### Must Not
- Practise the whole activity. Running more negotiations is experience, not practice, and it is what has already failed to produce improvement.
- Use outcome as the measure of a rep. Outcomes are confounded by the counterpart's constraints and by luck; a well-executed move in a lost negotiation is a successful rep.
- Practise without feedback. It reinforces the existing pattern, including the one you are trying to change, and it feels like progress.
- Set a target requiring high-stakes negotiations to practise. You will get four reps a year and no learning.
- Drill something already fluent. Practice should be at the edge of current capability, which is uncomfortable — comfort is the signal you are rehearsing rather than practising.
- Change targets before the review point. Switching early guarantees no measurable improvement in anything.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Narrow to one sub-skill
Take the improvement target and narrow it until it is a **specific behaviour in a specific moment**. Progressive narrowing looks like: "be more assertive" → "hold my position when pushed" → "when someone counters, I do not concede in the same conversation." The last one is drillable: you can execute it, and you can tell afterward whether you did. If you cannot say what executing it looks like, it is still too broad.

### Step 2 — Test the target for transfer
Does this sub-skill appear across many negotiation types, or only one situation? High-transfer targets — holding silence, asking for specifics before responding, stating a number without hedging, separating acknowledgement from agreement — pay off across every negotiation you have. Low-transfer targets are worth drilling only if the situation recurs often for you.

### Step 3 — Find high-frequency, low-stakes reps
The central move of the whole design. Most people have far too few real negotiations to learn from, so the reps must be manufactured from everyday situations with the same structure:

| Sub-skill | Everyday reps |
|---|---|
| Holding silence after a proposal | Any meeting where you make a suggestion |
| Not conceding in the same conversation | Any request for a decision — internal or personal |
| Asking for specifics before responding | Every piece of feedback or vague request you receive |
| Stating a number without hedging | Quoting timelines, effort estimates, availability |
| Acknowledgement without agreement | Any disagreement, at work or at home |
| Separating position from interest | Any argument, including domestic ones |

Target **several reps a week**, not several a year. Low stakes are a feature: they make failure cheap enough to actually attempt the uncomfortable version.

### Step 4 — Define the outcome-independent observable
Write what counts as a successful rep, phrased so it can be scored regardless of how things turned out. *"I paused for at least three seconds after making the proposal and did not speak first"* is observable. *"The meeting went well"* is not. This separation is what makes the loop work in a domain where outcomes are noisy — you are scoring execution, not results.

### Step 5 — Build in feedback
Three sources, in descending order of practicality:
- **Self-observation immediately after** — score the rep within minutes, while the memory is accurate. This is the workhorse.
- **A colleague who is present** — briefed on the specific behaviour and asked for one observation, not a general assessment.
- **Recording**, where the setting permits it — the most accurate and the least convenient.

Unobserved practice reinforces the existing pattern while feeling like effort, which is the worst combination available.

### Step 6 — Set the cadence and the log
Decide the target rep count per week and keep a minimal log: date, situation, executed y/n, and one line on what happened. Minimal is essential — an elaborate log is abandoned within a fortnight, and an abandoned log ends the loop. A single line per rep is enough to see the pattern.

### Step 7 — Review at the rep count, not the calendar
Set the review at a **number of reps** (twenty is a reasonable default) rather than a date, since rep frequency varies. At review: what is the execution rate, has it moved, and is the behaviour becoming automatic or still requiring deliberate attention? Automaticity is the goal — a behaviour that requires conscious effort will not survive a high-stakes moment, which is precisely when you need it.

### Step 8 — Decide the next target
When the current sub-skill executes reliably under low stakes, test it once in a real negotiation, then choose the next target from the self-assessment. Do not run two targets at once — divided attention produces two half-learned behaviours and no measurable improvement in either.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Can you actually get several reps a week, or does this design require negotiations you do not have?
- Is the observable scoreable without knowing the outcome?
- Are you practising something already fluent because the genuinely hard thing is uncomfortable?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Whole-activity practice.** "Negotiate more." That is experience, and experience has already failed to produce improvement here — which is why a loop is needed at all.
2. **Outcome-based scoring.** Judging a rep by how the negotiation went. Outcomes turn on the counterpart's constraints and on luck; a well-executed move in a lost negotiation is a successful rep and must be scored as one.
3. **Real-negotiations-only practice.** A design that requires significant negotiations. Four reps a year is not a practice loop; it is a hope.
4. **Unobserved practice.** Drilling with no feedback source. It reinforces whatever you currently do, including the error, and it feels productive throughout.
5. **Target breadth.** A sub-skill too broad to execute or score. If you cannot say what doing it looks like in a specific moment, narrow further before starting.
6. **Fluency drilling.** Practising something you already do well because it is pleasant. Practice should sit at the edge of capability, and comfort is the signal that it does not.
7. **Elaborate logging.** A tracking system with more than a line per rep. It is abandoned within a fortnight, and its abandonment ends the loop rather than just the logging.
8. **Target switching.** Changing focus before the review point, usually because progress is not visible yet. It guarantees no measurable improvement in anything, and it feels like adaptability.

## Verification

- [ ] Target narrowed to a specific behaviour in a specific moment, with execution described.
- [ ] Transfer tested; low-transfer targets justified by situation frequency.
- [ ] High-frequency low-stakes reps identified, targeting several per week.
- [ ] Observable defined so a rep can be scored without knowing the outcome.
- [ ] Feedback source built in, with self-observation timed within minutes.
- [ ] Log kept to one line per rep.
- [ ] Review set at a rep count rather than a date.
- [ ] Automaticity named as the goal, not merely execution.
- [ ] Next-target trigger defined, with one target at a time confirmed.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests rep availability and fluency-drilling.
- [ ] No rep scored by negotiation outcome.
- [ ] No design requiring high-stakes negotiations to practise.
