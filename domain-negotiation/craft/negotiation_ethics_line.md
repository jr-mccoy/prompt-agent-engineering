# The Ethics Line — Where Persuasion Becomes Manipulation, Decided in Advance

**Objective:** Negotiation involves persuasion, incomplete disclosure, and the pursuit of advantage — all legitimate. It also has a boundary, and the boundary is genuinely contested in the middle even where the ends are clear. The problem is not usually that people cannot tell right from wrong in the abstract. It is that the decision gets made **inside a situation that rewards deciding it favourably**: under time pressure, with a deal in reach, with a plausible argument available for why this particular case is different. Every one of those conditions degrades judgment in the same direction. This prompt moves the decision earlier. It separates the settled cases from the genuinely contested middle, supplies three tests that resolve most of the middle, produces **pre-committed limits** written down before the pressure arrives, and prepares the response for when the other side crosses their own line — which is a separate problem with a separate answer.

This is not a compliance document and it does not address legal obligations, which are a floor rather than the subject. It is about the space above the legal floor where the choices are actually yours.

**When to use:**
- Before a negotiation where you expect pressure to do something you would rather not.
- You did something in a past negotiation that you are still uneasy about.
- You are deciding how to respond to a counterpart who is behaving badly.
- You are setting expectations for a team that negotiates on your behalf.

**When NOT to use:**
- The question is legal rather than ethical — route to `domain-legal/`; legality is the floor, not the line.
- You need to defend against coercive tactics — `at-the-table/negotiation_hard_bargainer_defense.md`.
- You are deciding what information to protect, which is a strategy question with an ethics component — `preparation/negotiation_information_plan.md`.
- The influence is not bilateral and consented — you are analyzing a third party working on an audience that does not know it, or a pattern in a personal relationship — `domain-psy-ops/`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who negotiate and would prefer to decide their limits when they are not under pressure.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The negotiation type.** What you negotiate and how often.
2. **The pressures you face.** Targets, incentives, expectations from above.
3. **Past unease.** Anything you have done that you are not comfortable with in retrospect.
4. **Observed behaviour.** What counterparts in your field do, and what is treated as normal.
5. **Your constraints.** Professional obligations, organizational policy, and where they sit relative to your own line.
6. **Who you represent.** Whether you negotiate for yourself or on behalf of others.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Distinguish the **settled cases** (both ends) from the **genuinely contested middle**, and spend the effort on the middle.
- Apply the three tests — **publicity, reciprocity, and relationship** — to each contested case.
- Produce **pre-committed limits in writing**. A limit decided under pressure is not a limit; it is a preference that lost.
- Distinguish **legality from the line**. Much that is lawful sits above the line, and the law is a floor rather than an answer.
- Prepare the **response to the other side crossing**, which is a separate question with three distinct answers.
- Account for **incentive pressure** honestly — what your compensation, targets, or management rewards you for doing.
- Note the **asymmetry** where one exists: what is acceptable against a sophisticated repeat player may not be acceptable against an unrepresented individual.

### Must Not
- Treat legality as the standard. It is the floor, and the interesting decisions all sit above it.
- Resolve the contested middle in the abstract without reference to your actual situations. Generic answers do not survive contact with a specific deal.
- Decide limits during a negotiation. The situation systematically favours the permissive answer, and it supplies a plausible argument for it.
- Assume your counterpart shares your line. They may hold a different one honestly.
- Confuse being uncomfortable with being wrong. Holding a hard position, declining, and letting a deal fail are all uncomfortable and none is unethical.
- Use "everyone does this" as a resolution. It is a description of a field, not an argument, and it is frequently inaccurate about what everyone does.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Mark the settled cases at both ends
Quickly, because they are not where the work is. **Clearly out:** stating known falsehoods about material facts, fabricating competing offers, concealing a defect you are obliged to disclose, exploiting someone's incapacity, threats outside the negotiation's subject. **Clearly in:** not volunteering your reservation point, arguing your position forcefully, using a deadline that is real, declining to answer, walking away. Naming these first clears the ground and makes the middle visible.

### Step 2 — Enumerate your actual contested middle
The cases that recur in **your** field. Common ones:

| Contested move | The genuine argument for | The genuine argument against |
|---|---|---|
| Silence about a known defect | No duty to argue their side | They would not deal on these terms if they knew |
| Manufactured urgency | Deadlines are a normal device | It manufactures a fact to induce reliance |
| Exploiting their arithmetic error | Their error, their responsibility | Consent based on a mistake you saw |
| Strategic ambiguity | Precision is not owed | Designed to be misread |
| Implying an alternative you lack | Never stated it explicitly | The implication is the point |
| Anchoring far outside plausibility | Openings are expected to be high | It is a claim about value you do not hold |

Write yours. Generic lists do not resolve specific situations; your recurring cases will.

### Step 3 — Apply the publicity test
Would you be comfortable if this move, and your reasoning for it, were described accurately to the counterpart afterward — or to a colleague, or in a trade publication? Not *would you be caught*, but *would the description be one you would accept as fair*. This test resolves more of the middle than any other, because most contested moves depend on the counterpart not knowing what was done.

### Step 4 — Apply the reciprocity test
Would you consider it legitimate if done to you, by someone in their position? This catches the asymmetry that self-interest introduces. Note it is not "would you like it" — you would not like a firm counter-anchor, and a firm counter-anchor is entirely legitimate. The question is whether you would regard it as within bounds, which is a different and more demanding standard.

### Step 5 — Apply the relationship test
Would this survive the counterpart finding out during implementation, at renewal, or from a third party? Many contested moves are stable only while undiscovered, and discovery is more common than it feels at the time — through a colleague moving companies, a shared adviser, or the simple passage of time. A move that only works while hidden is a move with a delayed cost.

### Step 6 — Handle the asymmetry question
Your line may legitimately shift with the counterpart. A sophisticated repeat player with advisers has meaningful protection; an unrepresented individual negotiating something significant to them does not. Many people hold — reasonably — that the second case warrants more disclosure and less pressure than the first. Decide your position explicitly rather than defaulting, and note that the situations where you have the most advantage are the situations where this question matters most.

### Step 7 — Write the pre-committed limits
The output. Short, specific, and in writing: what you will not do, regardless of what is at stake. Not principles but rules — "I do not reference competing offers I do not have," "I disclose known defects material to the price," "I do not use a deadline I have invented." Rules survive pressure; principles get reinterpreted by it. Then note the incentive pressure honestly: what would your targets, compensation, or management reward you for doing here, and does any limit conflict with that? A limit that conflicts with your incentives needs to be written down more than one that does not.

### Step 8 — Prepare for their crossing
Separate question, three answers depending on severity. **Name it** — "I want to flag that the offer I was told about doesn't seem to exist" — which is often sufficient and preserves the deal. **Adjust the terms** — require verification, shorten commitments, tighten remedies — because conduct in the negotiation predicts conduct in implementation. **Withdraw** — where the behaviour indicates a counterparty you should not be in business with. Note explicitly: **their crossing does not move your line.** Reciprocal bad behaviour is the most common route across it, and it always feels justified at the time.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Which limit would break first under real pressure, and what would the pressure be?
- Are you writing limits you already comfortably keep, and avoiding the one that is actually contested for you?
- What did you do in a past negotiation that these limits would have prevented?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Legality as the line.** Treating lawful as acceptable. The law is a floor, and every interesting question in this document sits above it.
2. **In-the-moment deciding.** Resolving an ethical question during a negotiation. The situation systematically favours the permissive answer and helpfully supplies a plausible argument for why this case is different.
3. **Abstract resolution.** Answering the contested middle generically rather than against your recurring situations. Generic answers do not survive contact with a specific deal at a specific moment.
4. **"Everyone does this."** Treating field norms as a resolution. It describes a practice rather than justifying it, and it is frequently wrong about what everyone actually does.
5. **Discomfort-as-wrongness.** Concluding a move was unethical because it felt bad. Holding a firm position, declining, and letting a deal fail are uncomfortable and legitimate; the confusion produces soft negotiating dressed as integrity.
6. **Reciprocal crossing.** Adopting a counterpart's tactics because they used them first. It is the most common route across your own line and it feels justified throughout.
7. **Principles instead of rules.** Writing "I will be honest" rather than "I do not reference offers I do not have." Principles are reinterpreted under pressure; specific rules are harder to reason around.
8. **Incentive blindness.** Setting limits without noting what you are rewarded for doing. A limit that conflicts with your compensation is the one that will break, and it needs to be written down most.
