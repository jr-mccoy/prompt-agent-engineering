# Negotiation Style Self-Assessment — Your Default Under Pressure and What It Costs

**Objective:** Everyone has a default negotiating behaviour that appears under pressure, and it is reliably different from the one they would describe. The gap matters because the default is what actually operates in the moment that determines the outcome — the point at which the counterpart pushes, the silence extends, or the deadline compresses. This prompt identifies the revealed style from **evidence** rather than self-description: what you did last time it was hard, which move you reach for first, what you consistently avoid, and where your outcomes cluster. It maps five dimensions, identifies the specific situations where your default costs you (every style is well-adapted to some situations and badly adapted to others), and produces **one adjustment** worth drilling — because style is not changed wholesale, and attempting it produces someone negotiating badly in an unfamiliar mode.

This works best on the output of several `after-the-deal/negotiation_post_negotiation_debrief.md` runs. One negotiation is an anecdote; five show a pattern.

**When to use:**
- Your negotiation outcomes cluster in a way you cannot explain.
- You want to improve but do not know what to work on.
- Feedback has suggested something about how you negotiate that you want to test.
- Before designing a practice plan — `craft/negotiation_deliberate_practice_loop.md` needs a target.

**When NOT to use:**
- You have no negotiation history to work from — run several negotiations and debrief them first.
- You want to assess a single negotiation — `negotiation_post_negotiation_debrief.md`.
- The question is what to do in a live negotiation rather than what you tend to do — use `at-the-table/`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who negotiate regularly enough for patterns to exist.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Recent negotiations.** Ideally five or more, with outcomes.
2. **Outcomes relative to targets.** Where results landed against what you aimed for.
3. **What you did when it got hard.** The specific moves at the pressure points.
4. **What you avoid.** Conversations, moves, or moments you route around.
5. **Feedback received.** Anything anyone has said about how you negotiate.
6. **How you feel afterward.** Relief, frustration, or satisfaction — patterned across negotiations.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Work from **evidence, not self-description**. What you did, not what you would do.
- Assess across **all five dimensions**, since a single-axis style label (hard/soft) is too coarse to act on.
- Identify where the default is an **asset**, not only where it is a liability. Every style is well-adapted somewhere.
- Name the **specific situations** where it costs you, with examples from actual negotiations.
- Produce **one adjustment**, not a development plan. Style changes at the margin or not at all.
- Distinguish **style from skill**. Being uncomfortable with silence is a style trait; not knowing your BATNA is a preparation gap.
- Use **outcome clustering** as evidence — where your results consistently land relative to targets is more informative than any recollection.

### Must Not
- Assess from a self-description of how you like to negotiate. Stated and revealed styles diverge reliably, and the stated one is the aspirational one.
- Assume one style is correct. Accommodating is right in some relationships; competitive is right in some one-shot deals.
- Recommend wholesale change. It produces someone negotiating badly in a mode they have not internalized, which is worse than their default.
- Treat a preparation failure as a style trait. Not knowing your walkaway is a fixable gap, not a personality.
- Ignore the situations where the default works. Removing it entirely costs the advantage it provides.
- Confuse discomfort with error. Feeling bad after holding a firm position is not evidence that holding it was wrong.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Assemble the evidence base
List recent negotiations with: what you aimed for, what you got, what you did at the hardest moment, and how you felt afterward. Five or more is where patterns become visible. If you have fewer, note that the findings are provisional — and go run `after-the-deal/negotiation_post_negotiation_debrief.md` on the next several.

### Step 2 — Map the five dimensions
Rate yourself on each from evidence:

| Dimension | Low end | High end | Evidence to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assertion** | Concedes to keep things pleasant | Holds position past discomfort | What you did when pushed |
| **Discomfort tolerance** | Fills silence, closes fast | Sits in unresolved tension | Response to pauses and stalls |
| **Preparation** | Improvises, trusts the room | Over-prepares, may be inflexible | What you do the day before |
| **Relationship weighting** | Deal-first | Relationship-first, may under-claim | What you sacrifice when they conflict |
| **Information** | Discloses openly, builds trust fast | Guards closely, may block value creation | What you volunteer unprompted |

For each, cite the specific evidence. A rating with no example attached is self-description in disguise.

### Step 3 — Read the outcome clustering
Where do your results land relative to targets? The clusters are diagnostic:

| Pattern | Likely default |
|---|---|
| Consistently near your floor | Low assertion, or a floor set too high to be real |
| Consistently fast closes | Low discomfort tolerance |
| Good terms, damaged relationships | High assertion, low relationship weighting |
| Deals that were available and lost | High assertion or low flexibility |
| Good outcomes, exhausting process | Possibly fine — check whether the cost is sustainable |

Outcome clustering is the most reliable evidence available, because it is independent of how the negotiations felt.

### Step 4 — Name where the default is an asset
Before the costs. Every style is well-adapted somewhere: high relationship weighting is right in long-term partnerships and repeated dealings; high assertion is right in one-shot distributive deals; heavy preparation is right in complex multi-issue negotiations. Name the situations where yours works, because the adjustment in Step 6 must not remove it there.

### Step 5 — Name where it costs you, with examples
Now the liabilities, tied to actual negotiations rather than to hypotheticals. Be specific: not "I'm too accommodating" but "in the three renewals this year I accepted the first counter-offer within an hour, and in two of them the counterpart later mentioned they had room." The example is what makes the finding usable, and it is also what makes it credible to yourself.

### Step 6 — Separate style from skill gaps
Sort your findings into two piles. **Style:** discomfort with silence, difficulty holding a position, tendency to over-disclose, aversion to conflict. **Skill or preparation gaps:** not knowing your BATNA, no concession plan, no information plan, not establishing authority. The second pile is fixable directly by running the relevant `preparation/` prompts and is far higher-return than working on style. Do that pile first — a surprising proportion of what people diagnose as a style problem is a preparation problem with an emotional aftertaste.

### Step 7 — Choose the one adjustment
One. Specific. Situationally bounded. Not "be more assertive" but "when I receive a counter-offer, I do not respond in the same conversation — I say I'll come back tomorrow." Good adjustments are **behavioural rules**, not intentions, because rules survive pressure and intentions do not. Choose the adjustment that addresses the highest-frequency cost from Step 5, and write the trigger that activates it.

### Step 8 — Set the test
How will you know it is working? Define the observable: a change in outcome clustering, a specific behaviour appearing in a specific moment, or a counterpart response. Then set a review point — after three negotiations, not after one — and hand off to `craft/negotiation_deliberate_practice_loop.md` to build the drill.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Which dimension did you rate most favourably, and what evidence would contradict it?
- Are you describing the negotiator you want to be?
- Is the adjustment you chose the one that matters, or the one that is comfortable to work on?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Self-description.** Rating yourself on how you intend to negotiate. Stated and revealed styles diverge reliably, and the stated version is aspirational — which makes the whole assessment useless.
2. **Single-axis labelling.** Reducing style to hard-or-soft. It is too coarse to act on and it obscures the actual pattern, which is usually a specific behaviour in a specific moment.
3. **Style-as-defect.** Treating your default as a problem to eliminate. It is well-adapted to some situations, and removing it there costs more than it gains elsewhere.
4. **Preparation gaps misfiled as style.** "I'm not assertive enough" when the actual issue is not knowing your walkaway. The second is directly fixable and much higher-return; the first is slow work on the wrong thing.
5. **Wholesale change.** Attempting to become a different kind of negotiator. It produces someone performing an unfamiliar mode badly, which underperforms their honest default.
6. **Discomfort as evidence.** Concluding that a firm position was wrong because holding it felt bad. Feeling bad after negotiating well is common and is not diagnostic.
7. **Anecdotal basis.** Assessing from one memorable negotiation. Memorable ones are unrepresentative — that is usually why they are memorable. Use clustering across several.
8. **Comfortable adjustment.** Choosing the improvement that is easiest rather than the one addressing the highest-frequency cost. It produces visible effort and no change in outcomes.

## Verification

- [ ] Evidence base assembled from actual negotiations with outcomes, not from self-description.
- [ ] Findings marked provisional if fewer than five negotiations.
- [ ] All five dimensions rated, each with a specific cited example.
- [ ] Outcome clustering read as primary evidence.
- [ ] Situations where the default is an asset named before the liabilities.
- [ ] Costs tied to named negotiations with concrete examples.
- [ ] Findings sorted into style versus preparation gaps, with gaps prioritized.
- [ ] Exactly one adjustment chosen, expressed as a behavioural rule with a trigger.
- [ ] Adjustment addresses the highest-frequency cost, not the most comfortable one.
- [ ] Observable test and a review point after three negotiations defined.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests the most favourable rating and the aspiration question.
- [ ] No wholesale style change recommended.
- [ ] No preparation gap misfiled as a style trait.
