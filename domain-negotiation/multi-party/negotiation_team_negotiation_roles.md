# Team Negotiation Roles — Who Speaks, Who Watches, and How Not to Be Split

**Objective:** A team is a negotiating advantage only when it is coordinated. Uncoordinated, it is a liability with more surface area: more people who can be asked a question outside their lane, more chances for visible internal disagreement, more seams for a counterpart to work. The classic failures are specific and repeatable — the technical colleague who answers a commercial question helpfully and concedes a term nobody authorized; the junior member cultivated with warmth until they volunteer what the team's real constraint is; the moment two people on the same side visibly disagree and the counterpart negotiates against the softer one from then on. This prompt assigns four roles, sets the internal signals, establishes the caucus and no-solo-concession rules, and runs the pre-brief that makes them hold under pressure.

This handles **your side's** coordination. `negotiation_multi_party_alignment.md` handles coordination *across* three or more separate parties.

**When to use:**
- More than one person from your side will attend a consequential negotiation.
- A previous team session produced an unauthorized concession or a visible internal split.
- You are bringing a technical or functional specialist into a commercial conversation.
- The counterpart is known to work individual team members separately.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are negotiating alone — though `at-the-table/negotiation_authority_mandate_limits.md` still applies.
- The complexity is across multiple independent parties rather than within your own team — `negotiation_multi_party_alignment.md`.
- The gathering is informational rather than a negotiation.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, sales leads, people leaders, lawyers, and project leads taking a team into a negotiation.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The negotiation.** What is being negotiated and what this session must achieve.
2. **Your attendees.** Who is coming, their function, seniority, and experience negotiating.
3. **Their attendees.** Who will be present on the other side, and in what roles.
4. **Mandate.** What your side can agree to, and who holds that authority.
5. **Known seams.** Any internal disagreement, inexperience, or relationship the counterpart could work.
6. **Format.** In person, video, or mixed — mixed formats make coordination harder.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Assign **four distinct roles** — speaker, observer, technical authority, decider — even if one person holds two. Unassigned roles get filled improvisationally, badly.
- Establish the **no-solo-concession rule**: nobody agrees to anything outside their named lane without a caucus.
- Define **lane boundaries explicitly** and give each person the exact sentence for a question outside theirs.
- Set **internal signals** — how to request a break, flag a concern, or stop a colleague mid-sentence without it being visible.
- Run a **pre-brief** covering roles, mandate, the lane sentences, and the two or three questions most likely to be aimed at the wrong person.
- Prohibit **visible internal disagreement**. Disagreement is caucused, never performed in the room.
- Plan the **debrief**, because the observer will have seen things the speaker could not.

### Must Not
- Bring people without a role. Every attendee is a surface the counterpart can work; an attendee with no function is pure downside.
- Let the most senior person default to speaker. Seniority and negotiating skill are different, and putting the decider in the speaker seat removes your ability to defer.
- Allow the decider to be the primary speaker. Separating them preserves the "let me take that back to the team" move without it being a fiction.
- Let a specialist answer commercial questions. The helpful technical answer that concedes scope is among the most common unauthorized concessions.
- Correct a colleague in the room. Whatever the cost of the error, the cost of a visible split is higher and lasts the rest of the negotiation.
- Assume juniors will not be targeted. They are targeted precisely because they are, and because warmth works.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Assign the four roles
Name a person to each, and state what each does and does not do:

| Role | Does | Does not |
|---|---|---|
| **Speaker** | Carries the position, makes the asks, controls pace | Decide; concede beyond mandate |
| **Observer** | Watches the counterpart, tracks commitments, notes reactions | Speak, except when invited by the speaker |
| **Technical authority** | Answers factual and feasibility questions in their domain | Answer commercial, timing, or pricing questions |
| **Decider** | Holds final authority; usually silent | Speak first; be drawn into detail |

One person can hold two roles. Speaker and decider should be different people wherever possible — the separation is what makes "I'll need to take that back" a genuine process rather than a device.

### Step 2 — Draw the lane boundaries and write the sentences
For each attendee, write what is in their lane and give them the **exact sentence** for anything outside it: "That's a commercial question — [speaker] is closer to that than I am." Rehearse it. Under direct questioning, especially friendly questioning, people answer because not answering feels rude; a pre-rehearsed sentence removes the awkwardness that produces the unauthorized answer.

### Step 3 — Set the no-solo-concession rule
State it plainly and get explicit agreement: no one on this side agrees to anything — a number, a date, a scope change, a term — without a caucus. This includes agreeing in principle, which is the form most unauthorized concessions take. It includes hallway and pre-meeting conversations, which is where they most often happen.

### Step 4 — Establish internal signals
Agree three, and keep them unremarkable:
- **Request a break:** a neutral spoken phrase anyone can use — "should we take five and regroup?"
- **Flag a concern silently:** a written note or an agreed message; never a facial expression, which the counterpart reads as easily as your colleague.
- **Stop a colleague:** the speaker's agreed interruption line — "Let me come in there —" — which everyone has been told in advance is a signal, not a rudeness.

For remote or mixed formats, the private channel must be agreed and tested before the session.

### Step 5 — Close the seams
Identify what a counterpart would work and pre-empt each:
- **The specialist:** briefed to route commercial questions, and told explicitly that helpfulness is the failure mode.
- **The junior:** told they will likely be approached warmly and separately, that this is normal and not personal, and given the routing sentence.
- **The visible split:** the rule that disagreement is caucused; if a colleague says something wrong, it is corrected in caucus and restated by the speaker afterward, never contradicted in the room.
- **The side conversation:** breaks, arrivals, and departures are part of the negotiation; the no-solo-concession rule applies in the corridor.

### Step 6 — Run the pre-brief
Fifteen minutes before, covering: roles and lanes; the mandate — what can be agreed here and what cannot; the lane sentences, said aloud once each; the two or three questions most likely to be aimed at the wrong person, with the routing rehearsed; and the caucus trigger. Then confirm one thing explicitly: **who speaks first**, and what they open with.

### Step 7 — Use the caucus properly
Caucus on any of: an unexpected offer, a question the team has not aligned on, a request outside mandate, or any team member's flagged concern. During the caucus, take the observer's read first — they have been watching while the speaker was talking, and they will have seen more. Decide, then return with a single voice through the speaker.

### Step 8 — Debrief immediately
Within an hour, while it is fresh. The observer reports first and in full: what the counterpart reacted to, who deferred to whom on their side, what was avoided, what changed the room. Then reconcile: what was agreed, what was implied, what each person believes was committed. Unreconciled team recollections become the next session's internal disagreement.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Which person on your side would a skilled counterpart approach separately, and what would they ask them?
- If your team were asked the same question individually, would the answers match?
- What did you agree to in the room that nobody explicitly authorized?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Roleless attendees.** Bringing people because they are involved in the work. Each attendee is an additional surface; without a role and a lane sentence they are a liability, not support.
2. **Senior-as-speaker default.** Putting the most senior person in the speaker seat. It conflates seniority with negotiating skill and, worse, removes the deferral move by putting the decider in the conversation.
3. **The helpful specialist.** A technical colleague answering a commercial question accurately and generously, conceding scope or timing nobody authorized. Helpfulness is the mechanism; the routing sentence is the fix.
4. **Visible correction.** Contradicting a teammate in the room. The counterpart stops negotiating with the team and starts negotiating with whichever member is softer, for the remainder of the relationship.
5. **Expression-based signalling.** Using a look to flag concern. Counterparts read the room at least as well as your colleague does, and a visible flinch discloses more than the comment it was meant to prevent.
6. **Corridor exemption.** Treating breaks, arrivals, and walks to the lift as outside the negotiation. A large share of unauthorized concessions happen there, in a register that feels social.
7. **Junior underestimation.** Assuming junior members will not be worked. They are targeted precisely because they are junior, and because warmth is effective and hard to rebuff without a rehearsed line.
8. **Debrief deferral.** Postponing the debrief past the same day. The observer's read decays fast, and unreconciled recollections of what was agreed become the next session's internal split — which the counterpart will see.

## Verification

- [ ] All four roles assigned to named people, with lanes stated.
- [ ] Speaker and decider separated, or the exception justified.
- [ ] Every attendee has an exact out-of-lane sentence, rehearsed aloud.
- [ ] No-solo-concession rule stated and explicitly agreed, including corridors and breaks.
- [ ] Three internal signals agreed, none of them facial.
- [ ] Private channel tested if the format is remote or mixed.
- [ ] Seams identified with pre-emption for specialist, junior, and visible split.
- [ ] Pre-brief agenda includes who speaks first and their opening line.
- [ ] Caucus triggers named, with the observer reporting first.
- [ ] Debrief scheduled within the hour, with reconciliation of agreed vs. implied.
- [ ] Adversarial check identifies who would be approached separately and whether answers would match.
- [ ] No attendee without a role.
- [ ] No provision for correcting a colleague in the room.
