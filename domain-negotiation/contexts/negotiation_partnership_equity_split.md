# Partnership and Equity Split — The Conversation Nobody Wants to Have Early

**Objective:** Equity splits are negotiated badly for a structural reason: the right time to have the conversation is when the relationship is strongest and the discussion feels least necessary, and the wrong time — when contribution has diverged and someone feels short-changed — is when it feels urgent. The characteristic failure is an even split agreed in an hour to avoid discomfort, with no vesting, no departure terms, and no rule for deadlock, revisited eighteen months later when one partner has stopped contributing and holds half the company. This prompt runs the conversation while it is still cheap. It separates **past contribution** from **future commitment** — the latter matters far more and is the harder thing to price. It handles asymmetries in role, risk, capital, and opportunity cost. And it treats **vesting, departure terms, and a deadlock rule as prerequisites**, not refinements: an equity number without them is not an agreement, it is a hope.

`domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_partnership_cofounder_selection.md` decides whether to partner with someone. This negotiates the terms once you have decided to.

**When to use:**
- Agreeing an equity or profit split with a co-founder or partner.
- An existing split has become misaligned with contribution and needs reopening.
- Adding a partner to an existing arrangement.
- A partner is departing and the terms were never defined.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are still deciding whether to partner with this person — `personal_partnership_cofounder_selection.md`.
- You need the legal instruments drafted — this structures the negotiation; drafting is `domain-legal/corporate-ma/`.
- The relationship has already broken down and this is a dispute — that is a legal and possibly mediated process.

**Audience:** Founders, partners, and individuals negotiating an ownership or profit split.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The parties and their roles.** Who does what, now and intended.
2. **Contribution to date.** Time, capital, IP, relationships, and work already done.
3. **Future commitment.** What each will do, full or part time, and for how long.
4. **Risk borne.** Salary forgone, capital at risk, opportunity cost, personal guarantees.
5. **What already exists.** Any prior agreement, understanding, or informal split.
6. **The relationship.** Its history, and whether hard conversations have gone well before.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Separate **past contribution** from **future commitment**, and weight future far more heavily — the work overwhelmingly remains ahead.
- Make **vesting a prerequisite**, not a negotiable extra. Equity without vesting is a gift for showing up on day one.
- Define **departure terms before agreeing any number**: what happens to equity on leaving, being asked to leave, stopping contributing, or dying.
- Establish a **deadlock rule**. A 50/50 split with no tiebreaker is a structure that cannot make a decision the partners disagree on.
- Price **asymmetric risk** explicitly — forgone salary, capital at risk, and personal guarantees are not equivalent to time.
- Surface each partner's **actual expectations** about role, control, and exit, since these diverge silently and expensively.
- Document it. An unwritten split is not a split.

### Must Not
- Default to an even split to avoid the conversation. Equal may well be right; arriving at it by avoidance means the reasoning is unavailable when it is later questioned.
- Agree a number without vesting, departure terms, and a deadlock rule. These three are what convert a number into an agreement.
- Weight past contribution heavily. It is visible, measurable, and almost always small relative to what remains.
- Treat all contributions as commensurable by hours. A partner forgoing a salary and one working evenings are not in the same unit.
- Leave "what if one of us stops showing up" unaddressed because it seems distrustful. It is the most likely failure mode, and addressing it is what makes the partnership survivable.
- Assume shared expectations about control, exit horizon, or how much money is enough. These are the silent divergences that surface as equity disputes.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Name why this is hard, then proceed
Say it aloud to each other: this conversation is uncomfortable because it prices a relationship, and both of you would rather assume goodwill. Naming that reduces its force considerably. Then set the frame explicitly: **we are doing this now precisely because we get on** — the terms are for a future in which we might not, and agreeing them while we do is the cheapest this will ever be.

### Step 2 — Inventory past contribution
List what each partner has already contributed: time, capital, IP, customer relationships, reputation, prior work. Quantify what can be quantified. Then apply the discipline: for most ventures, past contribution is a small fraction of total contribution over the life of the arrangement. Record it, weight it modestly, and resist the pull of its visibility.

### Step 3 — Specify future commitment concretely
This is the decisive input and it is where vagueness concentrates. For each partner: full or part time, for how long, doing what specifically, and what they are giving up to do it. Get the uncomfortable specifics — a partner who intends to keep a job for six months, or who will step back when a child arrives, or who plans to exit in three years, should say so now. Almost every equity dispute traces to a divergence between stated and actual future commitment.

### Step 4 — Price the asymmetries separately
Four dimensions, not commensurable by hours:

| Dimension | Question | Weighting note |
|---|---|---|
| **Salary forgone** | Who is unpaid or underpaid, and by how much? | A real, quantifiable investment |
| **Capital at risk** | Who put money in, and could they lose it? | Distinct from time; usually the largest asymmetry |
| **Personal guarantee** | Who signed personally for a lease, loan, or liability? | Uncapped downside, often unpriced |
| **Opportunity cost** | What did each turn down? | Real but easy to inflate; require specifics |

Price each separately rather than converting everything to hours. A partner with a personal guarantee is exposed in a way no amount of time matches.

### Step 5 — Set vesting before discussing the number
Vesting is the mechanism that makes any split survivable, and it must be agreed before the percentages, or it becomes a concession rather than a structure. Standard shape: four years with a one-year cliff, applied **to everyone including the founder who was there first**. Then handle acceleration explicitly — on acquisition, on termination without cause — since unaddressed acceleration is a common late-stage dispute.

### Step 6 — Define departure terms
Four scenarios, each answered before any number is agreed:
- **Voluntary departure:** what happens to vested and unvested equity?
- **Removal for cause:** who decides, on what definition, and what is forfeited?
- **Stopping contributing without leaving:** the hardest and most common case — the partner who is still nominally in but has disengaged. What triggers a review, and who initiates it?
- **Death or incapacity:** does equity pass to an estate, and is there a buyback right?

The third is the one most likely to occur and least likely to be addressed, precisely because naming it feels like an accusation.

### Step 7 — Establish the deadlock rule
Two partners at 50/50 with no tiebreaker cannot resolve a disagreement, and the arrangement stalls at exactly the moment a decision matters. Options: a small equity asymmetry (51/49) with named domains of final authority; a designated tiebreaker for defined categories; an independent third party or board seat; or a buy-sell provision that lets either partner trigger a purchase at a valuation the other sets. Choose one and write it down. Deciding this while you agree is trivial; deciding it while you disagree is impossible by definition.

### Step 8 — Surface expectations and document
Before finalizing, surface the divergences that cause later disputes even when the equity was fair: expected exit horizon, how much money is enough, appetite for outside investment, whether either intends to draw a salary, how decisions get made day to day, and what each considers full commitment. Then document everything — the split, vesting, departure terms, deadlock rule, and the reasoning. The reasoning matters as much as the numbers, because it is what a future disagreement will be adjudicated against.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If one of you stopped contributing in month seven, what exactly happens — and is it written down?
- Are you agreeing to a number to end the discomfort of the conversation?
- What expectation are you each holding that has not been said aloud?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Avoidance-driven equality.** Splitting evenly to end the conversation. Equal may be the right answer, but reaching it by avoidance means no reasoning exists to defend it when contribution later diverges — which it will.
2. **Vesting omission.** Agreeing percentages with no vesting. Equity then belongs fully to whoever was present at the start, regardless of what follows, which is the most expensive single omission in partnership agreements.
3. **Past-contribution over-weighting.** Anchoring on work already done because it is visible and measurable. It is almost always small relative to the work remaining, and over-weighting it systematically disadvantages whoever will do more later.
4. **Hour-based commensuration.** Converting capital at risk, forgone salary, and personal guarantees into time-equivalents. They are different kinds of exposure and one of them is uncapped.
5. **The unaddressed disengagement case.** Not defining what happens when a partner stops contributing but does not leave. It is the most probable failure mode, and it goes unaddressed because raising it feels like an accusation about the person in front of you.
6. **Deadlock blindness.** A 50/50 split with no tiebreaker. It functions perfectly until the first genuine disagreement, at which point the structure guarantees paralysis.
7. **Assumed shared expectations.** Not surfacing exit horizon, money-is-enough thresholds, or investment appetite. These diverge silently and later present as equity grievances, because equity is the only lever available to express them.
8. **Undocumented agreement.** Relying on a shared understanding. Both partners will remember it sincerely and differently, and there is no record to arbitrate between two honest recollections.

- [ ] Future commitment specified concretely, including uncomfortable specifics.

- [ ] Vesting agreed before percentages, applying to all partners including the first-in founder.
- [ ] Acceleration addressed for both acquisition and termination without cause.
- [ ] All four departure scenarios answered, including stops-contributing-without-leaving.
- [ ] Deadlock rule chosen and specified.
- [ ] Expectations surfaced on exit horizon, sufficiency, investment, and salary.
- [ ] Split documented together with the reasoning behind it.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests the month-seven disengagement case against written terms.
- [ ] No number agreed before vesting, departure terms, and deadlock rule.
