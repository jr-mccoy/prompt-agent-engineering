# Severance Package Negotiation — What the Release Is Worth and What Else Is on the Table

**Objective:** A severance offer arrives at the worst possible moment — often in the same meeting as the news, with a signature deadline attached — and it is designed to feel final. It frequently is not. In many cases the employer is **buying something**: a release of potential legal claims, confidentiality, cooperation during transition, and a clean exit without dispute. What they are paying for it is a judgment, and it can move. More often still, the **terms around the amount** can move when the amount cannot: how long benefits continue, how equity and bonuses are treated, what the reference and announcement say, whether a non-compete is narrowed or waived, the exit date, and outplacement support.

This prompt maps those terms, uses the **review period** rather than signing under the pressure of the moment, and builds a counter that asks for what matters most to you. It is the employee's non-lawyer preparation. The **release itself is a legal document** — what claims it waives, what the review and revocation periods are, what restrictive covenants survive — and it belongs with an employment lawyer. `domain-legal/employment-labor/legal_employment_offer_and_separation_package.md` is the attorney-side review; use this prompt to decide what to ask for and that one (or a lawyer) to understand what you are signing.

The general machinery lives upstream: `preparation/negotiation_leverage_audit.md` for what you hold beyond your alternatives, and `preparation/negotiation_package_trade_design.md` for trading terms of different value to each side.

**When to use:**
- You have been offered a severance or separation package.
- You expect a layoff or negotiated exit and want to prepare.
- The employer says the package is standard and non-negotiable, and you want to test that.
- You care about one term — equity, benefits, a reference, a non-compete — more than the amount.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need to understand the release, the claims it waives, or its legal enforceability — an employment lawyer, via `domain-legal/employment-labor/`.
- You believe you were dismissed for a discriminatory or retaliatory reason — `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/workplace/` and a lawyer before negotiating.
- You are the employer designing a package — this is the employee side.
- You are negotiating a new job offer — `contexts/negotiation_salary_raise_promotion.md`.

**Audience:** Employees and executives who have been offered, or expect, a severance package.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The offer.** Amount, benefits, equity and bonus treatment, restrictive covenants, reference or announcement terms, exit date, and signing deadline.
2. **Your contract and plans.** Employment contract, equity plan documents, bonus plan, and any policy that sets a standard package.
3. **The circumstances.** Layoff, restructuring, performance, or negotiated exit — and anything that could suggest a legal claim, which goes to a lawyer first.
4. **Your priorities.** Which terms matter most: cash, health cover, equity, a clean reference, freedom to work for a competitor, time.
5. **Your alternatives.** Job-search prospects, savings runway, and how long you could manage without income. Tag estimates `known / inferred / guessed`.
6. **The counterpart.** Who communicated the offer, who has authority to change it, and how the organization has treated others who left.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Identify **what the employer is buying** — release, confidentiality, cooperation, a clean exit — and treat those as the source of your leverage.
- Map **every term that can move**, not only the amount.
- **Steelman the employer**: packages are often set by policy for consistency, budgets may be fixed, and others in the same round may be treated identically.
- Use the **review period**; ask for more time in writing if the deadline is short, and confirm the actual review and revocation periods with a lawyer rather than from memory.
- Route the **release, waived claims, and restrictive covenants** to an employment lawyer before signing.
- Rank your priorities and ask for the **terms that matter most to you** — often cheaper for the employer than cash.
- Keep all communication **professional and in writing**; you may need a reference from these people.
- Make a **specific counter** with a one-sentence basis for each item.

### Must Not
- Sign in the meeting, or before the review period is used and the release is understood.
- Threaten litigation you do not intend to bring or have no basis for; if you believe you have a claim, speak to a lawyer first.
- Disparage the employer or colleagues, in writing or elsewhere, during negotiation.
- Misrepresent competing offers, your job prospects, or your circumstances.
- Assume "standard package" means nothing can move — ask about non-cash terms.
- State legal deadlines, entitlements, or enforceability from memory.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Slow it down
Do not sign or commit in the meeting. Thank them, confirm you will review, and ask for the documents and the deadline in writing. If the deadline is short, request more time in writing.

### Step 2 — Screen for legal issues first
If anything about the circumstances suggests discrimination, retaliation, unpaid wages, or a contractual entitlement, speak to an employment lawyer before negotiating. That changes the negotiation entirely.

### Step 3 — Identify what they are buying
List what the employer gains by your signature: a release, confidentiality, non-disparagement, cooperation, a quiet departure. These are the reasons the package exists and the source of your room.

### Step 4 — Map every movable term
Amount; benefits continuation; bonus proration; equity vesting, acceleration, or extended exercise window; unused leave; exit date or garden leave; reference and announcement wording; non-compete narrowing or waiver; outplacement; equipment. Mark each: in the offer, absent, or improvable.

### Step 5 — Rank your priorities
Decide which terms matter most to you and which are likely cheap for them. A reference wording, a later exit date, or an extended exercise window often costs the employer little and matters a great deal to you.

### Step 6 — Assess your alternatives honestly
Estimate your job-search runway and financial runway, tagged. This sets how hard you can push and on which terms — health cover may matter more than cash if the runway is long.

### Step 7 — Build the counter
Write specific asks for your top priorities, each with a one-sentence basis — tenure, contribution, transition help, market conditions for your role. Keep it to the items that matter; a long list reads as a fishing expedition.

### Step 8 — Deliver and close in writing
Send the counter in writing, professionally. Expect a partial response; prioritize accordingly. Before signing the final version, have a lawyer review the release and covenants.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If the employer reads your counter as the prelude to a dispute, what in it caused that, and is it necessary?
- Which of your asks would you drop first, and does the counter make that easy for them?
- Have you had the release reviewed, or are you relying on what it seems to say?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Final by presentation.** Treating a package as non-negotiable because it arrived with a deadline and a confident delivery.
2. **Amount-only negotiation.** Negotiating cash while ignoring benefits, equity, reference, covenants, and timing, which are often easier to move.
3. **Signing under pressure.** Committing in the meeting or within days, before using the review period.
4. **Release unread.** Signing without understanding which claims are waived and which covenants survive.
5. **Bluffed litigation.** Implying a lawsuit you will not bring, which hardens the counterpart and can damage your reference.
6. **Burned bridges.** Hostile or disparaging communication that costs the reference you may need more than the extra weeks of pay.
7. **Laundry-list counters.** Asking for everything, which obscures what matters and invites a blanket no.
8. **Policy case ignored.** Refusing to accept that some terms are genuinely fixed for consistency, which spends goodwill on the terms least likely to move.
