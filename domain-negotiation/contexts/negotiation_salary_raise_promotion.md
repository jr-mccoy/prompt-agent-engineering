# Salary, Raise, and Promotion Negotiation — Evidence, the Ask, and the Budget Response

**Objective:** Compensation negotiation differs from commercial negotiation in ways that change the tactics. Your counterpart is usually **not your adversary** — a manager advocating for you internally is closer to an ally with constraints, and treating them as an opponent forfeits the advocacy you need. The real decision often happens **elsewhere and earlier**, in a budget or calibration cycle you are not in, which makes timing more consequential than argument. The **relationship continues** at close range regardless of outcome. And the **bands are frequently real** — the most common honest answer is that this specific number cannot move this quarter, which is a different problem from a counterpart who does not want to move. This prompt handles the specifics: contribution and market evidence that survives scrutiny, the ask itself, timing against the cycles that actually govern, the response to "there's no budget," and the non-salary dimensions that are genuinely available when cash is fixed.

The general machinery lives upstream — `preparation/negotiation_batna_analysis.md` for your floor and alternatives, `preparation/negotiation_opening_offer_design.md` for anchoring. If you are evaluating whether to accept an offer rather than negotiate it, that is `domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_career_offer_evaluation.md`.

**When to use:**
- Preparing to ask for a raise, a promotion, or a band change.
- Negotiating a starting salary or an offer's total package.
- You asked and were told there was no budget, and need the next move.
- A promotion has been agreed in principle with no compensation change attached.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are deciding whether to accept an offer at all — `personal_career_offer_evaluation.md`.
- You want live practice rather than preparation — `domain-conversation-practice/conversation_practice_simulator.md` (Preset B is this scenario).
- The issue is discrimination or a legal entitlement rather than a negotiation — route to `domain-legal/employment-labor/`.

**Audience:** Individuals at any level negotiating their own compensation, and managers preparing to advocate for a team member's.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Current and target compensation.** Base, variable, equity, and what you are asking for.
2. **Contribution evidence.** Specific outcomes with dates and, where possible, numbers.
3. **Market evidence.** Range data for the role, level, and location, with sources.
4. **Cycle timing.** When budgets are set, when calibration happens, when reviews land.
5. **Your alternatives.** Whether you have or could get another offer, and whether you would use it.
6. **The relationship.** Your manager's own constraints and how much latitude they actually hold.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Distinguish **contribution evidence** (what you delivered) from **market evidence** (what the role pays). Both are needed; they do different work with different audiences.
- Time the ask against the **cycle that actually governs** — budget setting and calibration, not the review conversation, which is usually downstream of a decision already made.
- Make a **specific number ask**, not a range and not a feeling. Ranges concede their lower bound instantly.
- Treat the manager as a **constrained advocate**, and give them what they need to make the case upward.
- Prepare the **"no budget" response**, which is the modal answer and is frequently true rather than tactical.
- Identify the **non-salary dimensions** genuinely available in this organization when cash is fixed.
- Know your **alternative honestly**, and decide in advance whether you would actually use it.

### Must Not
- Base the ask on personal need — rent, cost of living, a mortgage. It is sincere, it is not persuasive, and it cannot be taken upward by anyone.
- Threaten to leave unless you would. It is the strongest move available and it is single-use; a called bluff ends the leverage and often the relationship.
- Ask in the review meeting and expect a decision. By then the budget is usually allocated; the review conversation reports an outcome rather than sets one.
- Treat "no budget" as automatically tactical. Bands, cycles, and headcount envelopes are real constraints, and treating a real one as a bluff damages an ally.
- Accept a promotion with no compensation change without a written date for the review of that.
- Bring a competing offer you would not accept. If discovered — and it is often testable — it ends your credibility permanently.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Assemble contribution evidence
List specific outcomes with dates and numbers: what you delivered, what changed because of it, what scope you hold now that you did not before. Scope growth is the strongest single argument for a band change, because bands are defined by scope rather than by performance. Strip anything that is effort rather than outcome — hours worked and difficulty endured are invisible to a compensation process.

### Step 2 — Assemble market evidence
Gather range data for this role, level, and location, with sources you can name. Then position yourself within the range honestly, including the reasons someone might place you lower — a short tenure, a partial year, a scope that is broad but shallow. An honest self-placement is more persuasive than a maximal one, because it survives the scrutiny it will receive.

### Step 3 — Map the cycle
Establish when compensation is actually decided in this organization: when budgets are set, when calibration happens, when increases take effect, and how far ahead of the review conversation the decision is effectively locked. Then work backward. The ask should land **before** the decision, which is frequently one to two quarters ahead of the review meeting where people typically raise it. If you have missed the window, say so explicitly and plan for the next one rather than spending the ask on a month where nothing can move.

### Step 4 — Set the number and the basis
Name a specific figure. Derive it from the market evidence and your position in the range, not from your current salary plus a percentage — anchoring on your current number imports whatever error is already in it. Write the one-sentence basis you will say aloud: "Based on the range for this level and the scope I've picked up since [date], I'm asking for X."

### Step 5 — Equip the advocate
Your manager will usually have to make this case to someone else — their manager, HR, a compensation partner, a calibration group — in a room you are not in, possibly weeks later, from memory or a short note. Give them the artifact: a few lines they can forward, with your outcomes and the market basis. This single step distinguishes asks that get made properly from asks that get paraphrased into nothing. Ask directly what would help them make the case.

### Step 6 — Prepare the "no budget" response
Assume it. The response has three parts, in order:
1. **Test the frame, without challenging honesty:** "I understand this cycle is set. What would need to be true for this to move at the next one?"
2. **Convert to a commitment:** ask for a specific date, a specific number, and what evidence would be required — then get it in writing, however informally.
3. **Widen the dimensions:** ask what is available that is not base salary.

Do not accept "let's revisit later" without a date attached. An undated revisit is a decline stated politely, and it recurs indefinitely.

### Step 7 — Identify the available non-salary dimensions
When cash genuinely cannot move, establish what can: title and level (which affects future bands more than a one-time increase does), scope and reporting line, a one-off bonus that sits outside the salary envelope, equity refresh, learning or conference budget, flexibility and location, protected time, a defined path with named criteria. Rank these by what you actually value — and note that title/level changes usually compound where a bonus does not.

### Step 8 — Assess your alternative honestly and decide about the offer
Write what you would actually do if the answer is no, in full: stay and re-ask next cycle, look, or leave. Then decide about competing offers. A genuine offer is the strongest evidence available, and it is single-use and risky — some organizations respond by re-anchoring on retention, some by writing you off as departing. Only bring one you would accept. Never bring one you do not have.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If your manager had to argue this to their manager without you present, would your evidence be enough?
- Are you asking in a month when this can move, or in the month when you happen to feel ready?
- What would you actually do if the answer is a clear no?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Need-based justification.** Grounding the ask in personal costs. It is sincere and completely unusable — nobody can take "their rent went up" into a calibration conversation. Only contribution and market travel upward.
2. **Effort as evidence.** Citing hours, difficulty, or dedication. Compensation processes evaluate scope and outcome; effort is invisible to them and reads as an argument from fairness rather than from value.
3. **Review-meeting timing.** Raising it in the review conversation and expecting movement. The budget is usually already allocated; the review reports a decision made earlier by people who were not asked this question.
4. **Range asks.** "Somewhere in the mid-nineties." The lower bound is heard and conceded before any discussion begins.
5. **Current-salary anchoring.** Deriving the ask as a percentage of what you currently earn. It imports any existing under-payment into the new number and compounds it.
6. **Bluffed offers.** Implying a competing offer that does not exist, or one you would refuse. It is frequently testable, and discovery ends both the negotiation and your credibility for the remainder of your tenure.
7. **Undated revisits.** Accepting "let's look at this again later" with no date, number, or criteria. It is a polite decline that recurs indefinitely and gets harder to raise each time.
8. **Adversarial framing of the manager.** Treating the person who has to advocate for you as the opponent. They usually have real constraints and real latitude; making them defensive costs the advocacy that actually determines the outcome.

## Verification

- [ ] Contribution evidence is outcome-based with dates; effort-based items removed.
- [ ] Market evidence has named sources and an honest self-placement including counter-reasons.
- [ ] Compensation cycle mapped, with the ask timed before the decision locks.
- [ ] A specific number asked, derived from market range and scope rather than current salary.
- [ ] One-sentence basis written for delivery aloud.
- [ ] Advocate pack prepared in forwardable form, with a direct question about what would help.
- [ ] "No budget" response prepared in three parts, requiring a date, number, and criteria.
- [ ] No undated revisit accepted.
- [ ] Non-salary dimensions identified and marked for whether they compound.
- [ ] Alternative decided honestly in advance of asking.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests whether the evidence survives being argued in your absence.
- [ ] No ask based on personal need.
- [ ] No competing offer referenced that does not exist or would not be accepted.
