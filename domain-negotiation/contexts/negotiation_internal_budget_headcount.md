# Internal Resource Negotiation — Budget and Headcount Against Peers With Equal Claim

**Objective:** Internal resource negotiation has a specific structure that most people negotiate against rather than with. The pool is genuinely **fixed**, so this is distributive in a way most commercial negotiation is not — your gain is a named colleague's loss. The **decision-maker is not the counterpart**: you are nominally negotiating with peers, but allocating is done by someone above all of you, on criteria that are frequently unstated. The **relationship persists** at close range with everyone who loses. And the currency is **organizational priority, not need** — every team needs more, need is uninformative as a discriminator, and an argument built on it cannot be used by anyone. This prompt builds the case in the currency that allocates, finds the trades peers will actually make, times the ask to the cycle, and protects the working relationships that outlast the allocation.

`domain-personal-development/prompts/stakeholder/stakeholder_navigation_guide.md` covers organizational influence generally; this is the specific case of competing for a fixed resource.

**When to use:**
- Preparing for a budget or headcount round.
- Competing with peer teams for a fixed pool of engineering, design, or analyst capacity.
- Your allocation has been cut and you are deciding how to respond.
- You need capacity from a team that does not report to you.

**When NOT to use:**
- The negotiation is with an external party — use the rest of the domain.
- You are the allocator rather than a claimant — that is a prioritization decision; see `domain-decision-making/`.
- The issue is your own compensation — `contexts/negotiation_salary_raise_promotion.md`.

**Audience:** Managers, project leads, executives, and individuals competing for internal resources.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **What you are asking for.** Budget, headcount, or specific capacity, quantified.
2. **What it enables.** The outcome, expressed in the organization's own terms.
3. **The pool and the claimants.** Total available, and who else is asking.
4. **The allocator.** Who decides, and what they are measured on.
5. **The cycle.** When allocation happens and when it effectively locks.
6. **Relationships.** Your working relationships with the peers you are competing against.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Build the case in the **allocator's currency** — their objectives, their metrics, their risks — not your team's need.
- Quantify the **cost of not doing it**, which is usually more persuasive than the benefit of doing it and is what survives a cut list.
- Map the **other claimants** and what they are asking for, since the allocator is choosing among you and will do so comparatively whether or not you acknowledge it.
- Find **trades peers will make** — sequencing, sharing, partial allocation, or a swap across cycles.
- Time to the **allocation cycle**, which locks well before the meeting where it is announced.
- Protect the **relationship with peers who lose**, because you will need them next quarter.
- Offer a **partial version** — the ask that survives a cut is the one that was designed to be cut.

### Must Not
- Argue from your team's need. Every team needs more; need does not discriminate between claims and gives the allocator nothing to decide on.
- Compete by diminishing a peer's case. It is visible, it is remembered, and it costs more in future cooperation than the resource is worth.
- Ask for the maximum and expect to negotiate down. Internal allocation frequently cuts rather than negotiates, and an inflated ask gets cut proportionally with everyone else's.
- Wait for the allocation meeting to make the case. The decision is usually substantially formed before it.
- Treat the peer as the counterpart. They are a competing claimant; the decision-maker is elsewhere, and the actual negotiation is with them.
- Accept a cut without establishing what is being traded away, in writing.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Translate the ask into the allocator's currency
Write what you are asking for and what it enables — expressed in the terms the allocator is measured on. Revenue, risk, cost, a committed deliverable, a regulatory obligation, a strategic priority they have named publicly. "The team is stretched" is invisible to an allocation process; "this is the constraint on the Q3 commitment you've already made externally" is not.

### Step 2 — Quantify the cost of not doing it
Usually more persuasive than the benefit of doing it, because it survives a cut list where upside does not. What breaks, slips, or costs more later? What risk materializes? What has already slipped for this reason? Use evidence from what has actually happened rather than projection — the strongest version of this argument is a thing that has already gone wrong once.

### Step 3 — Map the other claimants
List who else is asking, for what, and on what basis. The allocator is choosing among you comparatively, so understanding the field is understanding the decision. Note where another claim is **stronger** than yours — that is useful information, and pretending otherwise produces an ask that is misaligned with the outcome. Where a peer's claim is genuinely stronger, the productive move is often to support it and position your own for the next cycle.

### Step 4 — Find the trades
Peers frequently trade, and internal trades are cheaper than the equivalent external ones because the relationship value is high and the resource is fungible across time. Options: **sequencing** (you take Q3, they take Q4); **sharing** a resource across both needs; **partial allocation** with a defined trigger for the rest; **swaps** across resource types (you take the headcount, they take the budget); or **joint asks** where two claims served by one allocation are far more efficient than two competing ones. Talk to peers before the allocation, not after.

### Step 5 — Time to the cycle
Establish when allocation is actually decided — which is usually well before the meeting where it is communicated. Then make the case before it locks, to the allocator directly, in a form they can use. A case made in the allocation meeting is a case made after the decision, and the meeting is frequently a ratification.

### Step 6 — Design the ask to be cut
Internal allocation cuts more often than it negotiates. So structure the ask in tiers: **full** (what you actually need), **reduced** (what still delivers a coherent outcome), and **minimum** (below which the thing should not be started at all). Name the minimum explicitly — it protects against the worst internal outcome, which is a half-allocation that guarantees failure while consuming the resource. Say plainly: "below X, I'd rather we didn't start this."

### Step 7 — Protect the peer relationships
Two rules. **Never argue against a peer's claim** — argue for yours. Diminishing a colleague's case is visible to the allocator, who will discount you for it, and it is remembered by the colleague long after the allocation. **Tell peers what you are asking for** before the meeting. Surprise creates enemies; transparency frequently produces the trades in Step 4, and costs nothing since the allocator will see both claims anyway.

### Step 8 — Handle a cut properly
If you are cut, establish in writing what is being traded away: which commitment slips, which risk is accepted, which deliverable is descoped. This is not a protest; it is the allocator's decision made explicit, and it serves everyone. It also means the consequence, when it arrives, is a known accepted trade-off rather than a delivery failure attributed to you.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If you were the allocator with this pool and these claims, which would you fund — and is it yours?
- Are you arguing for your ask, or against someone else's?
- What will you have given up in peer goodwill, and is the resource worth it?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Need-based argument.** "My team is stretched." Every team is; need does not discriminate among claims and gives the allocator no basis for a decision. Translate into their currency or the ask does not compete.
2. **Peer attack.** Making your case by weakening a colleague's. The allocator sees it and discounts you for it, and the colleague remembers it well past the cycle in which you won.
3. **Inflated asks.** Padding the request expecting to negotiate down. Internal allocation cuts proportionally rather than negotiating, so padding produces a proportionally larger cut and a reputation for padding.
4. **Meeting-timed advocacy.** Making the case in the allocation meeting. The decision is usually substantially formed beforehand, and the meeting communicates it.
5. **Wrong counterpart.** Negotiating with the peer instead of the allocator. The peer cannot give you the resource; they can only trade with you, which is a different and more limited conversation.
6. **Undefined minimum.** Accepting whatever is allocated without a floor. Half-allocations that guarantee failure while consuming the resource are the worst internal outcome and are entirely avoidable by naming the minimum.
7. **Silent cuts.** Accepting a reduction without recording what is traded away. The consequence later presents as your delivery failure rather than as an accepted organizational trade-off.
8. **Surprise asks.** Peers discovering your claim in the room. It creates opposition where a prior conversation would frequently have produced a trade, at no cost, since both claims were going to be visible anyway.

## Verification

- [ ] Ask translated into the allocator's metrics, not team need.
- [ ] Cost of not doing it quantified, with evidence from what has already happened.
- [ ] Other claimants mapped, including honest assessment of stronger claims.
- [ ] Trades explored with peers before the allocation, not after.
- [ ] Cycle mapped and the case made before the decision locks.
- [ ] Ask tiered full / reduced / minimum, with the minimum stated explicitly to the allocator.
- [ ] Peers informed of your ask in advance.
- [ ] No argument made against another claimant's case.
- [ ] Any cut recorded in writing with the specific trade-off named.
- [ ] Adversarial check applies the would-I-fund-this test from the allocator's seat.
- [ ] No inflated ask expecting proportional negotiation.
- [ ] No case first made in the allocation meeting.
