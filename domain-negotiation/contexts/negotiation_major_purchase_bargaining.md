# Major Purchase Bargaining — Vehicles, Property, and One-Off Buys Against a Repeat Seller

**Objective:** This is the most asymmetric negotiation most people ever have. You buy a car every seven years; the person opposite sells several a week and has a scripted response to everything you are likely to say. They know the actual cost basis, the discount authority, the margin in each component, and the point at which you have invested enough time to stop shopping. You know the advertised price. This prompt closes as much of that gap as is closeable. It front-loads **research**, because information obtained beforehand is worth far more than anything won in the room. It insists on negotiating **the total**, since the monthly payment and the headline are both structures for concealing it. It prepares for the **second stage** — finance, warranty, protection, fees — where the margin frequently exceeds anything conceded on the price. And it makes the **walk-away real**, because for a one-time buyer against a repeat seller it is the only genuine lever there is.

`domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_major_purchase_research.md` decides what to buy and whether to buy. This negotiates the price once you have decided.

**When to use:**
- Buying a vehicle, property, or another large one-off item from a professional seller.
- You have decided what to buy and are about to discuss price.
- You are mid-purchase and the conversation has moved to finance or extras.
- A deposit has been taken and pressure is being applied.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are still deciding what or whether to buy — `personal_major_purchase_research.md`.
- The transaction is between private individuals, where the asymmetry is much smaller and the tone should differ.
- The purchase is a commercial procurement — `contexts/negotiation_vendor_procurement_buyside.md`.

**Audience:** Individuals making a large personal purchase from a professional seller.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **What you are buying.** Specific item, specification, and condition.
2. **Research done.** Transaction prices actually paid, not advertised prices.
3. **Your total budget.** All-in, including everything.
4. **Alternatives.** Other sellers, other items, waiting, and not buying.
5. **Timing.** Yours, and any seasonal or period-end pressure on theirs.
6. **Financing.** Whether you need it and whether it is arranged independently.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Research **transaction prices**, not asking prices. What sellers ask is a starting position; what buyers actually paid is the range.
- Negotiate the **total, all-in figure**. Monthly payment, trade-in, and headline price are separable levers a seller can move against each other.
- Arrange **financing independently first**, then treat any seller financing as a competing offer to be beaten rather than as a service.
- Prepare for the **second stage** — extras, warranties, protection products, fees — and set a decision rule for it before arriving.
- Keep the **walk-away real and available**, which requires not being emotionally committed and not having travelled too far to leave.
- Separate the components — item price, trade-in, financing, extras — and negotiate each **on its own**, since bundling is how movement in one is funded by the others.
- Set the **total ceiling in advance**, in writing, and treat it as fixed.

### Must Not
- Negotiate the monthly payment. It is the most effective concealment device available: term length, rate, and deposit can absorb almost any price.
- Disclose your budget, your timeline, or how much you like the item. Each is directly convertible into price.
- Let a trade-in be bundled with the purchase price. Bundled, an apparent gain on one is funded by a loss on the other, and the total is unexamined.
- Buy extras under time pressure at the point of sale. Warranties and protection products carry high margin and are sold when your resistance is lowest.
- Treat a deposit as a commitment to complete. It is refundable far more often than the pressure applied implies, and if it is not, that is information you should have had before paying it.
- Assume the advertised price is the starting point. It is a marketing figure and frequently unrelated to the transaction range.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Establish the real range
Find what people actually paid — completed sales, price guides, comparable listings that have sold rather than listings that are live, and forums or communities where transaction prices are shared. The gap between asking and transaction prices is the single most valuable piece of information in this negotiation, and it is obtained entirely before any conversation. Write the range, and where in it you intend to land.

### Step 2 — Set the total ceiling in writing
One number: the maximum all-in figure including every fee, tax, delivery charge, and add-on. Write it down before any contact. This is the reservation point, and its function is to survive a moment several hours from now when you are tired, invested, and being told this is a good deal. Write it somewhere you will actually look at it.

### Step 3 — Arrange financing independently
Get a rate from a bank or credit union before speaking to the seller. This does two things: it gives you a number to beat, and it removes the seller's ability to move value between price and finance. If they can beat your independent rate, take theirs — that is a genuine win. If they cannot, you have already arranged it, and the finance stage becomes a short conversation rather than a second negotiation.

### Step 4 — Separate the components
State early and hold: item price first, alone. Then trade-in, separately. Then financing, separately. Then extras, separately. This is the single most valuable structural move available to a buyer, because bundled negotiation is how an apparent concession on one component is funded by another. If asked "what monthly payment are you looking for," the response is: *"I'd rather agree the total price first, then we can talk about how it's paid."*

### Step 5 — Anchor on the research and stay quiet
Open with a number grounded in transaction data, stated plainly: *"Based on what these have been selling for, I'm at X." *Then stop. Do not justify further, do not explain your circumstances, and do not fill the silence. The pause is the most reliable tool a buyer has, and it is the one most consistently given away.

### Step 6 — Handle the standard moves
- **"Let me check with my manager."** Standard, and it functions as both a real approval step and a pressure device. Response: wait. Do not improve your offer while they are away, and do not treat the return with a small improvement as requiring reciprocity.
- **"What would it take to get you to buy today?"** Do not answer with a number. *"I'm not deciding today."* This removes the deadline they are constructing, which is the tactic's entire purpose.
- **"This price is only good today."** Let it expire. Prices in this market do not vanish, and a genuinely time-limited offer is rare enough to be worth losing occasionally. See `at-the-table/negotiation_hard_bargainer_defense.md`.
- **"We're already losing money on this."** No response required. It is a script.

### Step 7 — Prepare for the second stage
The extras and finance stage frequently carries more margin than the item price, and it arrives when you have already committed emotionally, invested hours, and relaxed because the negotiation felt finished. Set the rule now, in advance: **buy no add-on product at the point of sale.** Extended warranties, paint and fabric protection, gap insurance, and service plans can all be bought later, elsewhere, usually cheaper, after research. The correct sentence is prepared and short: *"I'm not adding anything today."*

### Step 8 — Keep the walk-away real
Two conditions make a walk-away credible to you rather than merely stated. **Do not become emotionally committed** — if you have named the car or pictured yourself in the house, the floor has already moved. **Do not let sunk time bind you** — hours invested are gone whether or not you buy, and the seller knows time invested predicts closing. If the total exceeds your ceiling, leave, and mean it. If you are wrong about the ceiling, you can return; sellers reliably take a returning buyer's call.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Do you know the actual transaction range, or only asking prices?
- If the total came in £500 over your ceiling, would you walk — honestly?
- Which stage have you not prepared for, and what will it cost you there?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Monthly-payment negotiation.** Discussing the payment rather than the total. Term, rate, and deposit can absorb almost any price, which is precisely why the conversation is steered there.
2. **Bundled components.** Letting price, trade-in, finance, and extras be negotiated together. An apparent gain on the trade-in funded by the item price is the oldest structure in this market, and it is invisible unless the components are separated.
3. **Asking-price anchoring.** Treating the advertised figure as the reference point. It is a marketing number; the transaction range is frequently well below it and is discoverable in advance.
4. **Second-stage unpreparedness.** Negotiating the item hard and then accepting extras and finance without resistance. This stage often carries the larger margin and arrives when your guard is down and the negotiation feels concluded.
5. **Budget disclosure.** Stating what you can afford, what you want to spend monthly, or when you need the item. Each converts directly into price.
6. **Emotional commitment.** Deciding you want this specific item before agreeing the price. The walk-away stops being available, and experienced sellers detect this early and price accordingly.
7. **Sunk-time binding.** Continuing because of hours already invested. Time spent is gone either way, and time invested is a well-known predictor of closing — which is why the process is long.
8. **Deposit-as-commitment.** Treating a paid deposit as binding you to complete. It is frequently refundable, the pressure applied implies otherwise, and its refundability is something to establish before paying rather than after.

## Verification

- [ ] Transaction prices researched, distinct from asking prices, with sources named.
- [ ] Total all-in ceiling written down before any contact with the seller.
- [ ] Financing arranged independently before negotiating.
- [ ] Component separation stated, with the monthly-payment deflection script prepared.
- [ ] Opening anchor grounded in transaction data, followed by silence.
- [ ] Responses prepared for manager-approval, buy-today, expiring-price, and losing-money moves.
- [ ] Second-stage rule set in advance: no add-on products at point of sale.
- [ ] Alternative purchase route for wanted add-ons identified.
- [ ] Walk-away conditions checked for emotional commitment and sunk-time binding.
- [ ] Deposit refundability established before any deposit is paid.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests the honest walk-away at a small overage.
- [ ] No monthly payment negotiated in place of the total.
- [ ] No budget, timeline, or enthusiasm disclosed.
