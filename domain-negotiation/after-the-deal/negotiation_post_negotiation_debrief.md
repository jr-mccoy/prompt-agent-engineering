# Post-Negotiation Debrief — What the Outcome and Their Behaviour Actually Revealed

**Objective:** Negotiation is one of the few professional skills where practice does not reliably produce improvement, because the feedback is slow, noisy, and easy to misread — a good outcome from luck is indistinguishable from a good outcome from skill unless someone separates them deliberately. This prompt does that separation. It compares the result against the **reservation point and target set beforehand**, not against how the result feels. It identifies which specific moves changed the number and which merely felt productive. It reads the counterpart's behaviour retrospectively, since their concessions at the end reveal what their real constraints were once concealment stopped paying. And it distinguishes **decision quality from outcome quality**, so that a good result from a bad process is recorded as luck rather than banked as skill.

This is the **deal** debrief. `difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_post_review.md` handles the relational debrief of a hard conversation, and `domain-psychology/client-self-use/relational/clientself_conflict_postmortem.md` handles interpersonal conflict — this one is about terms, moves, and calibration.

**When to use:**
- A negotiation has concluded, whether it closed or not.
- You want to know whether a good outcome was skill or circumstance.
- A negotiation went badly and you want the specific cause rather than a general feeling.
- You are building a personal pattern library and need structured inputs — see `craft/negotiation_pattern_library_builder.md`.

**When NOT to use:**
- The debrief needed is emotional or relational — `difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_post_review.md`.
- The negotiation is still live and you need a mid-course correction — `at-the-table/`.
- The deal closed and the question is implementation — `negotiation_implementation_and_relationship.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who want negotiating experience to compound rather than merely accumulate.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The negotiation and its outcome.** Final terms, or the fact of no deal.
2. **Your pre-negotiation numbers.** Reservation point, target, and opening — as set beforehand, not as remembered.
3. **The sequence.** What was offered, countered, and conceded, in order.
4. **Their behaviour.** What they pushed on, conceded, and how the pattern changed near the end.
5. **What you prepared.** Which prompts or preparation you actually ran.
6. **Time elapsed.** Whether this is immediate or retrospective — both are valid, and they surface different things.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Compare the outcome against the **pre-negotiation** reservation point and target, using the numbers as originally set. Retrospectively adjusted targets make every outcome a success.
- Separate **decision quality from outcome quality**. Score the process and the result independently; the four combinations mean different things and only one is repeatable.
- Identify **which specific moves moved the number**, distinguishing them from activity that felt productive but changed nothing.
- Read the counterpart's **end-game behaviour** for what it revealed about their true BATNA and constraints.
- Name **one change** to preparation or execution for next time — one, specific, and actionable. A list of six is a list nobody applies.
- Record what was **luck**, honestly, including when the outcome was good.
- Note whether the **preparation done was proportionate** — over-preparation is a real cost and rarely audited.

### Must Not
- Produce a highlight reel. A debrief that only surfaces what went well produces no change.
- Score the outcome against how it felt. Relief is not evidence of a good deal; discomfort is not evidence of a bad one.
- Adjust the target retrospectively to match the result. This is the single most common way negotiators avoid learning anything.
- Attribute the outcome entirely to skill or entirely to circumstance. Both are almost always present.
- Confuse a good outcome with a good process. A deal that closed above target after a badly run negotiation is a warning, not a template.
- Generate a change list so long that none of it is implemented.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Record the numbers as they were
Write the reservation point, target, and opening **as set before the negotiation**, then the actual outcome. Retrieve the original figures rather than recalling them; memory adjusts them toward the result. Compute the gaps: outcome vs. target, and outcome vs. reservation point.

### Step 2 — Score decision quality and outcome quality separately
Two independent scores, then the combination:

| | Good outcome | Bad outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Good process** | Repeatable — bank the method | Unlucky — keep the method |
| **Bad process** | Lucky — do not bank it | Instructive — change the method |

The top-right and bottom-left cells are where the learning is, and both are routinely misfiled — the unlucky good process gets abandoned, and the lucky bad process gets repeated until it fails.

### Step 3 — Trace what actually moved the number
Walk the sequence and mark each of your moves: **moved the number**, **held ground**, or **no effect**. Be strict — most moves have no effect, including many that felt significant at the time. Then check the reverse: which of *their* moves changed your position, and whether your response was planned or improvised. Improvised responses that worked are still improvisation, and they will not reliably work again.

### Step 4 — Read their end-game behaviour
Near the close, concealment stops paying and behaviour becomes more informative. What did they concede quickly at the end that they had defended for weeks? What did they refuse even when refusing risked the deal — that is a real constraint. How fast did they move once a deadline approached? Update your estimate of their true BATNA, and note where your earlier estimate was wrong and by how much. This is the highest-value section for future negotiations with the same counterpart.

### Step 5 — Audit the preparation
Which preparation did you actually do, and which of it was used? Preparation that was never referenced was cost without return. Equally, name what you needed and did not have — the question you could not answer, the benchmark you lacked, the authority you had not established. Then answer the proportionality question: was the prep tier right for this negotiation's stakes, per `preparation/negotiation_prep_depth_triage.md`?

### Step 6 — Name the luck
State explicitly what was outside your control and how it cut: a competing bidder appearing or not, a deadline moving, the counterpart's internal situation, who happened to be in the room. Naming luck is what protects you from banking it as skill — and this matters most when the outcome was good, which is exactly when nobody does it.

### Step 7 — Extract the single change
One change, specific enough to act on: not "prepare better" but "derive the reservation point in writing before the first call, because I entered with a range and defended the wrong end of it." Write the trigger — the point in the next negotiation where this change applies — so it is attached to a moment rather than to an intention.

### Step 8 — Log it for the pattern library
Record in a form that accumulates: situation type, counterpart type, what worked, what did not, calibration error on their BATNA. One debrief teaches little; twenty structured debriefs reveal your systematic biases, which is where the real improvement is. Feed `craft/negotiation_pattern_library_builder.md`.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If the outcome had been worse, which move would you now be blaming — and was it actually the cause?
- Are you crediting skill for something that turned on a fact you did not know at the time?
- What would the counterpart's debrief say about you?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Retrospective target adjustment.** Recalling the target as close to whatever was achieved. It is nearly universal, it is unconscious, and it makes every negotiation a success and every debrief worthless. Retrieve the original numbers.
2. **Highlight reel.** Cataloguing what went well. Comfortable, and it produces no change. The debrief's value is concentrated in what did not work and why.
3. **Feeling-based scoring.** Judging the deal by relief or discomfort. Relief tracks the end of stress, not the quality of terms; discomfort frequently accompanies a well-defended floor.
4. **Outcome-process conflation.** Treating a good result as evidence of a good process. The lucky-bad-process cell is where repeated failures are incubated, because the method gets banked and reused until conditions change.
5. **Move over-attribution.** Crediting a move that coincided with movement rather than caused it. Test each: would their concession have come anyway, given their deadline?
6. **Luck denial.** Omitting the external factors, especially after a good outcome. Unnamed luck becomes remembered skill, and remembered skill produces overconfidence in the next negotiation.
7. **Change-list inflation.** Producing eight improvements. None will be implemented, and the debrief becomes a ritual. One change, with a trigger attached, actually happens.
8. **Preparation under-audit.** Never asking whether the preparation was proportionate. Over-preparation is a genuine cost — hours spent, and sometimes deals lost to slowness — and it is almost never examined because it feels virtuous.

## Verification

- [ ] Reservation point and target retrieved as originally set, not recalled.
- [ ] Process quality and outcome quality scored independently, with the cell identified.
- [ ] The cell's implication stated — including "do not bank this" for lucky-bad-process.
- [ ] Every move classified moved / held / no effect, strictly.
- [ ] Responses to their moves marked planned or improvised.
- [ ] End-game behaviour read for their real constraints and true BATNA.
- [ ] Earlier BATNA estimate quantified as wrong-by-how-much.
- [ ] Preparation audited for both waste and gaps, with proportionality assessed.
- [ ] Luck named explicitly, especially where the outcome was good.
- [ ] Exactly one change extracted, with a trigger attached.
- [ ] Pattern library entry recorded in accumulating form.
- [ ] Adversarial check includes what the counterpart's debrief would say.
- [ ] No target adjusted retrospectively.
- [ ] No outcome scored by how it felt.
