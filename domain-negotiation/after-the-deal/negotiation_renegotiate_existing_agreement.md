# Renegotiating a Live Agreement — Reopen Terms Without Burning the Relationship

**Objective:** Reopening a live agreement is structurally different from negotiating a new one, and the difference is not the terms — it is that you are asking someone to give up something they already have. That triggers loss aversion, which is roughly twice as strong as the equivalent gain, and it raises a question that a fresh negotiation never does: *why should I?* The answer determines everything. A renegotiation grounded in a genuine change of circumstance is a legitimate business conversation. The same request with no such basis is opportunism, and it is filed as a fact about your reliability that outlasts the amendment. This prompt builds the case, prices the relationship cost honestly, tests amend-versus-exit before committing to either, sequences the approach so nobody is ambushed, and prepares for the predictable counter — that if you are reopening one term, everything is open.

Runs downstream of `negotiation_implementation_and_relationship.md`, which is where the need for a change usually surfaces.

**When to use:**
- Circumstances have changed and the current terms no longer work.
- You are considering asking for a change to an agreement in force.
- The counterpart has asked to reopen and you need to respond.
- A term is producing a result nobody intended when it was drafted.

**When NOT to use:**
- The agreement is at renewal, where terms are open by default — that is a new negotiation; use `preparation/`.
- The other side is in breach and the question is enforcement — that is legal; route to `domain-legal/`.
- Terms are workable and the discomfort is about the deal having been imperfect. Renegotiating a merely-disappointing deal spends relationship capital for very little.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who need to change an agreement already in force.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The agreement and the term at issue.** What exists, and what needs to change.
2. **What changed.** The circumstance that makes the current term unworkable, with dates and evidence.
3. **When it changed relative to signature.** Whether it was foreseeable at the time is the decisive question.
4. **The relationship state.** How the original negotiation went, and how implementation has gone since.
5. **Your alternatives.** What happens if they refuse — from `preparation/negotiation_batna_analysis.md`.
6. **What you can offer.** Terms you could improve in exchange.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Build the **change-of-circumstance case** with evidence and dates. The ask is legitimate to the extent that something genuinely changed after signature.
- Answer honestly whether the change was **foreseeable at signature**. If it was, you are asking for relief from your own bad forecasting — a real ask, but a different and weaker one.
- Price the **relationship cost** explicitly, including the effect on how reliable you are considered in future dealings.
- Test **amend versus exit versus absorb** before committing. Absorbing a bad term is sometimes cheaper than the reopening.
- Offer **reciprocity**. A renegotiation with nothing offered is a demand, and it is received as one.
- Sequence so the counterpart is **not ambushed** — a renegotiation sprung in a routine meeting produces a defensive answer that is then hard to move.
- Prepare for the **everything-is-open counter**, which is the standard and reasonable response.

### Must Not
- Reopen without a reason the counterpart would accept as fair. Opportunistic reopening is recorded as information about you and priced into every subsequent dealing.
- Use a minor breach as a pretext to reopen commercial terms. It is transparent, and it wastes a breach you may genuinely need.
- Threaten exit you would not execute. A bluff that is called ends both the renegotiation and the credibility of every future position.
- Ask for the change without knowing what you would do if refused. Refusal is a likely outcome and needs a plan.
- Assume the original negotiators are still there. New people on either side have no attachment to the original bargain and no memory of what was traded for what.
- Treat a renegotiation as a fresh negotiation. The starting point is an agreement that is currently binding, and behaving otherwise licenses them to do the same.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — State the term and the problem it is causing
Write the specific term, what it now produces, and the concrete cost of leaving it unchanged. Quantify where possible. "The pricing is uncomfortable" is not a case; "the volume tiers assumed 40% growth and we are at 4%, which puts unit cost 31% above the level the business case required" is.

### Step 2 — Build the change-of-circumstance case
Establish what changed, when, and with what evidence. Then answer the decisive question honestly: **was this foreseeable at signature?**

| Situation | Strength of ask |
|---|---|
| External, unforeseeable, post-signature | Strong — a legitimate business conversation |
| External, foreseeable but unpriced | Moderate — you mispriced a known risk |
| Internal to you, post-signature | Weak — your circumstances are not their problem |
| Nothing changed; the deal is just poor | None — this is opportunism |

Only the first two support a renegotiation framed as fairness. The others can still be asked, but they must be asked as a favour, priced as a favour, and reciprocated accordingly.

### Step 3 — Test amend, exit, and absorb
Three options, honestly compared. **Amend:** the cost of asking, including relationship. **Exit:** what termination or non-renewal actually costs, including switching and disruption. **Absorb:** the cost of living with the term to its end. Absorbing is systematically undervalued because the ongoing cost is visible while the relationship cost of reopening is not. Compute all three before choosing.

### Step 4 — Price the relationship cost
Name what reopening costs beyond the terms: their view of your reliability, their willingness to be flexible when *you* need something informally, their posture at renewal, and what they tell others. Then the asymmetry that matters most: **a counterpart who has seen you reopen an inconvenient agreement will price that into every future deal with you**, usually by requiring tighter terms. That cost is real and durable, and it is the one that renegotiation decisions systematically omit.

### Step 5 — Design the reciprocity
Decide what you offer for the change. Options: extend the term, accept a different constraint, improve a term they care about, offer a reference or a case study, accelerate payment, or expand scope elsewhere. Reciprocity does two things — it makes the ask a trade rather than a demand, and it gives the counterpart's negotiator something to take to their approver, who will otherwise see a pure giveaway and refuse.

### Step 6 — Sequence the approach
Do not spring it. The sequence that works: flag that there is something to discuss and book time for it; give them the context in advance so their first reaction is not in the room; make the ask with the case and the reciprocity together; then give them room to consult rather than pressing for an answer. Ambushed counterparts give defensive answers, and a defensive answer becomes a position they then have to defend.

### Step 7 — Prepare for "then everything is open"
This is the standard response and it is not unreasonable. Prepare three things. **The narrow frame:** "I'm asking about one term, and I'm offering X for it — I'm not looking to reopen the agreement." **Your defensive list:** the terms you most need to keep, in case it does widen. **The stop condition:** the point at which a widening renegotiation becomes worse than the original problem, at which you withdraw the ask cleanly and say so.

### Step 8 — Plan for refusal
Decide in advance what you do if they say no: absorb it, escalate, exit, or return later with a better case. Decide *before* asking, because deciding after a refusal is done under the influence of having been refused. If the answer is "absorb," ask whether the ask was worth its relationship cost at all — and if not, do not make it.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If they made this exact request of you, would you consider it reasonable?
- Are you reopening because something changed, or because you have realized you negotiated badly?
- If they say yes but remember it, what does that cost at renewal?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Opportunistic reopening.** Asking without a genuine change of circumstance. It is usually transparent, and it is filed as durable information about your reliability rather than as a one-off request.
2. **Foreseeability evasion.** Presenting a foreseeable risk that you failed to price as an unforeseeable change. Counterparts check timelines, and being caught reframing your own mispricing as their problem costs more than the term.
3. **Absorb under-weighting.** Failing to price living with the term. The ongoing cost is visible and the relationship cost of reopening is not, so the comparison is systematically biased toward reopening.
4. **Demand without reciprocity.** Asking for a change and offering nothing. It gives their negotiator nothing to take to an approver, which usually converts a possible yes into a structural no.
5. **Uncalled bluffs.** Threatening exit you will not execute. When called, you lose the renegotiation and the credibility of every future position with this counterpart.
6. **The ambush.** Raising it without warning in a routine meeting. The defensive first answer becomes a position they must then defend, which is much harder to move than an initial response given after consideration.
7. **Breach as pretext.** Using a minor implementation failure to justify reopening commercial terms. It is visible, it poisons the conversation, and it spends a breach you may genuinely need later.
8. **Personnel assumption.** Assuming the people who negotiated it are still in place. New counterparts have no attachment to the original bargain, no memory of what was traded for what, and no reason to honour an unwritten understanding.

## Verification

- [ ] The term's cost quantified rather than described.
- [ ] Change-of-circumstance case built with dates and evidence.
- [ ] Foreseeability at signature answered honestly, with the ask's strength graded.
- [ ] Amend, exit, and absorb all costed, including relationship cost.
- [ ] Relationship cost includes what they will require in future dealings.
- [ ] Reciprocity designed, with a note on why it works for their approver.
- [ ] Approach sequenced with advance context; no ambush.
- [ ] Everything-is-open response prepared with narrow frame, defensive list, and stop condition.
- [ ] Refusal response decided before the ask is made.
- [ ] Adversarial check applies the would-I-find-this-reasonable test.
- [ ] No exit threatened that would not be executed.
- [ ] No breach used as a pretext for commercial reopening.
