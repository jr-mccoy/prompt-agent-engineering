# Counter-Offer Message — Structure the Written Counter So It Advances Rather Than Resets

**Objective:** A written counter-offer does two jobs, and most do only one. The obvious job is to move the terms you want moved. The job that gets missed is to **ringfence what is already agreed**, so that each round narrows the open set instead of reopening everything. A counter that restates the entire position invites the counterpart to restate theirs, and the negotiation runs in place. This prompt structures the counter in four parts — acknowledge, accept-and-lock, counter-with-basis, close-with-a-step — and adds the property that matters most in written negotiation: the message must be **forwardable**. Your counterpart will paste it to their approver, and it needs to make your case in your absence, without you there to explain it.

This is the specific-message companion to `negotiation_written_async_message.md`, which governs the written channel generally.

**When to use:**
- You have received an offer or a redline and need to respond in writing.
- A written negotiation is looping, with the same terms reopened each round.
- Your counter must survive being forwarded to someone who has not seen the conversation.
- You need to move several terms at once without appearing to reject the whole proposal.

**When NOT to use:**
- The counter involves a significant concession best made live — check channel fit in `negotiation_written_async_message.md`.
- You are designing the first offer rather than responding — `preparation/negotiation_opening_offer_design.md`.
- The relationship has degraded and the next move is repair rather than terms.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals responding to an offer in writing.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Their offer.** The full proposal or redline, term by term.
2. **What you accept.** Terms you are prepared to lock now.
3. **What you counter.** Terms you need moved, with your position on each.
4. **Your basis.** The justification for each counter — benchmark, comparable, cost, precedent, policy.
5. **Your concession plan.** From `preparation/negotiation_concession_anchoring_plan.md`, so this round's movement is planned rather than improvised.
6. **Who will read it.** The recipient and whoever they forward it to.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- **Acknowledge their offer specifically** before countering — naming something concrete you appreciate, which costs nothing and materially changes reception.
- **Accept and lock** every term you are not contesting, stated explicitly as agreed. This is what makes each round narrow the open set.
- Attach a **basis to every counter**. An unjustified counter is a number to be split; a justified one is a position to be engaged.
- Counter **once per issue** with your actual position, not an inflated placeholder — written negotiation punishes visible padding because it is re-readable.
- Make the message **forwardable**: self-contained, professional, and persuasive to a reader who was not in the conversation.
- End with a **specific next step and a date**, so the message cannot be parked.
- Keep the **open-item list explicit**, so both sides can see the set shrinking.

### Must Not
- Restate the entire deal. It signals that nothing is settled and invites a full re-response.
- Counter every term because several need movement. Countering everything reads as rejection and produces a defensive full re-counter.
- Justify a counter with your own need ("this doesn't work for our budget"). Needs are not persuasive to an approver; standards are.
- Include a concession without its condition in the same sentence — the concession forwards, the condition does not.
- Apologize for countering. It signals the counter is soft and invites a re-counter to the original.
- Leave the accepted terms implicit. Silence on a term is not agreement in writing, and it will be reopened.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Sort their offer term by term
Three columns: **accept** (locking now), **counter** (moving, with your position), **park** (not addressing this round, deliberately). Everything in their offer goes in exactly one column. Anything unassigned will be reopened later, by whichever side benefits.

### Step 2 — Draft the acknowledgement
One or two sentences naming something specific and genuine about their proposal — the structure, a term that works, the speed of the response. Specificity is the entire mechanism; generic appreciation reads as a formality and does nothing. This costs no substance and measurably changes how the counter is received.

### Step 3 — Accept and lock
State the accepted terms explicitly as agreed: "We're aligned on the term length, the payment schedule, and the support tier — treating those as settled." This is the highest-value sentence in the message. It shrinks the open set, creates a written record of agreement, and makes reopening those terms a visible move rather than a quiet one.

### Step 4 — Write each counter with its basis
For each contested term: your position, then the basis. The basis should be external where possible — a published benchmark, a comparable deal, a cost build-up, a policy, a precedent. Format each as a single tight unit so it survives being read alone:

> *Pricing: we'd need to land at X. That's the midpoint of what [benchmark] shows for this scope, and it's consistent with what we've done on comparable engagements.*

Avoid basing a counter on your own constraint. "Our budget won't stretch" gives the approver nothing to approve with; "this is the market midpoint" gives them a defensible reason to say yes.

### Step 5 — Apply the one-counter-per-issue rule
State your actual position on each contested term, once. Written channels are re-readable, so visible padding is more costly here than in a room — a counter obviously inflated for negotiating room is re-read, noticed, and discounted on every subsequent term. Move per the concession plan, not per the mood of the exchange.

### Step 6 — Check forwardability
Read the message as their approver, who has not seen the conversation. Does it explain itself? Is the reasoning visible? Does it make the counterpart's case *for them* when they take it upward? A counter that requires verbal explanation to be persuasive will be paraphrased badly by the person forwarding it. Where possible, write a line the recipient can quote directly to their approver.

### Step 7 — Close with a step and a date
End with something specific and dated: "If that works, I'll have the revised draft to you by Thursday" or "Happy to walk through the pricing basis on a call this week — does Wednesday morning work?" A message ending in "let me know your thoughts" has no forcing function and will sit.

### Step 8 — Final checks
Confirm: every accepted term is explicitly locked; every counter has a basis; every concession carries its condition in the same sentence; the open-item list is visible; nothing apologizes for countering; and the tone survives the one-register-colder conversion of the written channel.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If they accept everything you locked and reject every counter, are you content with that outcome?
- Which counter has the weakest basis, and would you drop it rather than let it weaken the others?
- What in this message could be quoted back at you in the renewal?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Full restatement.** Rewriting the whole deal in the counter. It signals nothing is settled, invites a complete re-response, and guarantees the negotiation runs in place across rounds.
2. **Implicit acceptance.** Not naming the terms you agree to. Silence on a term is not agreement in writing; it is an open item that will be reopened by whoever benefits.
3. **Need-based justification.** "This is outside our budget." Approvers cannot approve on the basis of your constraints; they approve on the basis of standards. Externalize every basis you can.
4. **Counter-everything.** Contesting all terms because several need movement. It reads as rejection of the proposal and triggers a defensive full re-counter rather than a narrowing.
5. **Visible padding.** An obviously inflated counter, which in a re-readable channel is noticed, discounted, and applied retroactively to your credibility on every other term.
6. **Naked concessions.** Movement written without its condition in the same sentence. The concession is quotable and permanent; the condition is not.
7. **Apologetic framing.** "Sorry to push back on this, but..." It marks the counter as soft and invites a re-counter to the original position.
8. **Non-forwardable drafting.** A counter that only makes sense with verbal context. The person who forwards it will not reproduce your explanation, and your case arrives at the approver in their paraphrase.

## Verification

- [ ] Every term in their offer sorted into accept / counter / park; none unassigned.
- [ ] Acknowledgement is specific, not generic.
- [ ] Accepted terms explicitly named and stated as settled.
- [ ] Every counter carries a basis, and bases are external rather than need-based.
- [ ] One counter per issue, at the actual position, consistent with the concession plan.
- [ ] Every concession carries its condition in the same sentence.
- [ ] Open-item list visible so the narrowing is legible to both sides.
- [ ] Message passes the approver-with-no-context read.
- [ ] A quotable line is provided for the recipient to carry upward.
- [ ] Closes with a specific next step and a date.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests the accept-all-locks / reject-all-counters outcome.
- [ ] No apology for countering.
- [ ] No full restatement of settled terms.
