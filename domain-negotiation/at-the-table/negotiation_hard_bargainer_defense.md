# Hard Bargainer Defense — Name and Neutralize Coercive Tactics Without Escalating

**Objective:** Coercive negotiating tactics work by producing a feeling — urgency, discomfort, obligation, or intimidation — and then relying on you to relieve that feeling by conceding. The defense is not toughness; it is recognition. Once a tactic is identified as a tactic, most of its force is gone, because the pressure was never about the substance. This prompt takes what is happening to you, identifies the specific move, explains the mechanism it relies on, and gives three graduated responses: **absorb** (let it pass, proceed as if it did not happen), **name** (make the tactic explicit, which usually retires it), or **exit** (make continuation conditional). It closes with the walkaway check, because the correct response to some tactics is to leave.

This assumes you have a floor. Without a reservation point from `negotiation_batna_analysis.md`, every tactic on this list works, because you have no reference point from which to judge whether the pressure is worth resisting.

**When to use:**
- You are in or heading into a negotiation with someone using pressure tactics.
- Something in the conversation felt wrong and you want to identify what happened.
- A negotiation is scheduled with a counterpart known to bargain aggressively.
- You conceded more than intended last time and want to know which move produced it.

**When NOT to use:**
- The counterpart is negotiating firmly but fairly — firm is not coercive, and treating it as such damages a workable negotiation. Firmness holds a position; coercion manufactures pressure unrelated to the merits.
- You are the one deploying these tactics and want them sharpened — this domain does not provide that.
- The dynamic is emotional escalation rather than tactical pressure — use `negotiation_emotional_flooding_at_the_table.md`.
- The relationship is personal rather than transactional — use `difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_pre_brief.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals negotiating against a counterpart who is applying pressure rather than argument.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **What happened.** The specific words, offer, or behaviour, quoted as exactly as you can recall.
2. **When in the process.** Opening, mid-negotiation, or at the close — timing changes what a move means.
3. **Your reservation point.** From `negotiation_batna_analysis.md`.
4. **Your alternatives.** How good the no-deal outcome actually is right now.
5. **Relationship horizon.** One-shot, repeated, or indefinite.
6. **How you felt.** The reaction the move produced — urgency, guilt, intimidation, obligation. This is diagnostic.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- **Identify the specific tactic** by name before recommending any response. Generic advice to "stay firm" is not a defense.
- Explain the **mechanism** — the feeling the tactic manufactures and the concession it is meant to buy. Naming the mechanism is most of the cure.
- Offer **three graduated responses** (absorb / name / exit) with the conditions favouring each, rather than a single prescription.
- Provide the **actual words** for the chosen response. Under pressure, people use the sentence they have rehearsed or none at all.
- Run the **walkaway check**: does this tactic, or the pattern of tactics, change the assessment of whether to be in this negotiation at all?
- Distinguish **firm from coercive**. A counterpart holding a position with reasons is not running a tactic, and misclassifying them wastes a workable deal.
- Note the **relationship cost** of each response, since naming a tactic is a real move with consequences in a continuing relationship.

### Must Not
- Recommend matching aggression. Escalation converts a tactical problem into a relationship problem and hands the advantage to whoever is more comfortable in conflict.
- Recommend capitulation framed as pragmatism. Conceding to a manufactured deadline teaches the counterpart that manufactured deadlines work — including in every future negotiation with you.
- Treat every hard move as coercion. Some counterparts genuinely have a final number, a real deadline, and limited authority.
- Advise naming a tactic when the evidence is ambiguous. Naming a tactic that was not one is an accusation, and it is expensive.
- Assume the tactic is personal. Most are scripted, deployed on everyone, and reveal nothing about what the counterpart thinks of you.
- Ignore that a tactic can be both a tactic and true — a real deadline can also be used as pressure.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Record what happened verbatim
Write the words or behaviour as exactly as possible, plus when in the process it occurred and what you felt. The feeling is a diagnostic input: urgency points to deadline tactics, guilt to relationship leverage, intimidation to dominance display, obligation to the nibble.

### Step 2 — Identify the tactic
Match against the catalogue:

| Tactic | Recognition signal | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme anchor** | Opening far outside plausible range | Distorts your sense of the range; makes their second number look reasonable |
| **Exploding offer** | "This is only available until [soon]" | Manufactured urgency; removes deliberation time |
| **The nibble** | Small extra request after agreement | Exploits your investment in a completed deal |
| **Good cop / bad cop** | One reasonable, one hostile counterpart | Makes the "reasonable" position feel like a concession |
| **Manufactured deadline** | Unverifiable time pressure | Compresses your process; you concede to close |
| **Take-it-or-leave-it** | Refusal to negotiate further | Forecloses the range; tests whether your floor is real |
| **Personal attack** | Comments on you, not the substance | Shifts you from analysis to self-defense |
| **Late authority reveal** | "I have to run this by..." after agreement | Extracts a further concession as the price of approval |

### Step 3 — Test whether it is actually a tactic
Ask three questions. Is the pressure connected to the merits, or manufactured alongside them? Is the constraint verifiable? Would a reasonable counterpart in their position genuinely be here? If all three point to legitimacy, this is firmness, not coercion — stop and negotiate the substance.

### Step 4 — Choose the response level
- **Absorb** when the tactic is weak, the relationship matters more than the point, or naming it would inflate a minor move. Proceed as though it did not happen. Silence in response to an extreme anchor is itself a strong response.
- **Name** when the tactic is clear, repeated, or materially distorting the process. Naming retires most tactics because they depend on going unremarked.
- **Exit** when the tactic is coercive enough that continuing rewards it, or when your alternatives are good. Making continuation conditional is the strongest available move and requires a real floor.

### Step 5 — Write the words
Draft the actual sentence. Working patterns by response level:
- *Absorb:* "Let's come back to that. On the scope question —"
- *Name:* "I want to flag what's happening: we agreed the terms, and now there's an additional condition. I'd like to close on what we agreed."
- *Exit:* "If the offer expires tonight, then it expires — I'm not able to make a decision of this size on that timeline. If it's still open next week, I'm interested."

Keep them short, non-accusatory, and free of the counter-aggression the tactic was designed to provoke.

### Step 6 — Handle the specific high-frequency cases
**Exploding offer:** the response is almost always to let it explode. Genuine offers rarely vanish; the ones that do were priced for someone who would not deliberate. **The nibble:** reopen the whole package rather than conceding the increment ("if we're adding that, we should look at the pricing again"). This makes nibbling costly and stops it permanently. **Take-it-or-leave-it:** do not test it directly; give them a face-saving route back ("I understand that's where you are — if anything changes on the term length, I'd take another look").

### Step 7 — Run the walkaway check
Compare the current offer against your reservation point, honestly, right now. Tactics work by making the floor feel negotiable. Restate the floor and ask: independent of how uncomfortable this is, is the deal on the table still above it? If not, the tactic has already worked and the correct response is to leave.

### Step 8 — Assess the pattern and the relationship cost
One tactic is a move; three is a negotiating style, and a style tells you what the relationship will be like after signature. Note the relationship cost of your chosen response, and whether a pattern of coercion should change the terms you require — more protection, shorter commitment, tighter remedies.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Are you labelling firmness as coercion because you dislike the position?
- What would you do if this exact offer arrived with no pressure attached — and does that change your answer?
- If you name the tactic and are wrong, what does that cost?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Firmness misread as coercion.** Treating a counterpart with a genuine final number as a manipulator. The test is whether the pressure is connected to the merits or manufactured alongside them. Getting this wrong destroys workable deals.
2. **Escalation matching.** Responding to aggression with aggression. It converts a tactical problem into a relationship problem, and it favours whoever is more comfortable in conflict — usually the person who opened aggressively.
3. **Comfort-restoring concession.** Conceding to end the discomfort, then rationalizing it as pragmatism. The concession is real; the pragmatism is retrospective. Check against the reservation point, not against how the room feels.
4. **Naming on ambiguous evidence.** Calling out a tactic that was not one. It is an accusation, it is remembered, and it is very hard to withdraw.
5. **Personalizing the script.** Reading a scripted tactic as a statement about you. Most are deployed identically on everyone and carry no information about their view of you.
6. **Exploding-offer compliance.** Deciding fast because the deadline was stated confidently. The correct default is to let it explode and observe what actually happens — which is usually nothing.
7. **Nibble accommodation.** Conceding the small extra because the deal is nearly done and the increment seems trivial relative to your sunk effort. Each accommodation guarantees the next one. Reopen the package instead.
8. **Tactic-or-true false dichotomy.** Assuming a deadline is either genuine or a tactic. It is frequently both, and the response is the same: verify it, and do not let its existence compress your judgment.

## Verification

- [ ] The behaviour recorded verbatim, with timing and the feeling it produced.
- [ ] A specific named tactic identified, with confidence stated.
- [ ] The mechanism named — the feeling manufactured and the concession sought.
- [ ] Legitimacy test run; firm-but-legitimate explicitly distinguished from coercive.
- [ ] All three response levels offered with conditions and relationship costs.
- [ ] An actual script written, short and non-accusatory.
- [ ] Walkaway check run against the reservation point independent of how the room feels.
- [ ] Pattern assessed across the whole negotiation, not just this move.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests the coercion label and the no-pressure counterfactual.
- [ ] No recommendation to match aggression.
- [ ] No concession recommended on the basis of manufactured urgency.
