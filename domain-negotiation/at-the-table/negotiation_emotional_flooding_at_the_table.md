# Emotional Flooding at the Table — De-escalate Without Conceding on Substance

**Objective:** Negotiations produce heat, and heat produces the single most expensive reflex in the field: buying calm with a concession. The concession works — the room settles — and it teaches everyone present that escalation is profitable, including in every subsequent conversation. This prompt handles the emotional layer separately from the substantive one. It distinguishes a **tactical display** (deployed for effect, responds to being unrewarded) from **genuine flooding** (a real physiological state, responds only to time and acknowledgement), because treating either as the other makes it worse. It supplies a de-escalation sequence built on the separation of **acknowledgement from agreement** — the move that lets a person be fully heard while the position does not move a millimetre. It covers your own flooding, which is the case people prepare for least and encounter most. And it handles repair when you were the one who escalated.

This is the emotional-layer companion to `negotiation_hard_bargainer_defense.md`, which handles calculated coercion. The two overlap: an anger display can be both genuine and tactical, and Step 2 resolves which response leads.

**When to use:**
- The counterpart has become angry, personal, or has threatened to walk.
- You feel yourself becoming reactive and need a protocol rather than an instinct.
- A previous session escalated and you need to reopen without repeating it.
- The negotiation carries history and heat is likely.

**When NOT to use:**
- The pressure is calculated and cold rather than emotional — `negotiation_hard_bargainer_defense.md`.
- The deadlock is emotional but stable rather than acute — `negotiation_impasse_breaker.md` (emotional impasse).
- The conversation is fundamentally a relationship conversation — `difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_pre_brief.md`.
- Anyone's safety is at issue — leave, and treat that as the whole decision.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals negotiating where emotion has entered the room.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **What happened.** The trigger and the response, as precisely as recalled.
2. **Who escalated.** Them, you, or both.
3. **History.** Prior friction between the parties, and whether this is a pattern.
4. **The substantive position at the moment of escalation.** What was on the table when it happened.
5. **Your own state.** Whether you are currently regulated enough to continue.
6. **Relationship horizon.** Whether this relationship continues after the deal.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Separate the **emotional response from the substantive position**. The position does not move because the temperature changed.
- Classify the display as **tactical, genuine, or both**, and lead with the response matched to the dominant component.
- Use **acknowledgement without agreement**: name what they are experiencing and why it makes sense from where they sit, while conceding nothing on the merits.
- Include a **self-regulation protocol** for the user's own flooding, with an explicit rule for when to stop rather than continue.
- Specify the **break rule** — when to call one, how to call it without it reading as retreat, and what to do during it.
- Design **re-entry**: how the conversation restarts, and at what position.
- Address **repair** if the user escalated, without over-apologizing into a substantive concession.

### Must Not
- Recommend any substantive concession as a de-escalation move. This is the central prohibition of the prompt.
- Tell the user to match the emotional register. Escalating in return converts a recoverable moment into a relationship rupture.
- Recommend suppressing or denying the emotion, theirs or the user's. "Let's keep this professional" reads as dismissal and reliably escalates.
- Treat genuine flooding as a tactic. A flooded person cannot process argument regardless of quality; continuing to reason at them extends the state.
- Continue negotiating while the user is flooded. Decisions made in that state are systematically worse and frequently irreversible.
- Apologize for the position when apologizing for the delivery. These are separable, and conflating them concedes substance under cover of manners.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Record the trigger and the current position
Write what preceded the escalation and what was on the table at that moment. The substantive position is recorded now specifically so that it can be returned to unchanged later — writing it down is what protects it.

### Step 2 — Classify the display
Test against both patterns:

| Signal | Tactical | Genuine |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Sudden, at a decision point | Builds, often after accumulation |
| Content | Directed at terms and leverage | Directed at treatment, respect, history |
| Recovery | Fast once it produces movement | Slow regardless of movement |
| Repeats | At each concession point | Around a consistent theme |
| Effect of not rewarding it | Subsides | Continues or intensifies |

Most real escalations are **both**. Lead with the response to the dominant component; if genuinely mixed, lead with the genuine response, because addressing real feeling costs nothing substantive while ignoring it is unrecoverable.

### Step 3 — Apply acknowledgement without agreement
The core move, in two parts held strictly separate. **Acknowledge:** "You've been clear that the timeline shifting has cost your team real work, and I don't think that's an unreasonable thing to be frustrated about." **Do not concede:** no "so let me see what I can do," no movement on terms, no hint that the frustration purchased anything. Then stop and let the acknowledgement land. Most people escalate because they believe they have not been heard; being heard resolves it, and being heard is free.

### Step 4 — Run the self-regulation protocol
If you are the flooded one: name it internally, slow your speech deliberately, and use the holding line — "I want to give that a proper answer rather than a fast one. Can we take ten minutes?" Then apply the hard rule: **do not make or accept any offer while flooded.** Physiological arousal degrades exactly the judgment this situation requires, and the decisions made in it tend to be the irreversible ones. If you cannot regulate within one break, end the session.

### Step 5 — Call the break correctly
A break is the most reliable de-escalation tool available and is routinely avoided because it feels like losing. Call it as process, not retreat: "Let's take fifteen — I'd rather come back to this properly." During it: do not rehearse grievances, do not draft rebuttals, do not seek allies to confirm you were right. Re-read the position recorded in Step 1. If possible, change something physical about the setting on return.

### Step 6 — Re-enter at the recorded position
Restart at the substance, from exactly where Step 1 recorded it. State this explicitly and neutrally: "Where we were is [position]. I'd like to pick up there." Any drift from the recorded position — in either direction — is the escalation having produced an effect, which is the outcome this prompt exists to prevent.

### Step 7 — Repair if you escalated
Separate delivery from substance. **Apologize for delivery, specifically and once:** "I was sharp with you earlier and that wasn't warranted — I'm sorry." **Do not apologize for the position:** the terms are unchanged and saying so is not rudeness. Over-apologizing after your own escalation is one of the most common routes to an unintended concession, because the impulse to repair spills across the boundary into substance.

### Step 8 — Decide whether to continue at all
Some escalations are information. If the counterpart's conduct indicates the relationship will be adversarial through implementation, that changes what terms you should require — more protection, shorter commitment, tighter remedies — or whether to proceed. Ask this coldly, after regulation, not during.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Did the position move at all between the escalation and re-entry, in either direction?
- Are you classifying genuine feeling as tactical because that is easier to respond to?
- If this is how they behave while they still need something from you, what does implementation look like?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Buying calm.** Conceding to settle the room. It works immediately, teaches that escalation pays, and the lesson persists into every future interaction with this counterpart.
2. **Register matching.** Escalating in return. It converts a recoverable moment into a rupture, and it hands the advantage to whoever is more comfortable in conflict.
3. **Emotion suppression.** "Let's keep this professional." It is heard as dismissal, confirms the person is not being taken seriously, and reliably intensifies what it was meant to end.
4. **Reasoning at a flooded person.** Continuing to argue the merits to someone physiologically unable to process them. The argument does not land, and the attempt extends the state.
5. **Negotiating while flooded yourself.** Making or accepting an offer under arousal. This is where the irreversible mistakes concentrate; the break rule exists specifically to prevent it.
6. **Apology spillover.** Apologizing for delivery and, carried by the repair impulse, adjusting terms. Keep the two strictly separate — a specific apology for conduct, no movement on substance.
7. **Break avoidance.** Refusing to pause because it feels like conceding ground. A break costs fifteen minutes; continuing while either party is flooded costs the terms.
8. **Tactical over-classification.** Reading real hurt as manipulation because manipulation has a cleaner response. Getting this wrong is unrecoverable — a person whose genuine grievance was treated as a tactic does not return to good faith.

## Verification

- [ ] The substantive position at the moment of escalation recorded before any response is designed.
- [ ] Display classified tactical / genuine / both, with the dominant component identified.
- [ ] Acknowledgement script names the feeling and concedes nothing.
- [ ] The script explicitly excludes any "let me see what I can do" formulation.
- [ ] Self-regulation protocol includes the no-offer-while-flooded rule and a stop condition.
- [ ] Break framed as process, with during-break rules that exclude grievance rehearsal.
- [ ] Re-entry returns to the recorded position, with a drift check in both directions.
- [ ] Repair separates delivery apology from substantive position.
- [ ] The continue-or-stop question answered after regulation, not during.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests for position drift and for tactical over-classification.
- [ ] No substantive concession recommended anywhere as a de-escalation move.
- [ ] No advice to suppress, dismiss, or match the emotion.
