# Closing and the Final Concession — Recognize the Close, Structure the Last Move, Refuse the Nibble

**Objective:** The endgame is where carefully accumulated value most often leaks away. Three specific failures do it. First, **misreading closeability** — spending the reserve concession on a counterpart who was going to agree anyway, or on one who was never going to agree at all. Second, **structuring the final move badly** — giving the last concession unconditionally, so that instead of purchasing closure it purchases another round. Third, **the nibble** — the small additional request that arrives after agreement, when your investment in a completed deal is at its maximum and your resistance at its minimum. This prompt handles all three: the closeability signals, the conditional structure that makes a final concession final, the lock-down sequence before anyone leaves, and the package-reopening response that makes nibbling unprofitable permanently.

This spends the reserve that `preparation/negotiation_concession_anchoring_plan.md` deliberately held back. If no reserve was planned, the endgame has nothing to close with.

**When to use:**
- The negotiation appears to be nearing agreement and you need to know whether it actually is.
- You are deciding whether to make a final concession, and how to structure it.
- Agreement has been reached and you want it locked before it drifts.
- A small extra request has arrived after you thought the deal was done.

**When NOT to use:**
- The negotiation is still mid-range and concession strategy is the question — `preparation/negotiation_concession_anchoring_plan.md`.
- Movement has stopped entirely rather than narrowed — `negotiation_impasse_breaker.md`.
- The blockage at the close is approval rather than terms — `negotiation_authority_mandate_limits.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals approaching agreement in a negotiation with material stakes.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Current positions.** Where each side stands and what remains open.
2. **Your reserve.** What was held back for the close, per the concession plan.
3. **Signals so far.** What the counterpart has said or done suggesting readiness or its absence.
4. **Deadline structure.** What forces a decision, and on whom.
5. **Authority position.** Whether the person present can actually conclude.
6. **What has been agreed.** Every term settled so far, as understood.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Assess **closeability against explicit signals** before spending anything. The reserve is spent once.
- Make the final concession **conditional and explicitly final** — tied to acceptance now, not offered into an open room.
- **Lock the agreement** before the meeting ends: terms restated aloud, confirmed, and put in writing the same day.
- Respond to a nibble by **reopening the package**, never by conceding the increment.
- Distinguish a counterpart **testing whether more is available** from one with a genuine remaining obstacle. The responses differ entirely.
- State what the close **signals for implementation and the next negotiation** — the last move is the one they remember.
- Confirm the closing terms clear the **reservation point** with the reserve spent, before spending it.

### Must Not
- Spend the reserve to create momentum. Its function is to purchase closure; used for anything else it is simply a concession, and the close then has nothing behind it.
- Offer the final concession unconditionally. "I could probably do X" without an attached acceptance condition converts your last move into a new baseline.
- Leave the room without a written summary. Agreements drift within hours, and the drift is not random — it moves toward whoever writes first.
- Concede the nibble because it is small. Size is not the issue; the precedent is. One accommodated nibble reliably produces the next.
- Congratulate the counterpart on a hard-won deal in terms that suggest you would have gone further. Post-close relief routinely discloses more than the whole negotiation did.
- Treat agreement in principle as agreement. Unspecified terms are unagreed terms.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Assess closeability
Score against signals rather than intuition:

| Closeable | Not yet |
|---|---|
| Asking about implementation, timing, onboarding | Still re-arguing settled points |
| Bringing in the approver or legal | Introducing new issues |
| Questions shift from whether to how | Repeating positions without movement |
| Concessions arriving without being asked | Every concession requires extraction |
| Language moves to "when" | Language stays at "if" |

Two or more "not yet" signals means the close is premature and the reserve should not be spent.

### Step 2 — Confirm the arithmetic
Before spending anything: state the current offer, subtract the reserve, and confirm the result still clears the reservation point with margin. A final concession that lands you on or below the floor is not a close; it is a capitulation with ceremony.

### Step 3 — Identify what actually remains
Separate the genuine remaining obstacle from a test of whether more is available. A **genuine obstacle** is specific, consistent with everything they have said, and usually procedural or approval-related. A **test** is general, arrives without a reason, and shifts if you address it. Address obstacles; decline tests.

### Step 4 — Structure the final concession
Three properties, all required. **Conditional:** it exists only if accepted now — "if we can sign on that basis today, I can do X." **Explicitly final:** say so plainly, in words you will not have to retract — "that's my last move on price." **Reciprocal where possible:** attach something, even small — a faster signature, a reference, a longer term. An unconditional final concession is a new opening position, and will be treated as one.

### Step 5 — Deliver and then stop talking
Say the final concession and stop. The silence after a final offer is doing the work; filling it — with justification, with softening, with an alternative — reliably converts a final offer into a penultimate one. If they counter anyway, the response is to restate, not to move: "That's where I am."

### Step 6 — Lock the agreement
Before anyone leaves: restate every agreed term aloud and get explicit confirmation on each. Name the open items rather than letting them pass silently. Then commit to sending a written summary the same day, and send it — the party who writes first defines the record, and memory reliably drifts toward each side's own interests. Agreement in principle with unspecified terms is not agreement.

### Step 7 — Refuse the nibble
When a small extra request arrives after agreement, the response is invariable: reopen the package. "Happy to look at that — if we're adding it, we should revisit the pricing at the same time." This is not aggressive and it is not a refusal; it simply prices the increment. A nibble is profitable only when it is free, and this makes it never free. Applied once, it usually ends nibbling in this and every subsequent negotiation with the counterpart.

### Step 8 — Manage the post-close moment
Relief discloses. The minutes after agreement are when negotiators reveal how much further they would have gone, how relieved they are, or how good the deal was for them — all of which is priced into the next negotiation and sometimes into this one's implementation. Be warm, be brief, be specific about next steps, and say nothing about the range.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Would this have closed without the final concession?
- Is what remains a genuine obstacle or a test, and what evidence separates them?
- What did the close teach them about how you behave under time pressure?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Premature reserve spending.** Giving the final concession to a counterpart who was closing anyway. The tell is unprompted concessions and implementation questions — both indicate a deal already landing. Check the signals before spending.
2. **Unconditional finals.** "I could probably stretch to X" with no acceptance condition. This is not a close; it is a new opening, and it will be countered from there.
3. **Soft finality.** Saying "that's about as far as I can go" — hedged language that invites one more push and that you will have to retract, costing credibility at the exact moment it matters most.
4. **Post-offer talking.** Delivering the final concession and then justifying it. Every additional sentence after a final offer erodes its finality; the silence is the mechanism.
5. **Nibble accommodation.** Conceding the small extra because the deal is done and the increment is trivial next to the effort invested. It is always trivial, and it always recurs. Reopen the package instead.
6. **Verbal-agreement reliance.** Leaving without a written summary. Recollections diverge within hours and diverge self-servingly. Whoever writes first sets the record.
7. **Principle-agreement inflation.** Treating "we're agreed in principle" as a deal. Unspecified terms are unagreed, and they reappear as concessions requested during documentation.
8. **Post-close disclosure.** Relief-driven candour — "honestly, I had more room" or visible delight — which prices directly into implementation behaviour and the next negotiation.
