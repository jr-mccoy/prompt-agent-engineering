# Live Question Sequencing — Surface Interests in the Room Without Signalling Need

**Objective:** Questions are the highest-return activity in a negotiation and the most commonly wasted. Wasted three ways: asked in an order that triggers defensiveness before rapport exists, phrased so the counterpart can hear what you are fishing for, and abandoned after the first answer — which is almost always the prepared one. This prompt builds the sequence you will actually use. It sorts questions by **function** (context, interest, constraint, test), orders them **low-threat to diagnostic**, rewrites extractive phrasings into ones a counterpart wants to answer, and attaches a **follow-up** to each so you get past the surface response. It handles the three questions everyone needs but nobody asks well — budget, timeline, alternatives — and it covers what to do with silence, which is a question in its own right.

This is the live counterpart to `preparation/negotiation_information_plan.md`, which decides *what* to learn and *what* to protect. This one decides how to ask.

**When to use:**
- Preparing for the first substantive conversation in a negotiation.
- Your questions have been getting short or evasive answers.
- You need to confirm inferred interests from `negotiation_interest_mapping.md` in the room.
- The counterpart is more experienced than you and you want to learn rather than argue.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are deciding what information to protect rather than what to ask — `preparation/negotiation_information_plan.md`.
- You need to interpret answers you have already received — `negotiation_reading_signals_and_bluffs.md`.
- The conversation is relationship-primary rather than deal-primary — `difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_pre_brief.md`.

**Audience:** Executives, founders, salespeople, people leaders, lawyers, and individuals who want to leave a negotiation knowing more than they arrived with.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The negotiation.** What is being negotiated, with whom.
2. **What you need to learn.** From `negotiation_information_plan.md`, ranked by strategic dependence.
3. **Inferred interests to confirm.** From `negotiation_interest_mapping.md`, especially the guessed ones.
4. **Format and time.** How long the conversation is and whether it is live, video, or written.
5. **Relationship state.** How much trust exists, and whether this is a first conversation.
6. **What they may be guarding.** Where you expect resistance.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Assign each question a **function** — context, interest, constraint, or test — so the sequence covers all four rather than clustering on one.
- Order **low-threat to diagnostic**. Sharp questions asked early get prepared answers; the same questions asked after twenty minutes of genuine exchange get real ones.
- Phrase every question so the counterpart **wants to answer it**. The rewrite test: does this sound like someone trying to understand, or someone building a case?
- Attach a **follow-up** to every question. The first answer is the prepared one; the second is where the information is.
- Include **what would confirm vs. overturn** the underlying inference for each test question.
- Plan the **budget, timeline, and alternatives** questions specifically, since these are the three that most reveal your own priorities when asked clumsily.
- Specify **what to do with silence** — both theirs and yours.

### Must Not
- Design questions that telegraph what you need. "Is the timeline flexible?" tells them timing matters to you before they answer.
- Ask leading questions that invite the answer you want. You will get it, and it will be worthless.
- Stack multiple questions in one turn. The counterpart answers the easiest and the rest are lost.
- Ask questions whose answers you could have researched. It spends attention and signals what you care about, for a fact you could have looked up.
- Fill silence after asking. The pause after a question is doing the work; filling it releases the pressure and answers the question for them.
- Treat an answer about their alternatives, budget, or deadline as verified. These are negotiating inputs, not facts.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — List what you need to learn, ranked
Pull the ranked acquisition list from `negotiation_information_plan.md`, or build it now: what the strategy depends on, ordered by how much breaks if the assumption is wrong. Strip anything researchable — those never become questions.

### Step 2 — Assign each item a question function
Sort into four types, each doing different work:

| Function | Asks about | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Context** | Their situation, history, pressures | "How did this come to be a priority now?" |
| **Interest** | Why something matters to them | "What makes the term length the piece you keep coming back to?" |
| **Constraint** | What they cannot do, and why | "What would have to be true for that to be approvable?" |
| **Test** | Whether a specific inference holds | "If timing weren't an issue, would the scope still be the sticking point?" |

A sequence with no interest questions produces a transaction; one with no constraint questions produces a deal that fails at approval.

### Step 3 — Rewrite for willingness
Take each drafted question and apply the rewrite test: does this sound like inquiry or like case-building? Common conversions:
- "What's your budget?" → "What kind of range were you working toward, so I can tell you quickly whether we're in the same neighbourhood?"
- "Are you talking to other vendors?" → "How are you approaching the decision — is this a comparison process or a fit question?"
- "Is the deadline real?" → "What happens on your side if this lands in April instead of March?"

Each conversion keeps the information sought and removes the adversarial frame.

### Step 4 — Order low-threat to diagnostic
Sequence so early questions are ones anyone would answer comfortably and later ones require accumulated trust. Note the approximate point in the conversation for each. The rule: never ask a question in the first five minutes that you would be uncomfortable answering in the first five minutes.

### Step 5 — Attach follow-ups
For every question, write the follow-up that gets past the first answer. The most useful are the simplest: "What's behind that?", "Say more about that part", "What would change it?", and a repeat of their own key phrase as a question. Also write the **silence follow-up** — asking, then saying nothing, which is frequently the most productive follow-up available.

### Step 6 — Handle the big three
Write the specific approach for budget, timeline, and alternatives:
- **Budget:** ask for a range and offer the reason ("so I can tell you quickly if we're aligned"). Giving a reason converts an extraction into a courtesy.
- **Timeline:** ask what drives it, not whether it is real. The driver reveals whether it is genuine, and the question is unobjectionable.
- **Alternatives:** ask about their *process*, not their options. "How are you approaching the decision?" gets further than "who else are you talking to?"

### Step 7 — Plan the silences
Two kinds. **After your question:** do not fill it. Count to five internally; the counterpart will usually continue, and the continuation carries more than the first answer. **After their statement:** a pause invites elaboration without the pressure of another question, and is the single cheapest information-gathering move available.

### Step 8 — Set the capture and adaptation rules
Decide what you will note during the conversation and how (a counterpart who sees you writing selectively learns what you value — write throughout or not at all). Then set the adaptation rule: if an early answer overturns an inference, which later questions become irrelevant, and which new one replaces them.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Which question most reveals your own priorities, and can it be rephrased or dropped?
- If they answer everything strategically, what would you still learn from *how* they answered?
- What are you not asking because you assume you know the answer?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Telegraphing.** Questions whose subject reveals what you need. Asking three questions about timing establishes that timing is your constraint before any answer arrives. Distribute sensitive questions across functions and time.
2. **First-answer acceptance.** Taking the prepared response and moving on. The first answer to any significant question is the one they came ready to give; the information is in the second and third.
3. **Question stacking.** Two or three questions in one turn. The counterpart answers whichever is easiest and the others vanish without either party noticing.
4. **Leading questions.** "So flexibility on timing would help you, wouldn't it?" produces agreement that carries no information and forecloses the real answer.
5. **Interrogation framing.** A sequence of pointed questions with no reciprocal disclosure and no warmth. It triggers defensiveness, shuts down volunteered information, and converts a conversation into a deposition.
6. **Silence-filling.** Asking a good question and then, uncomfortable with the pause, softening it or answering it yourself. This is the most common way a well-designed question is wasted.
7. **Researchable questions.** Spending a question on something a filing, a colleague, or a website would have told you — paying attention and signalling interest for nothing.
8. **Answer credulity.** Recording their stated budget or alternatives as fact. Everything a counterpart says about their constraints is a move; note it as a claim with a confidence tag.

## Verification

- [ ] Acquisition list ranked, with researchable items stripped before question design.
- [ ] All four question functions represented; no clustering on one.
- [ ] Sequence ordered low-threat to diagnostic, with conversation timing noted.
- [ ] Every question passes the rewrite test — inquiry, not case-building.
- [ ] Every question has a follow-up attached.
- [ ] Test questions carry confirm/overturn conditions.
- [ ] Budget, timeline, and alternatives each have a specific non-telegraphing approach.
- [ ] Silence plan specified for both after-question and after-statement.
- [ ] Capture method chosen so it doesn't signal what you value.
- [ ] Adaptation rule set for an overturned early inference.
- [ ] Adversarial check names the most revealing question and the unasked assumption.
- [ ] No stacked questions in any single turn.
- [ ] No leading questions.
