# Difficult Conversation Post-Review — Debrief What Just Happened

**Objective:** Run a structured debrief on a difficult conversation right after it happens, while memory is fresh and emotion is high. The high emotion is exactly why structure matters: left alone, the mind either replays the conversation as a highlight reel of the user's best lines or as a loop of their worst moment, and neither is the truth. This prompt forces the factual account first (what was actually said and done, no interpretation), then the interpretation layer, then the crucial gap between what the user said and what the other person seemed to actually *hear*. It converts the conversation into two ledgers — what the user owes the other person now, and what they owe themselves — and ends by extracting the durable pattern: what this conversation reveals about how the user handles difficult conversations, and the one concrete change to make in preparing for the next one.

**When to use:**
- Right after a hard conversation (firing, breakup, hard feedback, boundary-setting, declined request).
- When the conversation didn't go as planned and you're spinning on it.
- When the conversation went well and you want to bank the lesson before it fades.
- As the closing half of a loop with `difficultconvo_pre_brief.md`.

**When NOT to use:**
- Mid-conflict, when the situation is ongoing and a debrief would be premature — wait until the conversation has actually concluded.
- For routine conversations that carried no real stakes.
- As a substitute for an owed apology or repair — if you already know you owe a repair, do that; this organizes it, it doesn't replace it.

**Audience:** Managers, executives, founders, and individuals processing a difficult conversation they just had.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The conversation.** With whom, when, and its purpose.
2. **What you intended going in.** The goal you held (ideally from the pre-brief).
3. **Roughly what was said.** Your recollection of the actual exchange, as close to verbatim as you can manage.
4. **How it ended.** The concluding moment — agreement, rupture, ambiguity, abrupt end.
5. **Your current emotional state.** Named honestly (relieved, guilty, angry, numb, vindicated). It colors the recall and is worth flagging.
6. **Any owed follow-ups already known.** Things you already promised or sense you must do.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Produce the **factual account first** — what was said and done, in sequence, *with no interpretation*. Strip adjectives and motive attributions.
- Only *after* the facts, write the **interpretation layer** — what you think it meant, clearly labeled as interpretation, not fact.
- Surface **what surprised you** — the moments that didn't go as expected, since surprises are where the user's model of the other person was wrong.
- Name **what you said well** — specifically, not as self-soothing. Bank the moves that worked.
- Name **what you'd say differently** — specifically, with the better version, not just "I should have been clearer."
- Identify the **say/hear gap** — the difference between what you said and what the other person seemed to actually take away. This gap is usually where the next problem starts.
- Build two ledgers: **what you owe them** (follow-up, missing context, repair, a promise to keep) and **what you owe yourself** (decompression, a boundary you held, a pattern to notice).
- Extract the **pattern** — what this conversation reveals about how you handle difficult conversations generally — and **one concrete change** to your preparation for next time.

### Must Not
- Blend facts and interpretation in the factual section. "She got defensive" is interpretation; "she crossed her arms and said 'that's not fair'" is fact.
- Let the debrief become either a highlight reel (only your good lines) or a self-flagellation loop (only your worst moment). Both are distortions.
- Conflate "they disagreed" with "I did it badly." A well-handled conversation can still produce an unhappy other party.
- Skip the say/hear gap because it's uncomfortable. It's the most diagnostic part.
- End at insight. The output must include a concrete preparation change, not just a realization.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Flag your state
Name your current emotional state in one line. This is a calibration note: relief inflates self-grading, guilt deflates it, anger distorts the recall of what they said. Holding it explicitly reduces its pull.

### Step 2 — Write the factual account
In sequence, what was said and done. No adjectives, no motives, no "they seemed." Just the observable exchange, as close to verbatim as memory allows. If you can't separate a fact from an interpretation, move it to Step 3.

### Step 3 — Write the interpretation layer
Now interpret, labeled as such: what you think their reactions meant, what was going on under the surface, how you read the room. Keep it tentative.

### Step 4 — Surface the surprises
List what didn't go as you expected — their reaction, a question you didn't anticipate, your own response. Each surprise is a correction to your model of this person or of yourself.

### Step 5 — What you said well
Specifically. Quote the line or name the move. The point is to identify repeatable good behavior, not to feel better.

### Step 6 — What you'd say differently
For each weak moment, write the actual better version — the words you'd use instead. "Be clearer" is not an answer; the rewritten sentence is.

### Step 7 — The say/hear gap
For the core message, write two columns: what you said, and what they seemed to actually hear (from their response, questions, body language). Name the gap. Decide whether it needs closing with a follow-up.

### Step 8 — Two ledgers
**What you owe them:** follow-up actions, context you didn't give, a repair if you crossed a line, a promise to keep — each with a when. **What you owe yourself:** decompression, acknowledgment of a boundary you held, a pattern to keep watching, permission to have done a hard thing imperfectly.

### Step 9 — Extract the pattern and the change
What does this conversation reveal about how you handle difficult conversations in general (e.g., you over-cushion, you rush the ending, you fold at tears, you over-explain)? Name the pattern. Then name **one concrete change** to how you'll prepare for the next difficult conversation.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Fact/interpretation blending.** Smuggling motive and adjective into the factual account ("he was being manipulative"). Keep Step 2 observable; interpretation goes in Step 3.
2. **Highlight reel.** Recalling only your articulate moments. The debrief is for learning, which requires the weak moments too.
3. **Self-flagellation loop.** Recalling only the worst moment and generalizing it into "I'm bad at this." One bad line is data, not a verdict.
4. **Disagreement-as-failure.** Concluding you handled it badly because they were upset. Some well-handled conversations produce upset people.
5. **Vague rewrites.** "I should have been clearer" without writing the clearer version. The rewrite is the deliverable.
6. **Skipping the say/hear gap.** Avoiding the uncomfortable evidence that they heard something different from what you meant. That gap is the most actionable finding.
7. **Insight without change.** Ending at "I realize I tend to over-cushion" with no change to next time's prep. Insight that doesn't alter behavior is just rumination.
8. **State-blind grading.** Grading the conversation through relief or guilt without flagging the emotional lens. Name the state first.

## Output Format

```
# Post-Conversation Debrief — [with whom], [when]

## My state right now
[One line — relieved / guilty / angry / numb / vindicated. Calibration note.]

## Factual account (no interpretation)
1. [Said / did — observable only]
2. ...
Ending: [how it concluded, observably]

## Interpretation (labeled)
[What I think it meant — tentative.]

## Surprises
- [What didn't go as expected] → corrects my model of: [them / me]

## What I said well
- "[line / move]" — worth repeating because: [...]

## What I'd say differently
| Weak moment | Better version (verbatim) |
|-------------|---------------------------|
| [...] | "[...]" |

## Say / hear gap
| What I said | What they seemed to hear |
|-------------|--------------------------|
| [core message] | [...] |
Gap: [...]  → needs follow-up? [yes/no + what]

## What I owe THEM
- [Action] — by [when]
- [Repair / context / promise]

## What I owe MYSELF
- [Decompression / boundary held / pattern to watch / permission]

## Pattern & change
- Pattern this reveals: [how I handle difficult conversations generally]
- One concrete change to next time's prep: [...]
```

## Verification

- [ ] Emotional state is flagged before grading.
- [ ] The factual account is interpretation-free; interpretation is in a separate, labeled section.
- [ ] Surprises are listed and tied to a corrected model.
- [ ] "Said well" and "say differently" are both specific; rewrites are verbatim.
- [ ] The say/hear gap is named with a follow-up decision.
- [ ] Both ledgers (owe them / owe yourself) are filled, with whens on owed actions.
- [ ] A general pattern is extracted, not just a one-off observation.
- [ ] Exactly one concrete preparation change is committed for next time.
- [ ] No highlight reel, no self-flagellation loop, no insight-without-change.
