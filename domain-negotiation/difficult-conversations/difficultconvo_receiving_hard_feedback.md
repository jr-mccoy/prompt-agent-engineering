# Receiving Hard Feedback — Stay in the Conversation and Separate Signal From Delivery

**Objective:** Receiving criticism well is a skill, and the reason it is hard is physiological rather than characterological: the reaction arrives before any judgment about whether the feedback is correct. In that state the reflexes are explanation, deflection, and — less obviously damaging in the moment but more so over time — over-agreement, which ends the conversation just as effectively while looking like humility. All three cost the same thing: the specifics. Vague feedback stays vague, you leave without knowing what to change, and the person who gave it concludes it did not land and does not try again. This prompt handles the moment: managing the reaction, asking the questions that turn "you're too aggressive in meetings" into something actionable, separating **content** from **delivery** when the delivery was poor, and setting the rule for deciding what to act on — because accepting everything and accepting nothing are both failures, and the second usually masquerades as discernment.

`difficultconvo_post_review.md` debriefs a hard conversation afterward. This is for being in one, on the receiving end.

**When to use:**
- You are about to receive feedback you expect to be difficult.
- You have just received criticism and are deciding how to respond.
- Feedback was delivered badly and you cannot tell whether the content is sound.
- You notice you become defensive and want a protocol rather than an intention.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are giving the feedback — `difficultconvo_pre_brief.md`.
- The "feedback" is bullying, or part of a pattern requiring a formal response — route to HR or `domain-legal/employment-labor/`.
- You need to process the emotional aftermath rather than handle the conversation — `domain-personal-development/prompts/resilience/resilience_criticism_processing.md`.

**Audience:** Anyone receiving difficult feedback — individuals, managers, executives, and founders.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The feedback.** What was said, as exactly as you can recall or anticipate.
2. **Who gave it.** Their role, their vantage point, and what they can and cannot see.
3. **How it was delivered.** Timing, tone, setting, and whether it was invited.
4. **Your reaction.** What you felt, physically and otherwise.
5. **Prior signal.** Whether you have heard anything like this before, from anyone.
6. **The relationship.** Whether this person has your interests in view.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Manage the **physiological reaction first**. Nothing else on this list is available while flooded.
- Ask for **specifics** before responding to substance. Most feedback arrives as a generalization, and a generalization cannot be acted on.
- Separate **content from delivery**. Badly delivered feedback can be entirely correct; well-delivered feedback can be wrong.
- Distinguish **what to act on** from what to note and from what to set aside — with a stated basis, not a feeling.
- Weigh **convergence**: feedback you have heard from more than one source deserves substantially more weight than a single view.
- Delay **evaluation**, not the conversation. Understand fully now; decide what is true later, when you can.
- Follow up. What converts a hard conversation into a changed relationship is what happens in the next fortnight.

### Must Not
- Explain. It is the strongest reflex and it ends the conversation — the person concludes the feedback did not land and stops elaborating, which costs you the specifics.
- Over-agree to end the discomfort. Rapid total acceptance closes the conversation as effectively as defending it, and it produces no change because nothing specific was understood.
- Debate the examples. Even if an example is wrong, disputing it moves the conversation to that example's accuracy and away from the pattern it was offered to illustrate.
- Dismiss content because delivery was poor. They are independent, and conflating them is the most common way real signal gets discarded.
- Accept everything. Uncritical acceptance is not humility; it is an abdication of judgment, and it makes you unpredictable to work with.
- Respond substantively while flooded. Anything said in that state is defence wearing the clothes of analysis.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Manage the reaction before anything else
Notice it: heat, tightness, a sudden narrowing of attention, the sentence already forming. That is arousal, not judgment, and it arrives before you have evaluated anything. Slow your breathing, and use the holding line: *"Let me take that in for a second."* A pause is unremarkable and it is the only thing that makes the rest possible. If it is severe, say so and ask for time: *"I want to give this proper thought rather than react — can we pick it up tomorrow?"*

### Step 2 — Ask for specifics
This is the highest-value move available and the one the reflexes crowd out. Most feedback arrives as a generalization that cannot be acted on. The questions:
- *"Can you give me a specific example?"*
- *"When did you most recently see that?"*
- *"What did it look like from where you were sitting?"*
- *"What would you have wanted me to do instead, in that moment?"*

The last is the most useful single question in receiving feedback. It converts a criticism into a described alternative, and it frequently reveals that the person's actual concern is narrower than their phrasing.

### Step 3 — Do not debate the examples
When an example comes, resist correcting it — even where you have a good factual objection. Disputing an example moves the conversation onto whether that instance was accurate and away from the pattern it was offered to illustrate, and it signals that further examples will be litigated. If an example is genuinely wrong in a way that matters, note it and return to it after the conversation.

### Step 4 — Separate content from delivery
If the feedback was delivered badly — in public, in anger, with a characterization of you rather than your behaviour — hold two things at once: the delivery was poor, **and** the content may be entirely correct. These are independent, and collapsing them is the most common route by which real signal is discarded. The delivery is a separate conversation, worth having later and separately, and it is not a reason to discard the content now.

### Step 5 — Reflect back and confirm
Before responding to substance, say what you have understood: *"So what I'm hearing is that in the design reviews, I move to a decision before people have finished making their case, and it means some of them stop contributing. Is that right?"* This does three things: it confirms you understood, it gives them a chance to correct, and it demonstrates you were listening rather than preparing — which is what makes them willing to do this again.

### Step 6 — Delay evaluation, not understanding
You do not have to decide in the room whether the feedback is true. Say so honestly: *"I need to sit with this. Some of it I recognize, some I want to think about."* This is not deflection — it is the difference between processing and reacting, and it is far better than either instant agreement or instant defence. Set a specific time to come back with a response.

### Step 7 — Decide what to act on
Afterward, sort it with a stated basis rather than a feeling:

| Weight | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Act on it** | Heard from more than one source, or from someone with a clear view of the behaviour, and specific enough to change |
| **Note and watch** | Single source, plausible, but you have not seen it yourself — look for it deliberately |
| **Set aside** | Inconsistent with everything else you know, from a limited vantage point, or about someone else's preference rather than your effectiveness |

Convergence is the strongest signal available. The same feedback from three unconnected people is very likely true regardless of how it feels; the same feedback from one person with a partial view may not be.

### Step 8 — Follow up
Within two weeks, go back: what you have thought about, what you are changing specifically, and — if you are not acting on part of it — say that honestly rather than letting it appear you agreed and did nothing. Then ask them to tell you if they see it again. This is what converts a hard conversation into a changed relationship, and it is what makes the person willing to be honest with you next time, which is worth considerably more than the feedback itself.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Did you explain, deflect, or over-agree at any point — and what did it cost you in specifics?
- Are you discarding content because the delivery was poor?
- If three people said this, would you still be evaluating it, or would you be acting?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Explaining.** The dominant reflex. It reads as rejection regardless of intent, the person stops elaborating, and you lose exactly the specifics that would have made the feedback useful.
2. **Over-agreement.** Accepting everything immediately to end the discomfort. It closes the conversation as effectively as defending, produces no change because nothing specific was understood, and is frequently mistaken for maturity by the person doing it.
3. **Example litigation.** Disputing whether an instance happened as described. It relocates the conversation onto that instance's accuracy and away from the pattern, and it teaches the person that examples will be contested.
4. **Delivery-content collapse.** Dismissing valid feedback because it was delivered badly. The two are independent; this is the most common way genuine signal is thrown away with a legitimate grievance attached.
5. **Flooded response.** Answering substantively while physiologically aroused. Whatever is said is defence in analytical clothing, and it is usually the part that gets remembered.
6. **Uncritical acceptance.** Acting on all feedback regardless of source or convergence. It is not humility; it is abdicated judgment, and it makes your behaviour a function of whoever spoke to you most recently.
7. **Silent non-action.** Appearing to accept feedback and then doing nothing. It is worse than disagreeing openly, because it costs the person's willingness to be honest with you again.
8. **Single-source over-weighting.** Treating one person's view from a partial vantage point as established. Weight by convergence and by whether the person can actually see the behaviour they are describing.

## Verification

- [ ] Physiological reaction addressed before any substantive response.
- [ ] Holding line prepared, with a time-request script for severe cases.
- [ ] Specifics requested with at least three questions, including what-should-I-have-done-instead.
- [ ] No example debated during the conversation.
- [ ] Content assessed independently of delivery quality.
- [ ] Poor delivery routed to a separate later conversation rather than used to discard content.
- [ ] Understanding reflected back and confirmed before responding to substance.
- [ ] Evaluation explicitly deferred with a date to return, not deflected.
- [ ] Feedback sorted act / note / set aside with a stated basis including convergence.
- [ ] Follow-up planned within two weeks, including honest statement of what is not being actioned.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests for the three reflexes and for delivery-content conflation.
- [ ] No substantive response given while flooded.
- [ ] No blanket acceptance recorded as humility.
