# Saying No Upward — Declining a Request From Someone With Power Over You

**Objective:** Declining a request from someone with authority over you produces two characteristic failures, and the first is far more common. **Agreeing to something undeliverable** feels like the safe answer in the moment and is the expensive one: the failure arrives later, is attributed to you, and costs more credibility than the refusal would have. **Flat refusal** is rarer and reads as unwillingness rather than capacity, which is a judgment about character rather than about workload. The route between them is that "no" is usually the wrong frame entirely. Most of what gets called saying no is really one of four different statements — *not possible*, *not without a trade*, *not by me*, *not without changing something else* — and three of those are not refusals but decisions that belong to the person asking. This prompt classifies which you are actually making, builds the trade-off framing that hands the decision back with its costs visible, and prepares wording that survives being repeated to someone more senior.

`difficultconvo_pre_brief.md` prepares hard conversations generally. This is the specific case where the power asymmetry runs against you.

**When to use:**
- You have been asked to take on something you cannot deliver alongside existing commitments.
- A request would require compromising quality, a commitment, or a boundary you hold.
- You have said yes too often and need to reset without a confrontation.
- You need to decline a client, a senior stakeholder, or your manager's manager.

**When NOT to use:**
- The request is unethical, unlawful, or unsafe — that is a different conversation and may need escalation or `domain-legal/`.
- The relationship is personal — `domain-personal-development/prompts/relationships/relationships_boundary_setting_script.md`.
- You are competing for resources rather than declining work — `contexts/negotiation_internal_budget_headcount.md`.

**Audience:** Individuals, managers, and project leads declining a request from someone with authority over them.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The request.** What has been asked, by whom, and by when.
2. **Why you cannot do it as asked.** Capacity, capability, conflict with a commitment, or judgment.
3. **Existing commitments.** What is already promised, to whom, and by when.
4. **What the requester actually needs.** The outcome, as distinct from the task they proposed.
5. **The relationship.** Its history, and whether you have declined before.
6. **The real deadline.** Theirs, and whether it is genuine.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Classify which of the **four statements** you are making, because three of them are not refusals and framing them as refusals creates a problem that does not exist.
- Lead with the **outcome they want**, not with your constraint. It establishes that you are solving their problem rather than protecting yourself.
- Make the **trade-off explicit and hand the decision back**. You are not refusing; you are declining to make a prioritization decision that is theirs.
- Be **specific about existing commitments** — what, for whom, by when. "I'm busy" is unusable; a named commitment is a fact they can weigh.
- Offer an **alternative**, wherever one exists: a later date, a reduced version, a different owner, a different approach.
- Use wording that **survives being repeated**, since it will frequently be quoted to someone more senior, without your tone.
- Know when **compliance is right** — sometimes the correct answer is to absorb it, and knowing this protects the credibility of your declines.

### Must Not
- Say yes to something you cannot deliver. The failure is worse than the refusal, it arrives later, and it is attributed to you rather than to the ask.
- Decline without offering the trade-off. A bare no forces them to either accept or overrule, which is a worse position for both of you than a visible prioritization choice.
- Justify with generalized busyness. It is unverifiable, it sounds like reluctance, and every colleague could say it.
- Make it about willingness. The moment it reads as "I'd rather not," it becomes a question about attitude, which is far more damaging than a question about capacity.
- Over-apologize. Extended apology signals the refusal is negotiable and invites another attempt.
- Escalate to your own manager before responding. Handle it directly first; escalating first signals you cannot manage the relationship.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Classify what you are actually saying
Four different statements, three of which are not refusals:

| Statement | Means | Frame as |
|---|---|---|
| **Not possible** | Genuinely cannot be done by anyone in the time | A fact, with the reason |
| **Not without a trade** | Possible if something else moves | A prioritization decision for them |
| **Not by me** | Possible, but someone else is better placed | A routing suggestion |
| **Not without changing something** | Possible with a changed scope, date, or standard | An options conversation |

Only the first is a refusal. Getting this classification right removes most of the difficulty, because the other three hand a decision back rather than blocking a request.

### Step 2 — Establish what they actually need
Ask before answering. The task requested is frequently not the only route to the outcome wanted, and the outcome is often achievable in a way that does not have the problem the task has. *"Before I answer — what's driving the date on this, and what does it need to achieve?"* This is not deflection; it regularly produces a version that works for both of you and that neither had in mind.

### Step 3 — Lead with the outcome, not the constraint
Open on their problem, not yours: *"I want to make sure the board pack lands properly."* Then the constraint. Leading with your capacity — however true — frames the conversation around your limitations, and the frame is what determines whether this reads as a problem-solving conversation or a reluctance conversation.

### Step 4 — Make the trade-off explicit
The core move. Name what is already committed, specifically, and hand the choice back:

> *"I can take that on. To do it by Friday I'd need to push the migration testing to the following week — that's committed to [team] for their release. I can do either; which would you rather I prioritize?"*

This is not a refusal. It is a prioritization decision that belongs to them, made with its cost visible. It is also very hard to respond to badly, and it frequently produces the resource or the extension that a flat no would not have.

### Step 5 — Be specific about the commitments
"I'm at capacity" is unusable — it is unverifiable and every colleague could say it. Name the commitment: what, for whom, by when, and what happens if it slips. Specificity converts your constraint from a claim about your feelings into a fact they can weigh against the new request, which is exactly what you want them doing.

### Step 6 — Offer the alternative
Wherever one exists: a later date that works, a reduced version delivered on time, a different owner better placed, or a different approach that gets the outcome more cheaply. An alternative demonstrates you engaged with the problem, and it changes the conversation from whether you will help to how. Where there is genuinely no alternative, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.

### Step 7 — Word it to survive repetition
Assume your response will be quoted to someone more senior, in summary, without your tone. Write it so the quoted version still reads well: specific, constructive, unemotional, and focused on outcomes rather than on your feelings about the request. Avoid anything that reads as complaint, and avoid humour, which does not survive paraphrase. A useful test: would you be comfortable if this sentence appeared in an email thread you were not on?

### Step 8 — Know when to say yes
Sometimes compliance is right: the request is genuinely important, it is rare, the relationship warrants it, or the cost of declining exceeds the cost of absorbing it. Say yes clearly, without martyrdom or a ledger — a yes delivered with visible resentment costs more than a no. And note the frequency: an unbroken record of yes makes every future decline harder and less credible, while a pattern of considered, specific declines makes the occasional yes meaningful.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- If this response were forwarded to their manager, how would it read?
- Are you declining because you cannot, or because you would rather not — and is the answer honest?
- What happens if you say yes and cannot deliver, and who carries that?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The undeliverable yes.** Agreeing to avoid the moment. The failure arrives later, is more visible, and is attributed to you rather than to the ask — costing far more credibility than the decline would have.
2. **The bare no.** Declining without a trade-off, alternative, or reason. It forces the requester to accept or overrule, both of which are worse for you than a visible prioritization decision would have been.
3. **Generalized busyness.** "I'm swamped." Unverifiable, indistinguishable from reluctance, and available to everyone — so it carries no information and is treated as none.
4. **Willingness framing.** Anything that reads as "I'd rather not." It converts a capacity question into an attitude question, which is a far more damaging thing to have raised about you.
5. **Over-apology.** Extended apologizing. It signals the position is soft and invites a second attempt, usually with more pressure.
6. **Premature escalation.** Taking it to your own manager before responding directly. It signals you cannot handle the relationship, and it is remembered as such.
7. **Martyred compliance.** Saying yes while making the cost visible through resentment. It costs more than the no would have, and it makes the next genuine decline read as continuation of a grievance rather than as information.
8. **Unrepeatable wording.** A response that only works with your tone attached. It will be summarized to someone more senior, and the summary is what gets acted on.
