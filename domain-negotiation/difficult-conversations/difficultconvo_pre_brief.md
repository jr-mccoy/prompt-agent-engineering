# Difficult Conversation Pre-Brief — One-Page Prep Before You Walk In

**Objective:** Produce a one-page brief for a difficult conversation that someone is avoiding or dreading. The brief forces clarity where dread produces fog. It separates the conversation's actual goal from the relief-seeking impulse to "just get it over with." It names what the other person needs to hear, and why, in terms they can absorb. It names what the user needs to say — and specifically what they'd regret leaving unsaid. It pre-scripts a response to the worst plausible reply, so the user doesn't get knocked off course. It locks venue and timing (private, unhurried, no audience). And it commits the user, in advance, to specific actions *after* the conversation regardless of how it lands — because the most common failure is not the conversation itself but the abandonment of follow-through when it goes sideways.

**When to use:**
- Firing or laying someone off, or delivering a serious performance message.
- Ending a romantic relationship, friendship, or partnership.
- Naming a problem in an ongoing relationship that you've been avoiding.
- Delivering hard, unwelcome feedback.
- Declining a major request (a promotion ask, an investment, a favor with stakes).
- Raising a boundary that will be uncomfortable to assert.

**When NOT to use:**
- Conversations that are difficult only because of nerves but carry no real stakes or downside — over-preparing those can make them stiff.
- Live crisis conversations happening right now (this is a prep tool; use it before, not during).
- Routine feedback that fits in a sentence and doesn't warrant a brief.

**Audience:** Managers, executives, founders, and individuals who have a hard conversation coming and want to walk in clear rather than improvise through dread.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The conversation.** Who it's with, the relationship, and what makes it difficult.
2. **What's happened.** The facts/events leading here, stated without spin.
3. **What you've been avoiding.** The thing you most don't want to say. (Often the actual point.)
4. **The other person.** How they tend to react under stress (deflect, attack, shut down, negotiate, plead).
5. **Constraints.** Anything that limits the conversation — legal, timing, things you can't promise.
6. **Your fear.** What you're most afraid will happen. Naming it defuses some of its power and reveals what to prep for.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- State the conversation's **actual goal** in one sentence — what's true/agreed/changed when it's done well. "Get it over with" is not a goal; it's an escape.
- Name **what the other person needs to hear**, and why, phrased so they can take it in rather than only defend against it. Lead with the message, not the wind-up.
- Name **what you need to say**, including the one thing you'd **regret not saying**. Surface it now so dread doesn't bury it.
- Script the **opening line** verbatim — clear, kind, and not buried under throat-clearing.
- Pre-script your **response to the worst plausible reply** (tears, anger, "you're making a mistake," a counter-offer, total silence). One prepared move per worst-case.
- Specify **venue and timing**: private, unhurried, no audience, with enough time to not rush the ending.
- Commit to **post-conversation actions regardless of outcome** — what you do/send/follow up on whether it goes well or badly. This is where good intentions usually fail.

### Must Not
- Soften the core message into vagueness to manage your own discomfort. Kindness is in the delivery, not in obscuring the point.
- Open with so much cushioning that the person doesn't realize a hard message is coming until they're blindsided mid-way.
- Script the conversation to win an argument. The goal is to deliver truth and preserve dignity, not to be proven right.
- Make promises in the moment to escape discomfort (an extension, a reversal, a vague "let's see") that you'll regret.
- Leave the after undefined. A difficult conversation with no follow-through commitment tends to unravel.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Name the actual goal
One sentence: what is true, agreed, decided, or changed when this conversation has gone well? Reject "get it over with," "make them understand I'm right," and "avoid them being upset." Those are not goals.

### Step 2 — Name the avoided thing
Write the sentence you least want to say. This is usually the load-bearing content. If the brief tiptoes around it, the conversation will too.

### Step 3 — What they need to hear, and why
State the core message in one or two plain sentences, phrased so the person can absorb it. Note *why* they need to hear it (so they can decide / fix / move on / understand). Decide where it goes — generally early, after a brief humane frame, not buried at the end.

### Step 4 — What you need to say, including the regret-test
List your key points. Then apply the regret test: if you walked out having not said one thing, what would it be? Mark that point as required.

### Step 5 — Script the opening line
Verbatim. Warm enough to be humane, clear enough that the person knows a real conversation is happening. Avoid both the cold cliché ("we need to talk") and the endless cushion.

### Step 6 — Prep for the worst reply
Identify the worst plausible reaction given Input 4. For each (pick 1–3), write your prepared response — how you hold the message with compassion without retracting it, escalating, or making a panic-promise.

### Step 7 — Set venue and timing
Private. Unhurried (don't schedule it before a hard stop). No audience. In person where the stakes warrant it. Note any practical detail (who else might be around, whether a follow-up is needed in writing).

### Step 8 — Commit to the after
Write the specific actions you'll take after, *regardless of how it goes*: the follow-up message, the documentation, the repair gesture, the boundary you'll hold, the thing you won't take back. Tie at least one to "even if it goes badly."

### Step 9 — Assemble the one-page brief
Compress all of the above onto one page. It should be glanceable in the thirty seconds before you walk in.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Goal-as-escape.** "My goal is to get through it." That's avoidance wearing a goal's clothes. Force a goal stated as an outcome.
2. **Message-softening.** Blurring the core message to ease your discomfort, leaving the person genuinely unsure what was said. Kindness lives in delivery, not vagueness.
3. **Cushion-burial.** So much preamble that the hard message arrives as a blindside. Frame briefly, then deliver.
4. **Win-the-argument framing.** Prepping to be proven right rather than to deliver truth with dignity. The other person's agreement is not the goal.
5. **Panic-promise.** Caving to a counter-offer or making a vague promise in the moment to escape discomfort. Pre-decide what you will and won't offer.
6. **Undefined after.** Ending the brief at the conversation. The follow-through is where it usually fails; commit to it now.
7. **Audience / rush.** Scheduling it in a semi-public place or before a hard stop, so it gets cut short or performed for onlookers. Lock private and unhurried.
8. **Regret omission.** Skipping the regret test and walking out having not said the one thing that mattered most.

## Output Format

```
# Difficult Conversation Brief — [with whom], [when]

## Actual goal (one sentence)
[What's true / agreed / changed when this goes well.]

## The thing I've been avoiding saying
[The sentence I least want to say — usually the point.]

## What they need to hear (and why)
- Core message: [1–2 plain sentences]
- Why they need it: [so they can ...]
- Where it goes: [early, after brief frame]

## What I need to say
- [point]
- [point]
- ** Regret-test (required): [the one thing I'd regret not saying] **

## Opening line (verbatim)
"[...]"

## Worst-reply prep
| Their worst reaction | My prepared response (hold message, don't retract/escalate/panic-promise) |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| [...] | [...] |

## Venue & timing
- Where: [private]
- When: [unhurried, no hard stop after]
- No audience: [confirmed]

## Post-conversation commitments (regardless of outcome)
- [Action] — even if it goes well
- [Action] — even if it goes badly
- What I will NOT take back / promise away: [...]
```

---

## Verification

- [ ] The goal is stated as an outcome, not "get it over with."
- [ ] The avoided sentence is written down explicitly.
- [ ] The core message is plain, absorbable, and placed early.
- [ ] The regret test is applied and the required point flagged.
- [ ] The opening line is verbatim, humane, and clear.
- [ ] At least one worst-reply scenario has a prepared, non-retracting response.
- [ ] Venue and timing are private and unhurried.
- [ ] Post-conversation commitments include at least one "even if it goes badly" action.
- [ ] No message-softening, win-the-argument framing, or undefined after.
- [ ] The whole brief fits glanceably on one page.
