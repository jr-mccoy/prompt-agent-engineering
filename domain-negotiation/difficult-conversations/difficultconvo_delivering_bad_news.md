# Delivering Bad News You Did Not Decide — Layoffs, Cancellations, and Denied Requests

**Objective:** Carrying someone else's decision to the person it affects is one of the hardest conversations in working life, and it is made worse by well-meant instincts. The instinct to soften produces a preamble that leaves the person waiting for a blow they can already sense. The instinct to be liked produces hedging that implies the decision might still move, which is a cruelty dressed as kindness — it costs them the chance to start adjusting and guarantees a second, worse conversation. The instinct to distance yourself produces "this wasn't my call," which abandons the organization in a way the person will remember and which helps them not at all. This prompt handles the specific case where the decision is **made, final, and not yours**: leading with the news, being honest about your own position without either lying or disowning, absorbing the reaction without reopening the substance, and providing the practical support that in most cases matters more than anything said.

`difficultconvo_pre_brief.md` prepares hard conversations generally, including ones where you decided and where an outcome is still open. This one is specifically for a final decision you are carrying.

**When to use:**
- Communicating a redundancy, cancellation, rejection, or denied request that you did not decide.
- Telling someone a project they have invested in is stopping.
- Delivering a decision you disagree with.
- Preparing a manager on your team to deliver news to theirs.

**When NOT to use:**
- The decision is still open, or you are consulting rather than informing — do not use a bad-news frame for a live conversation.
- You made the decision — you can still use this, but you must own it fully rather than describe it.
- The conversation is a negotiation — use the rest of the domain.
- The news relates to conduct or performance requiring a formal process — route to HR or `domain-legal/employment-labor/`.

**Audience:** Managers, executives, HR partners, founders, and individuals who have to tell someone something final and unwelcome.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The news.** What has been decided, precisely.
2. **Who decided, and why.** What you actually know about the reasoning.
3. **What is genuinely final.** And what, if anything, is still open.
4. **Your own position.** Whether you agree, and what you can honestly say about it.
5. **Practical consequences.** Timing, money, process, and what happens next for them.
6. **The person.** What this means for them specifically, and how they are likely to respond.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- **Lead with the news**, in the first two sentences. Everything else follows it.
- State plainly what is **final**, so the person does not spend energy on a door that is closed.
- Be honest about your own position **without disowning the organization**. There is a truthful middle between "I fought this" and "I agree completely."
- Give the **reasoning you actually know**, and say clearly when you do not know rather than inventing a rationale.
- Provide the **practical detail** — timing, money, process, next steps — because it is frequently what the person most needs and least gets.
- Let them **react without managing the reaction**. Silence, anger, and tears are all normal, and none of them require a response.
- Say **what happens next**, concretely, including what you will do and by when.

### Must Not
- Bury the news in preamble. The person senses it immediately, and the softening becomes something they endure while waiting.
- Imply the decision might change when it will not. It is the most damaging kindness available: it costs them time to adjust and guarantees a second conversation that is worse.
- Say "this wasn't my decision" as a way of stepping outside it. It helps them not at all, it is remembered, and it tells them you will do the same to someone else.
- Invent a rationale you do not have. Fabricated reasoning is usually detected, and it converts a hard conversation into a distrusted one.
- Over-explain. Extended justification is for your discomfort, not their understanding, and it invites a debate about reasoning that cannot change the outcome.
- Ask them to manage your feelings about delivering it. Their reaction is not an occasion for your distress.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Establish what is final and what you know
Before the conversation, write three things: what has been decided precisely, what is genuinely still open (often nothing, sometimes timing or terms), and what you actually know about the reasoning. Be clear with yourself about the boundary between what you know and what you are inferring — you will be asked, and the answer needs to be honest.

### Step 2 — Decide what you can honestly say about your own view
This is the hardest preparation. There is an honest middle between disowning and endorsing:
- If you agree: say so simply, without over-justifying.
- If you disagree but the decision is made: *"I'm not going to pretend I'd have reached the same decision. It has been made, and I'll be straight with you about what it means."* This is honest, does not disown, and does not invite them to campaign.
- If you cannot say: *"I'm not going to get into my own view — what I can do is be straight with you about what happens now."*

What you must not do is imply you fought it in order to be liked, or claim agreement you do not feel. Both are detected, and both cost you.

### Step 3 — Write the opening two sentences
The news, first, plainly, with no preamble:

> *"I've got difficult news. The decision has been made to close the project, and your role is affected. I want to walk you through what that means."*

No weather, no "how are you," no "as you know, things have been challenging." The person already knows something is wrong from the fact of the meeting; a preamble does not delay the blow, it extends the anticipation of it.

### Step 4 — State the finality clearly
Say explicitly what is closed: *"This is decided — I'm not here to consult on it."* Then say precisely what remains open, if anything: timing, transition, support, references, what they work on until a date. Being clear about the boundary lets them redirect their energy from contesting the decision to shaping what is actually shapeable, which is where their remaining influence is.

### Step 5 — Give the reasoning you have, and stop
Explain what you know, briefly. Then, if you do not know more, say so: *"I don't know the full reasoning, and I'm not going to guess at it."* This is far better received than an invented rationale. Resist the pull to keep explaining — extended justification serves your discomfort and invites a debate about reasons that cannot alter the outcome.

### Step 6 — Give the practical detail
Frequently the most valuable part of the conversation, and frequently the thinnest. Cover: dates, money, process, what they do between now and then, what happens to their work, what you will say to others and when, references, and who they contact for what. Have it written and give them a copy — people retain very little after news like this, and a document they can read afterward is a genuine kindness.

### Step 7 — Absorb the reaction
Whatever comes — silence, anger, tears, argument, questions you cannot answer — let it happen. Do not fill silence. Do not defend the decision against anger; acknowledge it instead: *"That's a fair thing to be angry about."* Do not treat argument as something to win — the decision is not moving, and saying so once is enough. If they need to stop, stop, and offer a second conversation. See `at-the-table/negotiation_emotional_flooding_at_the_table.md` for the mechanics.

### Step 8 — Close with concrete next steps and follow through
End with what happens next: what you will do, by when, and when you will speak again. Then actually do it. The gap between a decent delivery and a genuinely decent handling is almost entirely in the follow-through — the promised introduction, the reference written promptly, the check-in a week later. That is what the person remembers, and it is what everyone watching learns about how the organization behaves.

### Step 9 — Adversarial check
- Would they be able to state accurately what is decided and what is open, from what you said?
- Did you say anything to make yourself feel better that costs them something?
- What did you promise, and will you actually do it?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **The preamble.** Opening with context, small talk, or general difficulty. It does not soften the news; it extends the anticipation and adds the memory of enduring the run-up.
2. **False hope.** Hedging so the decision sounds contestable. It is the most damaging thing on this list — it costs them the time to begin adjusting, and it makes the second conversation, when they discover it was final, considerably worse.
3. **Disowning.** "This wasn't my call." It gives them nothing, it tells them you will step outside a decision when it is uncomfortable, and it is remembered by everyone who hears it repeated.
4. **Invented reasoning.** Supplying a rationale you do not actually have. It is usually detected, and it converts a painful but trusted conversation into a distrusted one.
5. **Over-explanation.** Continuing to justify. It serves your discomfort, and it invites a debate about reasoning that cannot change anything while implying it might.
6. **Reaction management.** Trying to move the person out of anger or tears. The reaction is not a problem to be solved, and attempting to solve it reads as wanting the conversation to be over.
7. **Emotional reversal.** Making your difficulty in delivering the news part of the conversation. However genuinely hard it is, it asks them to manage you at the worst possible moment.
8. **Thin practicalities.** Delivering the news fully and the logistics vaguely. Dates, money, and process are what they actually need, and what they will find they cannot remember an hour later.

## Verification

- [ ] The news appears in the first two sentences, with no preamble.
- [ ] Finality stated explicitly, with what remains shapeable named separately.
- [ ] Own position prepared as honest without disowning or false endorsement.
- [ ] Reasoning limited to what is actually known, with an explicit "I don't know" script.
- [ ] A stop point set for justification.
- [ ] Practical detail written out and prepared as a copy to hand over.
- [ ] Reaction plan covers silence, anger, argument, and needing to stop.
- [ ] Concrete next steps with dates and named follow-through commitments.
- [ ] Adversarial check tests whether they could accurately restate what is decided.
- [ ] No hedging that implies the decision might change.
- [ ] No "this wasn't my decision" as a distancing move.
- [ ] No invented rationale.
