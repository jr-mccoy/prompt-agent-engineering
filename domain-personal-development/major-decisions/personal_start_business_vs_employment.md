# Start a Business vs. Stay Employed

**Objective:** Structure the choice between starting a business and taking (or keeping) a job — by separating the tradeoffs that are real from the ones that are romanticized on both sides, testing your genuine risk tolerance against the actual downside rather than the imagined one, and giving full weight to the staged, reversible middle path. The output is one of three: start now, stay employed, or stage it — each with a tripwire and a defined fallback.

**When to use:**
- Deciding whether to leave a job to start (or go full-time on) a business.
- Choosing between a job offer and going out on your own.
- You have a business idea and keep oscillating between "I should just do it" and "I can't afford to."
- Weighing whether to keep a side project as a side project or make it the main thing.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want the mechanics of incorporating, funding, or the business plan itself — that's a different task; this decides whether to leap, not how to build.
- The real question is whether to quit a specific bad job irrespective of what's next — use `personal_quit_or_persist.md`.
- You're evaluating a specific job offer on its own terms — use `personal_career_offer_evaluation.md`.

**Audience:** An individual (or a couple, when the decision affects shared finances) deciding on their own path. Not for advising someone else. The financial and legal specifics belong to a fee-only advisor and an attorney — see the constraints. If the pressure around this decision becomes persistent distress, this is not a substitute for professional support — see `domain-psychology/`.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The two options as they actually stand.** The business (idea, stage, any traction or revenue) and the employment (current job, offer, or the realistic job you could get).
2. **Financial state.** Savings, monthly burn, dependents, debt, and how many months you could go with zero business income.
3. **The downside floor.** What actually happens if the business fails — how far do you fall, and can you get back to employment, and how fast?
4. **Risk tolerance evidence.** Not what you claim — how have you actually behaved under financial and career uncertainty before?
5. **What's pulling each way.** The specific pulls toward the business and toward the job, stated plainly.

If the business has no traction and no validation, note it: "start now" against zero evidence is a much riskier call than staging toward a validation milestone.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Separate **real tradeoffs** (income variance, runway, downside floor, autonomy, benefits, identity) from **imagined ones** (romanticized freedom, imagined wealth, fear-of-the-leap, "employees are trapped" / "founders are reckless" tropes).
- Test risk tolerance against the *actual modeled downside*, not a vague sense of being "a risk-taker" or "risk-averse."
- Model the downside floor concretely: what the worst realistic outcome is, and the path and speed of re-employment from it.
- Give the staged/reversible option full and equal consideration — building to a validation milestone while employed is often the highest-expected-value path and is systematically under-weighed by people who frame this as a binary leap.
- Route financial-structuring and legal specifics to a fee-only advisor and an attorney; keep this at the decision level.
- End with start-now / stay-employed / stage-it, plus a tripwire and a re-employment fallback.

### Must Not
- Romanticize entrepreneurship (freedom, being your own boss) or denigrate employment (safe but soul-crushing) — both are loaded framings, not analysis.
- Treat the decision as a binary leap when a staged path exists. The most reversible option that preserves the upside usually deserves to win.
- Confuse the appeal of *not being at the current job* with the appeal of *running a business*. Those are different, and conflating them is a classic error.
- Use expected value alone. At the individual level, the survivability of the downside matters more than the average outcome.
- Let identity ("I'm a founder / I'd never work for someone") drive the call. Sunk identity is as irrelevant as sunk cost.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Split real tradeoffs from imagined ones
Two columns. For each pull you listed, sort it:

| Real tradeoff (has an observable consequence) | Imagined / loaded framing (a story, not a consequence) |
|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Income variance, runway, benefits, downside floor, autonomy over work, ceiling on upside, identity cost | "Freedom," "be my own boss," "get rich," "employees are trapped," "founders are reckless," fear of the leap |

Anything in the right column gets named and set aside; it doesn't enter the scoring.

### Step 2 — Model the downside floor
- Worst realistic outcome of the business (not worst-imaginable, not best-case): burns $X over Y months, produces Z.
- After failure: can you return to employment? At what level, comp, and how many months of search?
- How far do you actually fall — and is the floor survivable (you recover) or catastrophic (debt, dependents, no re-entry)?

### Step 3 — Test risk tolerance against the modeled downside
- Given the Step 2 floor, not a vague self-image: could you live through it? Would the people who depend on you?
- How have you actually behaved under past financial/career uncertainty (input 4)? Behavior, not self-description.
- Where the modeled downside exceeds your true tolerance, the honest answer is stage-it or don't — not "be braver."

### Step 4 — Weigh the staged / reversible path
Lay out the middle explicitly:
- Build the business on the side to a **validation milestone** (first paying customers, revenue covering X% of burn, a signed contract) *before* leaving the paycheck.
- What milestone would de-risk the leap? How long to reach it while employed? What does the job cost you in time/energy toward it?
- This path trades speed for a dramatically better downside floor. Score it as a first-class option, not a compromise.

### Step 5 — Compare the three paths
For start-now, stay-employed, and stage-it: expected outcome, downside floor, reversibility, and fit with true risk tolerance. Use the same evidence for all three; don't score the exciting one on hope and the safe one on tedium.

### Step 6 — The call, tripwire, and fallback
One of three:
- **Start now** — the downside floor is survivable, risk tolerance genuinely covers it, and the cost of delay (a closing window, a co-founder, a time-sensitive opportunity) outweighs the value of staging.
- **Stay employed** — the business lacks validation and the downside floor exceeds tolerance, or the pull is really away-from-the-job rather than toward-the-business.
- **Stage it** — the business is promising but unvalidated; a milestone reached while employed converts the leap from a gamble into a de-risked step.

Attach a **tripwire** (a runway floor, or a date/milestone at which you either commit or re-employ) and a **re-employment fallback** (how you'd get back to a paycheck, how fast). Write a calibration anchor.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Romanticized freedom.** "Being my own boss" is a feeling, not a tradeoff. Running a business trades one set of constraints (a manager) for others (customers, cash flow, everything landing on you). Score consequences, not the fantasy.
2. **Away-from vs. toward.** Wanting out of the current job is not the same as wanting to run a business. If the pull is escape, a different job may dominate both options — check `personal_quit_or_persist.md`.
3. **Expected-value seduction.** A high average outcome with a catastrophic, non-survivable tail is worse for an individual than a modest outcome you can live through. Survivability first.
4. **Risk-tolerance self-flattery.** "I'm a risk-taker" rarely survives contact with the modeled downside and a dependent. Use past behavior, not self-image.
5. **Binary-leap framing.** Treating it as quit-or-don't ignores the staged path that usually has the best risk-adjusted profile. The absence of the middle option in the framing is itself the error.
6. **Validation skipped.** "Start now" against a business with zero traction is a bet on a story. A validation milestone is cheap insurance against an expensive year.
7. **Identity as the driver.** "Real founders just jump" / "I could never work for someone" are identity statements, not analysis. They belong in Step 1's right column.
8. **Benefits invisibility.** Health insurance, retirement matching, and stable cash flow are real compensation that the "employees are trapped" framing quietly deletes. Count them.
