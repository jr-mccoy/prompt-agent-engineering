# Family Planning Tradeoffs

**Objective:** Structure the tradeoffs involved in major family planning decisions without prescribing the outcome. These decisions are among the most consequential and deeply personal a person or couple faces, and they are also among the most poorly structured — driven by competing social pressures, biological urgency, partner dynamics, and identity questions that rarely surface clearly in conversation. This prompt walks each dimension explicitly, surfaces what each option costs and what it gives, and identifies the conversations that need to happen and with whom.

**When to use:**
- Deciding on timing of a first child.
- Deciding whether to have additional children.
- Geographic decisions that significantly affect family structure (proximity to parents, childcare access).
- Navigating divergent career trajectories for two partners with family in scope.
- Beginning to plan for aging parent support and how it interacts with other plans.

**When NOT to use:**
- The decision is already made and implementation is the question.
- Medical decisions about fertility treatment (those require medical consultation as the primary input, not decision structure).
- The user is in acute crisis; stabilize first.

**Audience:** Individuals or couples facing a family planning decision with multiple dimensions in play, where structuring the tradeoffs would help more than more conversation.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The specific question.** What are you actually deciding? (Timing of first child, a second child, staying near vs. moving away from family, how to handle one partner's career opportunity with a child in the picture, aging parent situation and options.)
2. **Current situation.** Ages, relationship status, geography, careers and their current trajectory, existing children if any, financial state.
3. **The competing pulls.** What are the arguments for each option, as you currently see them?
4. **What you haven't wanted to look at.** Often there's a dimension users know is there but have been avoiding.
5. **Partner alignment status.** Are you aligned? Discussing? Not yet discussed?

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Walk all six dimensions: biological/health considerations, career timing, financial readiness, geography of support, partner alignment, identity/values alignment.
- For each option, state explicitly: what this option costs, and what this option gives. Both sides.
- Surface optionality across life stages — what each choice keeps open or forecloses at 5, 10, and 20 years.
- Identify the conversations that need to happen and with whom (partner, medical provider, parents, employer).
- Address partner alignment: not "are you aligned in theory" but "what specifically has been said and what is the gap."
- Be direct about time-sensitivity where it exists (biological fertility windows are real; career windows are real) without manufacturing urgency that isn't there.

### Must Not
- Prescribe the "right" answer on whether to have children, how many, or when. The user decides; the prompt structures.
- Minimize the identity dimension. Family planning decisions are partly about who you are and want to become, not just logistics.
- Treat "we'll figure it out" as an answer to financial or support-system questions when planning is possible.
- Skip aging parent implications if they're in scope — this is one of the most commonly deferred conversations.
- Assume partner alignment exists because there's been no explicit disagreement. Elicit it.
- Treat social timeline pressure ("everyone our age is doing it") as evidence about what the right timing is for this person.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Restate and clarify the decision
What exactly is being decided, and by when does it need to be decided? Note which aspects are time-sensitive (biological, career, legal) and which have more latitude.

### Step 2 — Biological and health considerations
- Age and biological fertility window, if relevant. State plainly without softening.
- Health factors that affect timing or options.
- Medical inputs that should inform the decision (and whether they've been obtained).
- Genetic considerations, if any.

This section has factual components that don't bend to preference. Name them clearly.

### Step 3 — Career timing
For each person:
- Where is each person in their career trajectory? At what point does a break or shift have the most / least impact?
- What's the opportunity cost of a career disruption now vs. in 2 years?
- What does the employer or industry actually accommodate vs. what it claims to accommodate?
- Is there a near-term career event (promotion, vesting, transition) that significantly changes the calculus?
- Which partner's career can flex more, and what's the cost of that flexibility?

### Step 4 — Financial readiness
- Cost of childcare in your area, for as many children as relevant.
- Income impact of any parental leave taken.
- One-income scenario: is it possible? For how long? At what lifestyle impact?
- Emergency fund adequacy for a higher-expense period.
- Housing: does current or planned housing work, or is a move implied?
- Financial cost of supporting aging parents if that's in scope.

### Step 5 — Geography of support
- Who is geographically available to help (parents, siblings, close friends)?
- What is the realistic frequency and nature of that help?
- What would it cost to purchase equivalent support (childcare, eldercare)?
- What's the cost/benefit of moving closer to family vs. staying where you are for career and lifestyle?

### Step 6 — Partner alignment
Ask and answer explicitly:
- Does each partner want the same outcome on the core question?
- If yes: is the agreement on timeline, method, and division of responsibility also aligned, or just the headline?
- If not: what's the actual gap? Is it resolvable, and on what timeline?
- What conversations have happened? What hasn't been said directly?
- Who is leading the decision and who is following — and does that match each person's actual preference?

### Step 7 — Identity and values
- What kind of life do you want, and how does each option fit or not?
- What do you believe about the role of family, work, and self in a well-lived life — and does each option honor or violate that?
- What would you regret at 70 that you can avoid regret on now?
- Are social or family expectations influencing what you think you want vs. what you actually want?

### Step 8 — Optionality across life stages
For each option:
- What's still possible at 5 years if you choose this?
- What's harder or foreclosed?
- At 10 years?
- At 20 years?

Reversibility: which decisions are reversible (career pauses can often be recovered; biological fertility windows cannot).

### Step 9 — Surface the conversations
- What conversation needs to happen with your partner that hasn't happened?
- What conversation needs to happen with your employer?
- What conversation needs to happen with your parents (either as childcare resource or as care recipients)?
- What medical input do you need before this decision can be made well?

### Step 10 — What each option costs and gives
For each option under consideration:
- What does this option cost? (Opportunity costs, sacrifices, what you're giving up.)
- What does this option give? (What you gain, what becomes possible.)
- State both sides honestly. Don't minimize the costs to make the preferred option look cleaner.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Social timeline pressure.** "Most people our age are doing this" doesn't tell you what the right timing is for your situation. Separate what others are doing from what works for you.
2. **Partner-alignment assumption.** The absence of open disagreement is not alignment. Elicit positions directly.
3. **Financial underestimation.** People consistently underestimate the cost of childcare and the income impact of career pauses. Model it.
4. **Support system overestimation.** "Family will help" often means less than people expect. Ask specifically: who, how often, for what, under what conditions.
5. **Biological window minimization.** The window is real. Softening the timeline to avoid discomfort doesn't extend it.
6. **Identity question avoidance.** "I just need to figure out the logistics" — usually the logistics are clear and the identity question is what's hard. Don't let logistics-focus substitute.
7. **Career-sacrifice asymmetry.** One partner's career is often implicitly assumed to be the one that flexes. Name the assumption explicitly and ask whether it reflects both partners' values.
8. **Aging parent deferral.** "We'll deal with that when we get to it" — aging parent support has planning lead times. Don't let it stay deferred if it's relevant.
9. **Reversibility overestimation.** "We can always change course" — fertility doesn't wait, and some career paths close. Be accurate about what's genuinely reversible.
