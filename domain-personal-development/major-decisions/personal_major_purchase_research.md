# Major Purchase Research

**Objective:** Walk through the research process for a major purchase systematically, from clarifying the underlying job to establishing a target offer and walk-away criteria. The prompt forces you to separate what the purchase needs to do from what you'd like it to do, computes true total cost of ownership (not sticker), and builds a structured comparison before any money moves. Ends with concrete decision criteria so the purchase decision is made before you're standing in a showroom or talking to a seller.

**When to use:**
- Purchase represents one or more months of net income (house, vehicle, durable appliance, boat, equipment, significant electronics).
- You're researching before committing, not mid-negotiation under pressure.
- Multiple options exist and you're not sure how to compare them.
- You suspect you're being influenced by the first option you encountered.

**When NOT to use:**
- The purchase is already contractually committed — different analysis needed.
- The decision is primarily financial (covered in `personal_financial_decision_framework.md`).
- Emergency replacement where speed, not optimization, is the goal.

**Audience:** Individuals or couples making a high-stakes purchase where the research phase determines whether the result is satisfying or regretted.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The item and the candidates.** What are you considering buying? What specific models, options, or offerings are you comparing (even if the list is incomplete)?
2. **Current state and why it's insufficient.** What do you have now, or what absence is this purchase filling? What specifically isn't working?
3. **Budget boundary.** Not "what I hope to spend" but "the amount above which I will not go, including all acquisition costs." State it as a hard ceiling.
4. **Time pressure.** Are you in a forced-purchase window (lease ending, device broken, moving) or researching without urgency?
5. **What you've already considered or ruled out.** So we don't re-tread ground you've already covered.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Clarify the underlying job before evaluating options — what the purchase must accomplish that the current state doesn't.
- Compute TCO for each candidate, not just sticker price.
- Identify 5–10 attributes that actually matter for this decision; score each candidate per attribute.
- Distinguish deal-killer criteria (attributes where any candidate that fails is eliminated) from preference criteria (where tradeoffs are acceptable).
- Assess market timing: is this a good time to buy, or is there reason to wait?
- Produce explicit walk-away criteria (the price above which you leave, the attributes below which you don't buy regardless of price).
- Identify who to learn from vs. who is selling. Separate their incentives.

### Must Not
- Anchor the comparison to the first option seen or encountered. The first option shapes perception of all others — correct for this explicitly.
- Treat monthly payment as equivalent to total cost. Monthly payment obscures financing cost, opportunity cost, and TCO.
- Conflate wants with jobs. "I want a truck" is a want; "I need to haul X at least twice monthly" is a job. Wants can be satisfied by options that don't fulfill the job.
- Skip TCO. The purchase price is the smallest number for many durable goods (maintenance, insurance, fuel, depreciation, financing dwarf sticker in some categories).
- Reduce the decision to a single attribute (usually the most visible or the cheapest). Use the attribute matrix.
- Let urgency or social pressure override walk-away criteria established before the purchase moment.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Clarify the job
Write the job statement: "This purchase must enable me to [specific capability] under [conditions], reliably, for [time horizon]." Test each candidate: does it fulfill the job? Candidates that don't fulfill the job are eliminated regardless of price.

### Step 2 — Compute TCO per candidate
For each candidate, build TCO over the expected ownership horizon:
- Acquisition cost (sticker, taxes, fees, delivery, setup)
- Financing cost (if not cash: interest paid over loan term)
- Operating cost (fuel, utilities, subscription, consumables — annualized)
- Maintenance cost (warranty coverage, typical repair frequency, parts cost, labor access)
- Insurance cost (annual, over ownership horizon)
- Depreciation (expected resale value at end of ownership horizon vs. acquisition cost)
- Disposal cost (if applicable: removal, recycling, trade-in haircut)

TCO = acquisition + financing + operating + maintenance + insurance − expected resale, over ownership horizon.

### Step 3 — Build the attribute matrix
List 5–10 attributes that matter for this decision. For each, note:
- Deal-killer threshold (if any): the minimum acceptable value. Any candidate below is eliminated.
- Relative weight: how important is this attribute vs. others?
Score each non-eliminated candidate per attribute (1–5). Weighted score = Σ(attribute score × weight).

### Step 4 — Research methodology
For each attribute on your matrix:
- Who has independent data (reviews with methodology disclosed, long-term owner reports, reliability databases)?
- Who is selling and what is their incentive? (Dealer, manufacturer review, affiliate reviewer, sponsored content — discount their claims proportionally.)
- What are the failure modes people who bought this report? (Not "is it great" but "what went wrong, how often, how expensive?")

### Step 5 — Assess market timing
- Is this category seasonal? When do prices drop, inventory increase, or deals concentrate?
- Is there a new model or version coming that will either improve the product or depreciate current inventory?
- Are you under time pressure you can relieve (rental, repair, borrowed alternative)?
- What's the cost of waiting 30/60/90 days?

### Step 6 — Set walk-away criteria
Before you talk to any seller or enter any negotiation:
- Maximum price you'll pay (for each candidate — they may differ).
- Minimum attribute thresholds that must be met (not "I'd prefer" — deal-killers).
- Timeline: if no deal meeting criteria by [date], what do you do instead?

### Step 7 — Adversarial check
- Which option did you encounter first? Have you corrected for anchoring?
- Is the time pressure real or seller-manufactured?
- Are you solving a genuine job or rationalizing a want you had before researching?
- What do 12-month owners report (not initial impressions, not reviewers who returned the product)?

### Step 8 — Decision and target offer
- Recommended candidate based on attribute matrix and TCO.
- Target offer (for negotiable purchases): what you'll try to pay.
- Walk-away price: the number above which you leave.
- Next-best alternative if no candidate clears your criteria.
- Timeline commitment: by when will you decide?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Anchoring on first option.** The first price seen becomes the reference point for all subsequent comparisons. Name the first option you encountered and correct for its outsized influence explicitly.
2. **Monthly-payment confusion.** A salesperson who converts your budget to monthly payment has shifted your mental model from "what this costs" to "what this costs per month" — these are not equivalent. Always compute full cost.
3. **Want-job conflation.** "I want leather seats" is a want; it's not a job the purchase must do. Wants belong in preference criteria, not job criteria.
4. **TCO blindness.** For vehicles, appliances, and equipment, the operating cost over 5 years routinely exceeds the purchase price. Don't skip TCO.
5. **Reviewer incentive blindness.** Most review content is affiliate-monetized or manufacturer-comped. Check who benefits from a positive review before weighting it.
6. **Urgency override.** Real urgency (you have no car and a job starting Monday) is rare. Manufactured urgency (today-only deal, only one left) is common. Test whether your urgency is real before letting it compress your criteria.
7. **Recency bias from social network.** A neighbor who loves their new thing is recent, vivid, and positively selected (people don't typically broadcast purchases they regret). Seek out long-term owner forums and reliability data.
8. **Sunk-research cost.** After 20 hours of research you may feel committed to buying. You're not. Walk-away criteria override research investment.
9. **Deal excitement override.** A good deal on the wrong product is still the wrong product. The deal triggers purchase; the job should.

## Verification

- [ ] Job statement written before evaluating candidates.
- [ ] TCO computed for each candidate over full ownership horizon (not sticker only).
- [ ] Attribute matrix has 5–10 attributes with weights and deal-killer thresholds.
- [ ] Research sources assessed for incentive alignment.
- [ ] Market timing addressed: is now a good time?
- [ ] Walk-away criteria set before entering any negotiation.
- [ ] Anchoring on first option explicitly corrected for.
- [ ] Long-term owner data included, not just initial reviews.
- [ ] Recommendation includes target offer, walk-away price, and calibration anchor.
- [ ] No monthly-payment framing used.
