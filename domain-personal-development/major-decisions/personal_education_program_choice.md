# Education Program Choice

**Objective:** Evaluate programs (degrees, bootcamps, certifications, courses, self-study paths) against a concrete goal, producing a defensible comparison that includes actual outcome data, full cost, and a serious consideration of the no-program alternative. Forces the question "does this path actually produce the outcome I want, or am I buying access to a credential I believe will produce the outcome?" The two are not equivalent, and which one applies determines whether the program is worth it.

**When to use:**
- Evaluating one or more programs against a goal you've stated.
- Reconsidering whether formal education is the right path at all.
- Comparing programs across wildly different formats (degree vs. bootcamp vs. certificate vs. self-study).
- The cost is high enough that a mistake is painful (tuition, lost income, time).

**When NOT to use:**
- The credential is mandatory by regulation for the target role (medicine, law, certain engineering licensures) — in that case the credential question is answered; evaluate programs on quality, cost, and fit.
- The user is mid-program and committed — different analysis needed.
- The goal itself hasn't been defined; define the goal first.

**Audience:** Adults evaluating education investments at career-entry, career-transition, or advancement decision points.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Current state.** Education, skills, experience, and current role/situation.
2. **Target state.** The specific outcome you're trying to reach: role, skill set, income level, or capability. Be concrete.
3. **Program candidates.** The programs you're considering (name, format, duration, cost). Include at least one non-formal alternative.
4. **Constraints.** Time available (full-time vs. part-time), geography, income dependency during program, family obligations.
5. **Why now.** What's making this decision live at this moment?

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Evaluate each program's actual outcome track record — what graduates do, earn, and report, not what the program claims. Where data is unavailable, flag that as a risk.
- Compute full cost: direct tuition/fees plus opportunity cost (income foregone while enrolled, delayed career progression).
- Assess the no-program path: could the target state be reached without any formal program, and at what cost and probability?
- Differentiate signaling value from skill-building value for each program. Some programs primarily signal (credential to unlock gatekeeping); others primarily build skill. Most do both unevenly.
- Assess optionality after completion: what does each credential or program unlock beyond the immediate goal?
- Ask when "no program" is the right answer and make that case honestly if it applies.

### Must Not
- Accept marketing claims about outcomes at face value — employment rates, salary outcomes, and "partner companies" from program websites are systematically optimistic.
- Treat opportunity cost as zero. Time in a program is time not earning, not building on-the-job experience, and not building alternative paths.
- Conflate the goal with the credential. "I want to be a data scientist" and "I want a master's in data science" are related but not the same; the credential is a means, not the goal.
- Ignore the selectivity effect: programs with high employment outcomes may produce those outcomes because of who they admit, not what they teach.
- Recommend a program without stating what evidence was used to assess its outcome track record and how reliable that evidence is.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Clarify the goal
State the target precisely: role title, function, income range, type of work. If the user has stated a goal in terms of a credential ("I want to get my MBA"), reframe it in terms of the outcome the credential is supposed to enable. Then evaluate programs against the outcome, not the credential.

### Step 2 — Evaluate each program on outcome track record
For each candidate program:
- Employment rate in target role: what source, what definition (employed within 6 months? in field? at what salary?)?
- Earnings outcomes: median salary reported, by whom, on what population?
- What graduates actually do: survey alumni, find graduates on LinkedIn, look for forum discussions by recent graduates (not testimonials on the program site).
- Completion rate: what fraction who start, finish? Non-completion destroys ROI.
- How the outcome track record has changed over time: a bootcamp that placed 80% of graduates in 2019 may be placing 50% now.

### Step 3 — Compute full cost
For each program:
- Direct cost: tuition, fees, materials, housing if relocation required.
- Opportunity cost: income foregone × duration. If part-time, partial opportunity cost.
- Time cost: total hours (class + study + commute) — express in months equivalent.
- Debt load at graduation and debt-service impact on career flexibility.
- Break-even analysis: how long to recoup total cost (direct + opportunity) against income uplift from the credential?

### Step 4 — Assess the no-program alternative
Force this question: what's the probability of reaching the target state without any of these programs? Via:
- Self-study + portfolio + projects
- Employer-sponsored training while employed
- Adjacent role that builds the needed experience
- Apprenticeship, contract work, or direct entry

Estimate: time, probability of success, cost, and what it requires of the user that formal programs don't (discipline, self-direction, network development, sales of yourself without institutional backing).

### Step 5 — Differentiate signaling vs. skill-building
For each program, estimate:
- How much of the outcome track record is gating (employers require the credential as a filter, regardless of what's learned)?
- How much is competency (graduates are materially better at the target function)?
- If the role you're targeting eliminated the credential requirement tomorrow, would the program still be worth it?

This determines how to weight the program's value: pure signalers are only worth the gate they open; skill-builders deliver value even without the credential.

### Step 6 — Assess optionality and fit
For each program:
- What does the network unlock beyond the immediate goal?
- What roles, industries, or opportunities does this credential open that no-program doesn't?
- Learning format fit: does the program's format (cohort, self-paced, intensive, evening) match how you actually learn and the constraints you have?
- Life fit: geography, schedule, family, financial obligations.

### Step 7 — Adversarial check
- Have you found critics of each program, not just endorsers?
- Have you spoken with or found accounts from people who dropped out?
- Is the timing of this decision driven by a real need, or by social comparison (peers getting degrees, feeling behind)?
- Are you using education to defer a decision you're actually ready to make without more credentials?

### Step 8 — Decision
- Recommended path with rationale anchored in outcome track record, cost, and fit.
- Confidence in the recommendation and what would change it.
- Conditions under which the no-program path becomes the right answer.
- Calibration anchor: a sentence to write down today that future-you can audit against.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Marketing outcome conflation.** "95% employed within 6 months" is a claim. Check the definition, the population, and who measured it. It almost never matches independent data.
2. **Credential-goal conflation.** The credential is a means; the target role is the goal. Optimize for the goal, not the credential.
3. **Opportunity cost invisibility.** A $20K program with $60K in foregone income is a $80K investment. Model it that way.
4. **Selectivity bias.** A program that attracts strong candidates will show strong outcomes. Test whether the program or the intake cohort drove the result.
5. **Social comparison pressure.** "Everyone in my field has a master's" may be true in some sub-fields and false in others. Check what the people doing the work you want are actually credentialed with.
6. **Education-as-deferral.** Using a program to delay committing to a direction that's already clear. Expensive delay.
7. **No-program blindness.** Most people evaluate programs against each other rather than against doing nothing formal. The no-program path often loses this comparison only because it was never seriously modeled.
8. **Completion-rate skip.** A program where 40% of students don't finish changes the ROI math fundamentally.
9. **Alumni testimony bias.** Alumni who speak to admissions offices or appear in testimonials are selected for positive experience. Seek out forums, Reddit, and LinkedIn for unfiltered accounts.
