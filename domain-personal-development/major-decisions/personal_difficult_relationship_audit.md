# Difficult Relationship Audit

**Objective:** Structure an audit of a difficult relationship before taking consequential action — ending it, escalating it, distancing, confronting, or recommitting. The prompt surfaces what's specifically happening (behaviors, not characterizations), the concrete impact on the user, the user's own contribution, what's been tried and what hasn't, what the relationship offers that makes it worth working on, and what exit looks like at what cost. It aims to distinguish "this is hard but worth working on" from "this is harmful and the cost of staying exceeds the cost of leaving" — and to do so rigorously, not emotionally.

**When to use:**
- Before making a significant move in a difficult relationship (ending, confronting, distancing significantly).
- When you've been going in circles in your thinking and need a structured frame.
- When you're not sure if the problem is the relationship or your current state.
- When you need to separate what you know from what you're projecting.

**When NOT to use:**
- Safety is at risk — if there is any physical danger, that is the priority, not analysis.
- The relationship has already ended — this is a before-action audit tool.
- The user is in acute emotional distress; stabilize before analyzing.

**Audience:** Adults facing a significant decision about a relationship that's causing sustained difficulty.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The relationship.** Who (role, not name), how long, what kind.
2. **What's hard.** In your own words, what's the problem?
3. **What's been tried.** Conversations, changes, outside help, distance — what have you already done?
4. **What you're considering doing.** What actions are on the table? What are you afraid you need to do?
5. **What the relationship gives you.** Before the analysis starts: what does this relationship provide that you value?

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Ground the audit in specific behaviors and events, not characterizations. "They're selfish" is a characterization; "They canceled on me three times in two months without notice, and when I raised it they turned it back on me" is a behavior.
- Assess the concrete impact on the user — not "I feel bad" but what specifically is happening in your life, your mental state, your other relationships, your work.
- Surface the user's own contribution honestly. This is not blame-assignment; it's pattern completion. The user's behavior shapes the relationship's dynamics, even when the other person is the primary problem.
- Evaluate what's been tried vs. what hasn't. Many relationships are abandoned before the high-leverage intervention has been attempted.
- Assess what the relationship offers. A relationship that's purely negative is easy to leave; the hard cases are relationships that are both valuable and damaging.
- Estimate the cost of exit — what's actually lost, not just the pain of leaving.
- Distinguish hard-but-worth-working-on from harmful-and-staying-costs-more-than-leaving.

### Must Not
- Prescribe whether to stay or leave. The user decides; the audit structures.
- Accept characterizations as evidence. "They're toxic" is not evidence; specific repeated behaviors are.
- Minimize the user's contribution. "I only react to what they do" is usually not the complete picture.
- Treat the relationship's best moments as its representative state. Difficult relationships often have real warmth and connection; that's why they're hard to leave. The good moments don't negate the pattern.
- Use the audit to produce the verdict the user already wants. The prompt should push on both sides.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Behaviors, not characterizations
List the specific behaviors that are driving the difficulty:
- What does this person actually do? (Specific actions, statements, patterns.)
- How often? How recently?
- Under what conditions does the behavior occur? Under what conditions does it not?

If you find yourself writing characterizations ("they're narcissistic," "they're manipulative"), translate them into specific behaviors. What do they actually do that leads you to that characterization?

### Step 2 — Concrete impact on you
Not "it makes me feel bad" but:
- What specifically is happening in your life as a result of this relationship pattern?
- Sleep, work, other relationships, self-perception, physical health?
- How long has this been going on?
- Has the impact increased, decreased, or stayed constant over time?

### Step 3 — Your contribution
This is not about blame. It's about pattern completion:
- What do you do in response to the behavior? Does that response make it better or worse?
- What have you avoided doing that might have changed the dynamic?
- What expectations do you hold that the other person might not know about?
- Have you communicated what you need in terms that were direct and clear, not hinted at?

### Step 4 — What's been tried
For each intervention or conversation you've attempted:
- What did you do or say?
- What was the response?
- What changed (if anything) and for how long?
- What hasn't been tried that has a reasonable chance of changing the dynamic?

High-leverage interventions that often haven't been tried:
- Direct, non-blaming statement of impact and ask for specific change.
- Professional help (couples counseling, mediator, therapist for one person).
- Structured distance (not as punishment, but as reset — with explicit terms).
- Explicit conversation about the future of the relationship: "I need us to address this or I'm not sure this relationship can continue."

### Step 5 — What the relationship gives you
Inventory what's real and valuable:
- Companionship, history, support, co-parenting, professional relationship, love.
- What would you lose if this relationship ended or significantly changed?
- Is what it gives you available elsewhere at comparable cost, or is this relationship uniquely valuable?

Be honest here in both directions: don't minimize the value (to make leaving feel easier) or inflate it (to make staying feel necessary).

### Step 6 — The exit cost
What would it actually cost to leave or significantly restructure this relationship?
- Financial costs if applicable (divorce, shared housing, shared business).
- Social costs: shared friends, family, community.
- Identity costs: who you are in relation to this person, what you lose from your self-concept.
- Practical costs: shared children, caregiving, co-dependence.
- The grief: real and proportionate to the relationship's value.

What would the first 6 months look like? The first 2 years?

### Step 7 — Hard vs. harmful
The key distinction:
- **Hard relationships** are ones where both people have real investment, the difficulty comes from real differences or strains, and effort to address it has a reasonable probability of improving things. Hard is often worth working on.
- **Harmful relationships** are ones where the pattern causes sustained damage to one or both people, where the other person is either unwilling or unable to engage with the problem, and where staying requires accepting ongoing harm. Harmful is a different calculation.

Where does this relationship fall, and what's the evidence for that placement?

### Step 8 — Decision path
Based on the audit:
- What has not yet been tried that should be tried before exit?
- If the highest-leverage intervention has been tried — what's the result?
- What's the threshold event that would make the decision clear?
- If you're staying: what changes, by when, in order to make staying a choice rather than a drift?
- If you're leaving: what's the path, the timeline, and the support structure?

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Characterization-as-evidence.** "They're a narcissist" is not evidence. What do they specifically do, how often, and what's the impact? Characterizations feel precise but carry no information until unpacked.
2. **Best-moments representation.** The relationship's best moments are real but not representative. The pattern is what you're living in.
3. **User-contribution skip.** Even when the other person is the primary problem, your response pattern matters. Skipping your contribution produces an incomplete picture.
4. **Untried-intervention blindness.** People often leave relationships before the highest-leverage intervention has been attempted. What has not been tried that should be?
5. **Exit-cost minimization.** "I can just leave" — stated as though it's simple when it isn't. Model the actual cost.
6. **Exit-cost exaggeration.** The opposite problem: "I can't leave, too much at stake" — when the cost of staying exceeds the cost of leaving but the exit cost is felt more vividly.
7. **Other-person-as-fixed.** People change — under the right conditions. The question is whether the conditions for change exist in this relationship, not whether change is theoretically possible.
8. **Emotional-state contamination.** If you're in acute pain, anger, or relief, your assessment of the relationship is not reliable. The audit is most useful when done in a less activated state.
9. **Hard-vs-harmful conflation.** Calling a hard relationship harmful to justify leaving, or calling a harmful relationship hard to justify staying.

## Verification

- [ ] Behaviors listed as specific actions, not characterizations.
- [ ] Impact described concretely, not just emotionally.
- [ ] User's contribution assessed honestly (not blamed, not minimized).
- [ ] Attempted interventions listed with outcomes.
- [ ] High-leverage untried interventions identified.
- [ ] What the relationship gives assessed honestly in both directions.
- [ ] Exit cost modeled (financial, social, identity, practical, grief).
- [ ] Hard vs. harmful distinction made with supporting evidence.
- [ ] Decision path includes threshold event and calibration anchor.
