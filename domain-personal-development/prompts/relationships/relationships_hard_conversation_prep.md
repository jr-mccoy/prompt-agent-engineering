# Prepare for a Hard Personal Conversation

**Objective:** Walk the user through preparing for one specific, emotionally charged conversation in their personal life — naming the single outcome worth aiming for, separating what happened from the story they've built around it, drafting an opening that doesn't trigger defensiveness, genuinely modeling the other person's view, and building a plan to stay grounded if it heats up.

## When to Use

- Use when: you need to raise something hard with a partner, parent, adult child, sibling, or close friend (disappointment, a betrayal, a recurring hurt, an unmet need, a change you want).
- Use when: you keep rehearsing the conversation in your head, it keeps turning into an argument, and you want to break that loop with structure.
- Use when: you're worried you'll either chicken out and say nothing, or over-prepare into a courtroom speech.
- Don't use when: the conversation is with a manager, coworker, client, or other professional contact — use [difficultconvo_pre_brief.md](../../../domain-negotiation/difficult-conversations/difficultconvo_pre_brief.md) instead. This prompt is for personal relationships.
- Don't use when: you primarily need to set a single specific limit on a recurring behavior — use [relationships_boundary_setting_script.md](relationships_boundary_setting_script.md).
- Don't use when: there's a safety risk or a pattern of abuse — that is out of scope (see boundary note).

## Inputs / Context

Collect these. If 1–4 are missing, ask before drafting.

1. **Who, and the relationship.** Role, closeness, history relevant to this topic.
2. **The topic in one sentence.** What is this conversation actually about?
3. **What you want to happen** — your honest first answer, even if it's "I want them to admit they were wrong." (The prompt will help refine this into something achievable.)
4. **What's happened** — the specific events/behaviors, kept separate from your interpretation of them.
5. **What you're afraid of** in this conversation (they'll get angry, cry, shut down, leave, deny it). Optional but useful.

**Refusal logic:** If the situation involves abuse, threats, coercion, or any danger, stop. Do not coach the user on how to "have the conversation." State that this is not a communication-skills situation, and route to professional support (counselor, hotline, or trusted local resources). Likewise, if the user is processing relationship trauma, note that this prompt prepares a conversation — it is not therapy — and suggest a licensed professional for the deeper work.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Find the one achievable outcome

The user's first-stated want is often "be vindicated" or "make them change." Help convert it into an outcome the user can actually influence: to be heard, to express something honestly, to make a specific request, to understand their side, to decide a next step together. Name **one** primary outcome. If the user is aiming purely at changing the other person's feelings or admissions, flag that it isn't in their control and offer the controllable version.

### Step 2 — Separate facts from the story

Split the situation into two columns:
- **What happened** (observable, would survive a recording).
- **The story I'm telling about it** (my interpretation, motive-reading, meaning).

Both are legitimate, but conflating them is what makes openings sound like accusations. Name at least one place where the user's story might be wrong, or where a different read is plausible.

### Step 3 — Draft the opening

Write the first 2–4 sentences the user will say. A strong opening:
- States the topic plainly and the user's intent ("I want to talk about X, and I want us to come out of it okay").
- Leads with the user's own experience ("I've felt..." / "I noticed...") rather than a verdict on the other person.
- Avoids "you always / you never," diagnosis, and kitchen-sinking (dragging in unrelated grievances).

Offer one softer and one more direct version.

### Step 4 — Steel-man the other person (RP-02)

Genuinely take their perspective: How do they likely see this? What might they be protecting, afraid of, or needing? What's the most charitable read of their behavior that's still consistent with the facts? This is not to excuse them — it's so the user walks in able to hear, not just transmit. Note one thing the user might learn that would change their own view.

### Step 5 — Plan for the heat

Identify the 2–3 moments most likely to spike (a specific trigger phrase, a defensive counter-attack, tears, stonewalling). For each, give the user:
- A grounding move (pause, breath, name it: "I notice this is getting tense").
- A re-centering line that returns to the one outcome.
- A pre-decided exit ("Let's take a break and come back to this tomorrow") so the user isn't trapped into escalation.

### Step 6 — Define "good enough"

Set the realistic success bar: often it's "I said the true thing, kindly, and stayed regulated," not "they agreed." Write the one-line version the user can hold so they don't judge the conversation by whether the other person responded perfectly.

## Constraints

### Must
- Reduce the goal to one outcome the user can actually influence.
- Separate observable facts from the user's interpretation.
- Produce a concrete opening in the user's own voice, with a softer and firmer variant.
- Model the other person's perspective charitably and specifically.
- Provide grounding moves, re-centering lines, and a pre-decided exit for escalation.
- Set a realistic "good enough" bar.

### Must Not
- Coach a conversation with someone unsafe (refuse and route to help).
- Write a script that wins the argument, scores points, or extracts an admission.
- Encourage kitchen-sinking, ultimatums, diagnosis, or "you always/never" framing.
- Promise a particular reaction from the other person.
- Replace therapy — if relationship trauma is the real issue, name the limit and refer.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Vindication disguised as a goal.** "I want them to admit it" is not an achievable outcome; the user controls what they say, not what the other person concedes. Convert it before drafting.
2. **Story stated as fact.** Motive-reads ("they did it to hurt me") slip into openings as if they were events. Flag and relocate them to the story column.
3. **Fake steel-manning.** A perspective-take that's actually a thinly veiled "here's why they're wrong" defeats the purpose. Push for a read the user would accept if roles were reversed.
4. **Over-scripting.** A fully memorized speech makes the user brittle when the other person doesn't follow the script. Prepare the opening and the anchors, not every line.
5. **Kitchen-sink risk.** If the user lists five grievances, help pick the one this conversation is about; the rest can wait.
6. **Conflating prep with safety or therapy.** Re-screen: danger routes to support; deep trauma routes to a professional. This prompt prepares a single conversation, nothing more.

## Expected Output

A prep brief with: the one outcome, the facts/story split, the opening (two variants), the other person's likely perspective, the escalation plan, and the good-enough bar.

### Output Format

```
## The one outcome (something you can influence)
[single sentence]

## Facts vs. story
| What happened (observable) | The story I'm telling |
|---|---|
| [...] | [...] |
Where my story might be wrong: [...]

## Opening line — softer
[2–4 sentences, first person]

## Opening line — more direct
[2–4 sentences, first person]

## Their likely perspective
- How they probably see it: [...]
- What they may be protecting/needing: [...]
- One thing I might learn that changes my view: [...]

## When it heats up
| Likely spike | Grounding move | Re-centering line | Exit if needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| [...] | [...] | [...] | [...] |

## Good enough
[one line: what success looks like regardless of their reaction]
```

## Example Output

```
## The one outcome (something you can influence)
Tell my dad honestly that his comments about my parenting hurt, and ask for a specific change — without it becoming a fight about the past.

## Facts vs. story
| What happened (observable) | The story I'm telling |
|---|---|
| At dinner he said, in front of the kids, "That's not how we did it." | He thinks I'm a bad parent and is undermining me on purpose. |
| He's said versions of this 3–4 times this year. | He'll never see me as an adult. |
Where my story might be wrong: He may think he's "helping," not undermining — he gave advice like this to my sister too, who didn't take it personally.

## Opening line — softer
"Dad, I want to bring up something small but it's been sitting with me. When you comment on my parenting in front of the kids, it lands harder than I think you mean it to. Can we talk about it?"

## Opening line — more direct
"I need to ask you something. When you correct how I parent in front of the kids, it undercuts me with them. I'd like that to stop. Can we figure out a better way?"

## Their likely perspective
- How they probably see it: He's an experienced parent sharing what worked; he may feel useful, not critical.
- What they may be protecting/needing: To still matter, to feel his experience counts.
- One thing I might learn that changes my view: He may not have noticed the kids were listening, or that it's become a pattern.

## When it heats up
| Likely spike | Grounding move | Re-centering line | Exit if needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| "So I can't say anything now?" | Pause, breath. | "You can — I'm asking specifically about in front of the kids." | "Let's pick this up after dinner." |
| He brings up the past | Name it: "We're drifting." | "I want to keep this to the one thing." | "I don't want to relitigate; let's stop here for tonight." |

## Good enough
I told him the truth, kindly, and made one clear request — even if he got defensive in the moment.
```

## Verification

- [ ] The goal is a single outcome the user can influence, not an admission/feeling from the other person.
- [ ] Facts and interpretation are visibly separated, with at least one place the story might be wrong.
- [ ] An opening exists in two tones, first-person, with no "you always/never" or kitchen-sinking.
- [ ] The other person's perspective is modeled charitably and specifically (real steel-man, not a strawman).
- [ ] Escalation moments have grounding moves, re-centering lines, and a pre-decided exit.
- [ ] A realistic good-enough bar is set that doesn't depend on the other person's reaction.
- [ ] Inputs re-screened for safety/trauma; either would have triggered a refusal/referral.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** Anchors prep to one influenceable outcome rather than a vindication fantasy.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** Sequences outcome → facts/story → opening → perspective → heat plan → good-enough.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** Examines the conversation across the user's side, the facts, and the other person's view.
- **RP-02 (Audience-Specific Framing):** Drives the steel-man step — framing the message for how the other person actually hears it.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** Rails against ultimatums, diagnosis, and kitchen-sinking.
- **QA-20 (Dual-Failure Quality Test):** Guards both the harsh/attacking failure and the over-hedged "say nothing real" failure.
