# Build a Durable Strategy for One Difficult Family Relationship

**Objective:** Produce a strategy for one specific difficult family relationship that the user can actually sustain — naming what to stop trying to change, designing the contact and boundaries that protect them, and pre-planning for the recurring pattern — rather than re-litigating the same fight indefinitely.

**When to use:** A parent, sibling, adult child, in-law, or relative is a recurring source of stress (criticism, boundary-crossing, guilt, drama, one-sidedness) and you want a durable way to relate rather than a one-off fix. Also useful before a wedding, holiday, illness, or move forces more contact. Not for a single specific boundary conversation — use `relationships_boundary_setting_script.md`; not for a stay/estrange decision you're actively weighing — use `major-decisions/personal_difficult_relationship_audit.md`.

**Audience:** An individual doing this for themselves. Not for assessing someone else, not clinical. If the relationship involves abuse, threats, coercion, financial control, or any safety risk, this is not a communication problem — route to a licensed counselor, a domestic-violence resource, or trusted local support. These instructions will not coach "strategy" with someone who is unsafe.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **The person and the tie.** Relationship role, how much contact currently, whether contact is optional or structurally forced (shared home, caregiving, co-owned obligation, shared children/parents).
2. **The specific difficulty.** The concrete recurring behaviors — with a recent example each — not a character label. "Comments on my weight at every meal," not "is toxic."
3. **What you've tried.** Confrontations, boundaries, avoidance, going along — and what happened each time.
4. **What you actually need.** The realistic aim: less contact, calmer contact, protection of a specific thing (your kids, your finances, your peace), or a workable détente. Not "fix them."
5. **The constraint.** What keeps you in contact (love, obligation, shared family, guilt, logistics, inheritance, other relatives caught in the middle).

If input 2 describes abuse, fear, or control rather than difficulty, stop and route to safety support before any strategy. Difficult ≠ dangerous; this prompt is only for the former.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Classify the difficulty (fixed taxonomy)

Place the pattern in one primary type — this drives the strategy:

| Type | Signature | Core strategic move |
|---|---|---|
| **Critic** | Judgment, unsolicited advice, disapproval | Reduce the target surface; stop seeking approval |
| **Boundary-crosser** | Ignores limits, overreach, drop-ins, oversharing | Firm, repeated, self-enforced boundaries |
| **Guilt/obligation** | Debt-framing, martyrdom, "after all I've done" | Decouple your choices from their guilt |
| **Drama/chaos** | Crises, triangulation, conflict-seeking | Refuse the role; low engagement, no rescue |
| **Withholding/cold** | Absence, disinterest, one-sidedness | Lower expectations; invest elsewhere |
| **Volatile** | Unpredictable anger, blow-ups | Contact design + exits; never the flammable topics |

If two types combine, name the dominant one; strategy converges better on one.

### Step 2 — Separate the changeable from the fixed (DS-01)

Sort the situation into two columns:
- **What you cannot change:** their personality, their opinion of you, whether they'll ever apologize or "get it." Long-standing adult patterns rarely shift because you argued better.
- **What you control:** frequency, duration, setting, topics, your reactions, what you disclose, whether you attend, when you leave.

State plainly the single most costly thing the user keeps trying to change that they should stop trying to change. This is the strategy's center of gravity — the exhaustion usually comes from relitigating the unchangeable.

### Step 3 — Design the contact

Choose a sustainable contact posture from a fixed ladder, matched to input 4:

1. **Full/warm** — no change needed (rarely the case here).
2. **Structured** — see them, but on your terms: defined occasions, time-limited, public/neutral settings, an exit planned.
3. **Low-contact** — reduced frequency, shorter, selective topics, delayed replies.
4. **Minimal/transactional** — logistics only, grey-rock, no emotional supply.
5. **No-contact / estrangement** — a real option, not a failure (route the decision to the major-decisions audit).

Specify the concrete design: how often, how long, where, who else is present (a buffer relative), and the pre-decided exit line.

### Step 4 — Set the load-bearing boundaries

Name the 1–3 boundaries that actually protect input 4's need. Each as: the behavior, the limit, and **your** self-enforced response (leave the room, end the call, don't fund it, don't attend) — never a consequence that depends on the other person cooperating. For scripting a specific one, hand off to `relationships_boundary_setting_script.md`.

### Step 5 — Pre-plan the predictable pattern

Family difficulty is repetitive, which makes it plannable. For the 2–3 recurring flashpoints (the holiday comment, the money ask, the triangulation), pre-decide the response now, while calm: a deflection line, a topic the user won't engage, a grounding move, and the exit. Decide in advance so the moment doesn't hijack you.

### Step 6 — Set expectations and the one first move

State the realistic forecast in one honest sentence ("Mom will keep commenting; the goal is that it lands on me less and ruins fewer visits," not "Mom will stop"). Then name ONE concrete first move for the next month — a contact change, a boundary put into practice, or a flashpoint pre-scripted — with a check for whether the new posture is more sustainable than the old.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Classify the difficulty into one primary type from the fixed taxonomy.
- Explicitly separate what the user cannot change from what they control, and name the costly thing to stop trying to change.
- Choose a specific, sustainable contact posture with a concrete design.
- Tie boundaries to self-enforced responses the user controls.
- Pre-plan the recurring flashpoints while calm.
- End with one honest expectation and one concrete first move.

### Must Not
- Coach strategy with an abusive or unsafe person — refuse and route to support.
- Diagnose the family member (narcissist, borderline, etc.) — describe behavior, don't label pathology.
- Promise the person will change, apologize, or "come around."
- Frame estrangement as either the obvious answer or a moral failure — it's one posture among several, and the decision routes out.
- Build a boundary whose enforcement depends on the difficult person's agreement.
- Moralize about family loyalty or about cutting people off.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Difficult vs. dangerous.** Re-screen inputs: control, threats, financial coercion, and fear are abuse, not "a difficult mom." Route to safety support; do not produce a détente strategy for a dangerous person.
2. **Change fantasy.** The deepest trap is a strategy secretly aimed at making them different. If every move is really "so they'll finally see," relocate that to the unchangeable column.
3. **Armchair diagnosis.** A cluster-B label feels clarifying and changes nothing actionable. Keep to observable behavior and controllable responses.
4. **Estrangement as default or as taboo.** Neither pushed nor forbidden. Present no-contact as a legitimate option and route the actual decision to the decision-grade audit.
5. **Boundary that needs their cooperation.** "I need her to stop" isn't a boundary. Every limit must have a response the user can execute alone.
6. **One-conversation cure.** Durable family patterns aren't fixed by one talk. Set the expectation of repetition and self-enforcement, not resolution.
7. **Collateral damage ignored.** Other relatives are often caught in the middle. Note where a contact change affects them, without letting their comfort override the user's need.

- [ ] Difficulty is classified into one primary type; the person is not diagnosed with a disorder.

- [ ] A specific, sustainable contact posture and design are chosen.
- [ ] Boundaries each have a self-enforced response the user controls.
- [ ] Recurring flashpoints are pre-planned while calm, with exits.
- [ ] One honest expectation and one dated first move close the output.
- [ ] Estrangement is neither pushed nor forbidden; the stay/leave decision is routed out.
