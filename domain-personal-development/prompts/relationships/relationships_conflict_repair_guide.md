# Repair After a Rupture in a Personal Relationship

**Objective:** Help the user attempt a genuine repair after a rupture with someone in their personal life — by reconstructing what actually happened (RT-09), separating their part from the other person's, calibrating an apology that's neither self-flagellating nor defensive, drafting a real repair attempt, and planning for an outcome the user can't control, including recognizing when repair isn't available right now.

## When to Use

- Use when: you had a fight, said something you regret, were hurt by someone close, or there's been a breach (a let-down, a betrayal of trust, a boundary crossed) and you want to mend it.
- Use when: you keep replaying the rupture and want to move from rumination to a concrete repair attempt.
- Use when: you're unsure how much to apologize, or whether to apologize at all when you were also wronged.
- Don't use when: there's a safety risk, ongoing abuse, or coercion — repair is not the move, and that situation is out of scope (see boundary note).
- Don't use when: you're deciding whether the relationship is worth keeping at all — use [personal_difficult_relationship_audit.md](../../major-decisions/personal_difficult_relationship_audit.md).
- Don't use when: nothing's ruptured and you just want to keep things warm — use [relationships_network_cultivation_plan.md](relationships_network_cultivation_plan.md).

## Inputs / Context

Gather these. If 1–4 are missing, ask before drafting a repair.

1. **The relationship and the rupture.** Who (role), what happened, when.
2. **Your account of it** — what you did, said, felt; in your own words.
3. **What you think their experience was** — your honest guess at how it landed for them.
4. **What you want now** — reconnection, an apology from them, a reset, an understanding, or just to say your piece.
5. **History** — is this a one-off or a recurring pattern? Optional but changes the repair.

**Refusal logic:** If the rupture involves violence, threats, coercion, or a pattern of abuse, do not coach a repair or an apology. Making peace with someone who is unsafe can increase risk. State this plainly and route the user to appropriate professional support. If the rupture is entangled with relationship trauma, note that repair coaching is not therapy and a licensed professional may be the right next step.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Reconstruct what happened (RT-09)

Walk back the sequence: the trigger, what each person did, where it escalated. Separate the **event** from the **interpretation** (what was said/done vs. the meaning the user assigned). Find the root, not just the last exchange — fights often detonate on a small thing but are about an older one.

### Step 2 — Sort responsibility honestly

Map the user's part and the other person's part without collapsing into either "it's all my fault" or "I did nothing wrong." Most ruptures are co-authored, even when not 50/50. Name:
- What's clearly the user's to own (an action, a tone, a broken commitment).
- What's the other person's (which the user can name but not apologize for).
- What's shared dynamic (the pattern both feed).

### Step 3 — Calibrate the apology

Decide what a real apology covers and what it doesn't:
- **Own** the specific behavior and its impact, not a vague "sorry you feel that way."
- **Don't** apologize for things that aren't yours, for having needs, or as a tactic to end discomfort.
- **Don't** bundle the apology with a counter-accusation ("I'm sorry I yelled, but you started it") — that erases the apology.
If the user was primarily the wronged party, the "repair attempt" may be an invitation to talk rather than an apology; name that.

### Step 4 — Draft the repair attempt

Write the actual message or opening (text, call, or in-person). It should:
- Acknowledge the rupture and the user's part specifically.
- Express what the user wants (reconnection, a conversation, understanding).
- Leave room for the other person's experience rather than presenting a closed verdict.
Keep it short and human. Offer a softer and a more direct variant.

### Step 5 — Plan for the response you don't control

The user controls the attempt, not the reception. Prepare for:
- They reciprocate → next step toward reconnection.
- They're defensive or escalate → a non-escalating hold and a graceful pause.
- They're not ready → respecting that without abandoning the repair entirely.
- They reject it → what the user does to be at peace having tried.
For recurring patterns, pair the repair with a forward agreement so the same rupture doesn't simply recur (this is where a boundary may belong — see [relationships_boundary_setting_script.md](relationships_boundary_setting_script.md)).

### Step 6 — Define peace-with-having-tried

Set the success bar the user controls: a sincere, well-calibrated attempt. Reconciliation may or may not follow; the user's job is the honest attempt, not manufacturing the other person's forgiveness.

## Constraints

### Must
- Reconstruct the rupture to its root, separating event from interpretation.
- Allocate responsibility honestly across the user, the other person, and the shared dynamic.
- Calibrate the apology to what's actually the user's to own.
- Produce a concrete repair attempt in two tones.
- Plan for each plausible response, including rejection and "not ready."
- Set a success bar the user controls.

### Must Not
- Coach repair with an unsafe/abusive person — refuse and route to support.
- Push a self-blaming apology that owns things that aren't the user's.
- Produce a non-apology ("sorry you feel that way") or a but-bundled apology.
- Promise the repair will be accepted, or pressure the other person to forgive.
- Treat repair as a substitute for changing a recurring harmful dynamic.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Over-apology.** Owning everything to end discomfort isn't repair; it teaches both people the user is always at fault. Keep the apology to what's actually theirs.
2. **Under-apology / defensiveness.** "I only reacted to what they did" usually isn't the whole story. Find the genuine part to own even when the user was also wronged.
3. **But-bundling.** Any "I'm sorry, but..." reads as a counter-attack and cancels the repair. Strip it.
4. **Last-exchange myopia.** Repairing only the final blowup misses the older root; the same fight returns. Reconstruct to the source.
5. **Forgiveness coercion.** Repair offers a hand; it doesn't demand the other person take it. Watch for language that pressures.
6. **Repair-as-bypass.** If the rupture is the latest in a damaging pattern, a smooth apology that changes nothing is avoidance. Pair repair with a real forward change or route to the audit.
7. **Safety blind spot.** Re-screen: making peace with someone dangerous can raise risk. Refuse and refer.

A repair plan with: the reconstructed sequence, the responsibility map, the calibrated apology stance, the repair attempt (two tones), the response plan, and the controllable success bar.

## Example Output

```
## What happened (to the root)
- Trigger: I forgot my friend's birthday dinner and didn't show.
- Escalation: She texted hurt; I got defensive and said she was "being dramatic."
- Event vs. interpretation: Event — I missed it and called her dramatic. Interpretation — I assumed she'd understand because work was insane.
- Likely older root: She's felt like a low priority to me since I started the new job.

## Responsibility map
| Mine to own | Theirs (name, don't apologize for) | Shared dynamic |
|---|---|---|
| Missing the dinner; calling her "dramatic" | The all-caps texts escalated it | We both avoid saying "I feel unimportant" directly |

## Apology stance
- I'm owning: missing her birthday and dismissing her hurt by calling her dramatic.
- I'm not owning: that she was wrong to be upset — she wasn't.
- This is an apology (I was primarily in the wrong here).

## Repair attempt — softer
"I've been sitting with this and I owe you a real apology. I missed your birthday and then made it worse by calling you dramatic — that was unfair and I'm sorry. You weren't being dramatic; I let you down. Can I take you out properly this week? I want to make it right."

## Repair attempt — more direct
"I was wrong on both counts — missing your dinner and dismissing how you felt. I'm sorry. I'd like to make it up to you and also talk about why this keeps happening. Are you open to it?"

## If they...
| Response | My move |
|---|---|
| Reciprocate | Set the make-up dinner; bring the honest conversation. |
| Get defensive | "That's fair, I deserve that — I'm not here to defend myself, just to apologize." |
| Not ready | "I get it. The apology stands and so does the invitation, whenever you're ready." |
| Reject it | Accept it, stop pushing, and know I made a sincere repair. |

## Forward change (if recurring)
Tell her honestly that the job has eaten my time and I've let her slide — and put her recurring stuff in my calendar so it stops being on memory.

## Peace with having tried
I apologized specifically and sincerely and offered to change the pattern. Whether she's ready now is hers to decide.
```

## Verification

- [ ] The rupture is reconstructed to its root, with event separated from interpretation.
- [ ] Responsibility is split honestly across mine / theirs / shared — neither all-fault nor no-fault.
- [ ] The apology owns only what's the user's, with no "sorry you feel that way" and no but-bundling.
- [ ] A concrete repair attempt exists in two tones, short and human.
- [ ] Each plausible response (including rejection and "not ready") has a non-coercive move.
- [ ] For recurring ruptures, a forward change/boundary is included, not just a smoothing apology.
- [ ] Success is defined as a controllable attempt, not the other person's forgiveness.
- [ ] Inputs re-screened for safety; abuse would have triggered a refusal/referral.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** Centers the work on a genuine, well-calibrated repair attempt.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** Reconstruct → allocate → calibrate → draft → plan response → set bar, in order.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** Separates the user's part, the other's part, and the shared dynamic.
- **RT-09 (Root Cause Explanation Pattern):** Drives reconstructing past the last exchange to the older root of the rupture.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** Rails against over/under-apology, but-bundling, and forgiveness coercion.
- **QA-20 (Dual-Failure Quality Test):** Guards both self-flagellating over-apology and defensive under-apology.
