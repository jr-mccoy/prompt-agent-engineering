# Design and Rehearse a Boundary-Setting Conversation

**Objective:** Convert a recurring frustration in a personal relationship into one specific, statable boundary — a concrete request about a concrete behavior, with a script the user can say in their own voice, a prepared response to the predictable pushback, and a follow-through that the user controls (not one that depends on the other person agreeing).

## When to Use

- Use when: a recurring behavior from someone in your personal life (family member, friend, roommate, partner, neighbor) keeps producing resentment and you want to name a limit clearly instead of hinting, snapping, or going silent.
- Use when: you've "addressed it" before but it didn't land, and you suspect the message was too vague, too apologetic, or too aimed at changing their character rather than naming a behavior.
- Use when: you keep over-explaining, over-justifying, or pre-apologizing your way out of asking for what you need.
- Don't use when: the situation involves abuse, coercion, threats, or any safety risk — that is not a "communication skills" problem and is out of scope (see boundary note below).
- Don't use when: you actually want to decide whether to stay in or leave the relationship — use [personal_difficult_relationship_audit.md](../../major-decisions/personal_difficult_relationship_audit.md).
- Don't use when: the relationship is a professional/work one — that belongs to the difficult-conversations prep in `domain-negotiation/difficult-conversations/`.

## Inputs / Context

Gather these. If items 1–4 are missing, ask before drafting — a boundary built on vague inputs will be vague.

1. **The relationship.** Role (not name), how close, how long. One line.
2. **The recurring behavior.** The specific, observable thing they do — not a characterization. "Shows up 40 minutes late" not "is disrespectful."
3. **The concrete impact on you.** What it actually costs you (time, money, sleep, stress, missed things), not just "it bothers me."
4. **What you've tried, and what happened.** Hints, jokes, blowing up, silent treatment, a previous direct ask. What was the result?
5. **What you'd be willing to do on your own side if the behavior continues** — the part you control. (Leave earlier, stop covering, decline the next invite.) Optional; the prompt can help generate this.

**Refusal logic:** If the input describes physical danger, threats, financial coercion, isolation, or any pattern of control/abuse, do not produce a boundary script. State plainly that this is beyond communication-skills coaching and route the user to appropriate support (a licensed counselor, a domestic-violence hotline, or trusted local resources). Do not minimize, do not analyze the abuser's behavior, and do not coach negotiation with someone who is unsafe.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Translate the complaint into a behavior + impact

Restate the user's frustration in two clean parts: the **observable behavior** and the **concrete impact**. If the user gave a characterization ("they're selfish"), convert it ("they reschedule our plans the day-of, which means I turn down other invitations and then sit home"). Reflect this back for confirmation before continuing.

### Step 2 — Define the boundary as a request, not a demand for character change

A workable boundary names:
- The specific behavior you're asking to change.
- The specific alternative you're asking for (what "yes" looks like).
- The part you control if it doesn't change (your follow-through), framed as information, not threat.

Use this scoring check — a boundary should hit all three:

| Property | Pass test | Fail signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Specific** | Names one observable behavior and one concrete ask | "Be more considerate" |
| **Self-owned** | The consequence is something *you* do, not a punishment you impose on them | "Or else you'll regret it" |
| **Sayable** | The user can read it aloud without flinching or over-explaining | A paragraph of justification |

### Step 3 — Draft the script in the user's voice

Produce a short spoken script using a plain structure:
- **Observation** (behavior, neutral).
- **Impact** (on me, brief).
- **Request** (the specific ask).
- **My follow-through** (what I'll do, stated calmly, only if relevant).

Keep it under ~5 sentences. No diagnosing them, no "you always," no pre-apology ("I'm so sorry to even bring this up..."). Offer one warmer variant and one firmer variant so the user can match the relationship.

### Step 4 — Pre-load the predictable pushback

Name the 2–3 most likely reactions (defensiveness, guilt-tripping, "you're overreacting," tears, counter-accusation, or genuine surprise) and give the user one short, non-escalating reply to each. The replies hold the boundary without re-litigating the whole history. Include a "broken record" line the user can repeat if the other person tries to argue them out of it.

### Step 5 — Lock the follow-through

State the one thing the user will actually do — by when — that does not require the other person's cooperation. This is what makes a boundary real. If the user has no self-owned follow-through, the boundary is a wish; say so and help build one.

### Step 6 — Rehearsal note

Tell the user to say the script out loud once before the real conversation, and flag the single sentence most likely to come out shaky so they can practice it.

## Constraints

### Must
- Ground the boundary in one observable behavior and one concrete impact.
- Produce a script the user can actually speak — short, first-person, jargon-free.
- Tie the boundary to a self-owned follow-through the user controls.
- Prepare for the most likely pushback with non-escalating replies.
- Keep the tone direct and warm; a boundary is a request between equals, not a verdict.

### Must Not
- Coach boundary-setting with an unsafe or abusive person (refuse and route to help).
- Frame the boundary as changing the other person's character or personality.
- Build the consequence as a punishment, ultimatum, or manipulation.
- Pad the script with apologies, hedging, or long justifications.
- Diagnose the other person ("they're a narcissist") or the user (mental-health labels).
- Promise the boundary will "work" — the user controls the ask and the follow-through, not the outcome.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Characterization smuggled in as behavior.** "They're controlling" is not a behavior. Push for the specific action ("they check my location and ask why I didn't reply within an hour") before drafting.
2. **Consequence that depends on them.** "I need you to respect me" is not enforceable. The follow-through must be something the user does regardless of the other person's response.
3. **Boundary used as a weapon.** If the request is really about winning, punishing, or controlling the other person, name that — it's not a boundary, and it won't produce the relationship the user wants.
4. **Over-justification creep.** If the script grows past a few sentences with mounting reasons, it's drifting into defending the right to ask. Cut it back; a boundary doesn't need a legal brief.
5. **Mistaking a safety situation for a communication problem.** Re-screen the inputs: coercion, threats, monitoring, financial control, or fear are not boundary-script material. Route to professional support instead.
6. **Assuming one conversation fixes a long pattern.** Set the expectation that the user may need to restate the boundary calmly more than once; that's normal, not failure.

## Expected Output

A boundary plan with: the cleaned-up behavior+impact, the boundary stated as a request, two script variants (warmer / firmer), pushback responses, the self-owned follow-through, and a rehearsal note.

### Output Format

```
## What you're actually asking for
- Behavior: [observable]
- Impact on you: [concrete]
- The boundary (one sentence): [specific request + your follow-through]

## Script — warmer version
[≤5 sentences, first person]

## Script — firmer version
[≤5 sentences, first person]

## When they push back
| Likely reaction | Your reply (non-escalating) |
|---|---|
| [reaction] | [reply] |
Broken-record line: "[one line you can repeat]"

## Your follow-through (you control this)
- What you'll do: [...]
- By when: [...]

## Rehearsal note
Say it out loud once. The line most likely to wobble: "[...]"
```

## Example Output

```
## What you're actually asking for
- Behavior: My brother drops his kids at my place for "an hour" on weekends and comes back 4–5 hours later, often without texting.
- Impact on you: I cancel my own Saturday plans, I'm exhausted by evening, and I've started dreading his calls.
- The boundary (one sentence): I can watch the kids until 2pm with notice the day before; after that I'll take them to his place or to our parents'.

## Script — warmer version
"Hey — I love having the kids, genuinely. But the open-ended Saturdays have stopped working for me; I end up canceling my own stuff. Going forward, I can take them if you ask the day before, and I'll have them until 2. If you're running late past that, I'll bring them over to Mom and Dad's. That keeps it good for both of us."

## Script — firmer version
"I need to change how the Saturday childcare works. I can do it with a day's notice, until 2pm. Past 2, I'll drop them at your place or Mom and Dad's. I'm not able to do the all-day, no-notice version anymore."

## When they push back
| Likely reaction | Your reply (non-escalating) |
|---|---|
| "Wow, you're family, I thought you wanted to help." | "I do help, and I want to keep helping — that's why I'm setting a version I can actually keep doing." |
| "I can't always plan a day ahead." | "I get that. When you can't, I won't be available that day — no hard feelings." |
| Guilt/silence | "I know this is a change. The offer's real: a day's notice, until 2." |
Broken-record line: "A day's notice, until 2 — that's what I can do."

## Your follow-through (you control this)
- What you'll do: At 2pm I pack the kids' bag and drive them to our parents' or his place; I text him that I'm doing it.
- By when: Starting this coming Saturday.

## Rehearsal note
Say it out loud once. The line most likely to wobble: "Past 2, I'll drop them at your place." Practice saying it without an apology after it.
```

## Verification

- [ ] The boundary names one observable behavior and one concrete impact.
- [ ] The request is specific and not aimed at changing the other person's character.
- [ ] The follow-through is something the user controls without the other person's agreement.
- [ ] A spoken script exists in two tones, each ≤5 sentences, with no pre-apology or "you always."
- [ ] The 2–3 most likely pushbacks each have a non-escalating reply, plus a broken-record line.
- [ ] Inputs were re-screened for safety; abuse/coercion would have triggered a refusal and referral.
- [ ] No diagnosis of either party; no promise that the boundary will change the other person.
- [ ] A rehearsal step is included with the shakiest line flagged.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** Fixes the goal as a single statable boundary with a self-owned follow-through.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** Moves from complaint → behavior → boundary → script → pushback → follow-through in order.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** Scores the boundary across specific / self-owned / sayable before drafting.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** The Must/Must-Not rails keep the boundary from becoming an ultimatum or a character attack.
- **DD-02 (Vague-to-Concrete Translation):** Converts characterizations and resentments into observable behavior + concrete ask.
- **QA-20 (Dual-Failure Quality Test):** Guards both failure directions — a boundary that's cruel/ultimatum-like, and one so hedged it never actually asks.
