# Dating Again After Divorce or a Long Relationship

**Objective:** Decide whether, how and at what pace to start dating again — based on
observable readiness, a clear short list of what you want and will not accept this time,
and a paced first two months that you learn from rather than endure.

**When to Use:**
- You are divorced, separated, widowed or out of a long relationship, and thinking about
  dating for the first time in years.
- Friends say "you should get back out there" and you are not sure you want to, or how.
- You have started dating and keep ending up with a version of your ex, or rushing.
- You have children and want to date without unsettling them.
- **Not this prompt if** the relationship has just ended and daily life has collapsed — rebuild
  first with `domain-personal-development/prompts/life-transitions/lifetransition_post_breakup_rebuild.md`.
  For **when and how to tell your children or introduce a partner**,
  `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/divorce/parenting_divorce_new_partner_introduction_timing.md`;
  for co-parent communication, `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/co-parenting/`. For meeting
  matches safely and spotting scams or coercion, `relationships_early_dating_safety_plan.md`.
  Couples therapy and joint relationship work are clinical, not this prompt.

**Audience:** An adult doing this for themselves. Non-clinical. If grief, persistent low mood,
or trauma from an abusive relationship is the main issue, see `domain-psychology/` and a
licensed professional first. If an ex is monitoring, threatening or following you, that is a
safety matter — contact a domestic-violence service (in the US, the National Domestic Violence
Hotline, 1-800-799-7233; elsewhere `[VERIFY: local service]`).

## Inputs / Context

1. **The ending:** how long the relationship lasted, how long since it ended, legal status
   (divorce final, pending, separated), and whether you live with the ex or children.
2. **How you are now:** sleep, routines, friendships, how often you think about the ex and
   with what charge.
3. **Why now:** in your own words — companionship, sex, fun, a future partner, proving
   something, not wanting to be alone, others' pressure.
4. **What the last relationship taught you:** your own patterns, not only theirs.
5. **Children and custody schedule**, if any.
6. **Time and energy available** for dating, and what you are willing to try (apps, friends'
   introductions, activities).

## Method

1. **Read readiness against signals, not a calendar (DS-01).** There is no reliable "one month
   per year married" rule. Look at five signals and mark each yes / partly / no:
   - You can talk about the ex most days without strong anger, longing or tears taking over.
   - Your week has its own structure — routines, friends, interests — that does not need a
     partner to hold it up.
   - You want to meet someone for who *they* are, not mainly to fill a gap or show the ex.
   - You could hear "I'm not interested" from a date without it confirming you are unlovable.
   - Practical separation is settled enough — legal, money and housing. If a divorce is pending,
     ask your family-law attorney whether dating could affect it in your jurisdiction.
   Result: **ready**, **ready with care** (name the shaky signal and how you will watch it), or
   **not yet** (name the one thing to rebuild first and a date to re-check).
2. **Turn lessons into a ranked list (DP-29).** From the old relationship, separate what was
   about them from what was about you (how you handled conflict, gave up friends, ignored early
   doubts). Then write: three **non-negotiables** (values and behaviour — honesty, kindness to
   your kids, no heavy drinking — not height or job title); three to five **strong preferences**;
   and **nice-to-haves**. Ranked, so you can decide when someone has some but not all.
3. **Choose the shape you want now.** Casual and fun, companionship, a serious partner, open to
   remarriage or not, more children or not. Shape can change; saying it now prevents drifting
   into a relationship you did not choose.
4. **Design the first two months as an experiment (DP-11).** Low stakes: coffee or a walk,
   45–90 minutes, one date a week or fewer at first. Your own pacing rules: what you share early
   (not the whole divorce story on date one), how fast physical intimacy goes (your pace,
   renegotiable only by you), no introducing anyone to the children during this period.
5. **Debrief after each date.** Three questions: How did I feel with them — safe, interested,
   myself? Did their words match their actions? Which of my non-negotiables did I see evidence
   for or against? After four to six dates in total, review the pattern, not single dates.
6. **Protect the children's time.** Date on your child-free nights; do not cancel kids' plans
   for dates; no new person around the kids in the first months. When and how to tell them, and
   coordinating with the other parent, go to the parenting prompts named above.
7. **Check for false readiness and false conclusions (QA-12).** A rebound feeling of relief is
   not the same as readiness; one bad date is not proof "dating isn't for me"; a strong early
   rush is not proof of a good match.

## Output Format

```
# Dating-again plan — [name], [time since the relationship ended], [kids: y/n]
## Readiness | Signal | Yes / partly / no | Evidence |
Verdict: [ready / ready with care: … / not yet: rebuild … re-check on …]
## What I want now: shape · non-negotiables (3) · strong preferences · nice-to-haves
## Lessons I'm taking (mine, not theirs)
## First two months: where I'll meet people · pace · pacing rules · child-free nights
## Debrief questions (after every date) · review after 4–6 dates
## Routed: kids/co-parent → … · safety → … · support → …
```

## Verification

- [ ] Readiness is judged on the five signals with evidence, not on elapsed time.
- [ ] Non-negotiables are values or behaviours, ranked, three at most.
- [ ] At least one lesson concerns the user's own pattern.
- [ ] Pacing rules are the user's own and state that only they change them.
- [ ] Children's time is protected; telling them is routed to parenting prompts.
- [ ] Safety, abuse and clinical issues routed, not coached.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Time rules.** Some people are ready at six months, others not at three years.
2. **Checklist partners.** Lists of traits (job, height) predict little; behaviour and values do.
3. **Ex-shaped choices.** Feeling instantly "at home" with someone can mean familiar, not good.
4. **Dating as repair.** Dating to fix loneliness or prove worth often ends badly for both.
5. **One-date verdicts.** Judge the pattern across several dates, not the first awkward one.
6. **Kids as an afterthought.** Instability for children starts with schedule changes, not only
   introductions.

```
| Wanting the person, not the gap | yes | "I miss adult company and being curious about someone" |

| Practical separation | yes | decree final in May; finances split |
Verdict: ready with care — watch the rejection fear; debrief question 1 after each date.

## What I want now: companionship → maybe serious later; not more children.
Non-negotiables: 1) honest about other commitments 2) kind to waiters and kids 3) handles
disagreement without silent treatment. Preferences: active outdoors; lives within 30 min;
has own friends. Nice-to-have: likes books.
## Lessons (mine): I stopped seeing friends during the marriage; I ignored early doubts to
avoid conflict → now: keep Thursday climbing whatever happens; name a doubt within a week.
## First two months: one app + ask two friends for introductions; coffee/walks only, ≤1 per
week on kids-away weeks; no talk of the divorce details on date 1; nobody meets the kids

## Review after 6 dates: 1 Dec.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DS-01 Framework Application** — five observable readiness signals instead of a time rule.
- **DP-29 Value Hierarchy Construction** — ranked non-negotiables, preferences and nice-to-haves.
- **DP-11 Safe Experiment Design** — a low-stakes, paced first two months with debriefs.
- **QA-12 False Positives Identification** — relief, rushes and single bad dates misread as signals.
