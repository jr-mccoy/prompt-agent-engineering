# Map a Recurring Over-Reaction to Its Trigger and the Interpretation In Between

**Objective:** Take one reaction that recurs and comes out larger than the moment warrants, isolate its specific trigger and the split-second interpretation that converts trigger into reaction, and pre-plan one concrete intercept for next time.

**When to use:** You keep snapping at the same kind of comment; a particular tone makes you go cold or hot; you look back and think "why did I react like that — again." Use it on a *recurring* over-reaction, not a one-off. Not for reactions tied to trauma or that feel uncontrollable — those belong with professional support and the clinical resources below.

**Audience:** An individual doing this for themselves. Not for diagnosing why someone else over-reacts. Not clinical. If your reactions feel out of your control, are tied to past trauma, or put you or others at risk, this everyday audit is not a substitute for professional support — see `domain-psychology/client-self-use/coping-by-concern/clientself_anger_time_out_script_builder.md`, `domain-psychology/client-self-use/coping-by-concern/clientself_trauma_window_of_tolerance_check.md`, and a licensed professional.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **The reaction, named.** What you actually do when it fires (snap, go silent, shut down, over-explain, leave, get sarcastic). Behavior, not feeling.
2. **2–4 recent instances.** For each: where you were, who was involved, and the specific thing said or done right before you reacted. Concrete, one line each.
3. **The size mismatch.** In each instance, roughly how big the reaction was versus how big the situation was, in your honest estimate.
4. **What you told yourself in the instant (if recoverable).** The half-second thought between the trigger and the reaction ("they think I'm stupid," "he's doing it on purpose," "I'm being dismissed").
5. **The cost.** What these reactions cost you — a relationship strain, a reputation, your own peace.

If you can only give one instance, note it — a pattern needs at least two to locate the common trigger reliably.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Find the common trigger
Lay the instances (input 2) side by side and extract what they share. The trigger is rarely the whole situation — it's a specific feature: a tone, a word, being interrupted, being corrected in front of others, someone not replying. Name the smallest common feature across instances. If two distinct triggers appear, keep the one with the higher cost (input 5).

### Step 2 — Surface the interpretation in the middle
Between the trigger and the reaction is a fast interpretation — the meaning you assign in the instant. From input 4, name it as a sentence: "when [trigger] happens, I read it as [meaning]." Classify the meaning:

| Interpretation type | The instant read |
|---|---|
| Threat-to-status | "I'm being disrespected / made small" |
| Threat-to-competence | "I'm being shown up / exposed as not good enough" |
| Threat-to-autonomy | "I'm being controlled / cornered" |
| Threat-to-belonging | "I'm being rejected / left out" |
| Old-tape | the read matches an old relationship, not this person |

### Step 3 — Test the interpretation against the evidence
For the interpretation you named, ask: in these specific instances, what actual evidence supports that read, and what evidence contradicts it? Usually the reaction is to the interpretation, not the event — and the interpretation is a fast guess, sometimes right, often inflated. State which.

### Step 4 — Name the gap where an intercept fits
The reaction fires fast, but there's a gap — the moment you notice the body signal (heat, tightening, the urge to hit back) before the behavior. Name your earliest reliable warning sign from the instances. This is where an intercept goes.

### Step 5 — Pre-plan one intercept
Design one concrete, rehearsable intercept for the next time the trigger fires, anchored to the warning sign from Step 4. It must be specific and physical: a pause of a stated length, a single sentence to say ("give me a second"), a physical move (hands down, step back), or a question to ask instead of reacting ("what did you mean by that?"). Pick one. Rehearse the exact words/motion now so it's available under pressure.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Extract one common trigger from at least two instances.
- Name the interpretation as an explicit trigger→meaning sentence.
- Test that interpretation against the instance evidence.
- Produce exactly one rehearsable intercept anchored to a warning sign.

### Must Not
- Diagnose or explain the reaction as a disorder or a fixed trait ("you're just an angry person").
- Excuse the reaction or blame the other party — the point is the intercept, not fault.
- Offer a generic "count to ten / take deep breaths" without anchoring it to this specific trigger and warning sign.
- Produce a menu of coping strategies — one intercept.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't name the situation as the trigger.** "Meetings" isn't a trigger; "being corrected in a meeting" might be. Narrow to the specific feature.
2. **Don't assume the interpretation is wrong.** Sometimes the read is accurate and the *size* of the reaction is the problem, not the read. Separate accuracy from proportion.
3. **Don't treat a one-off as a pattern.** If only one instance exists, say the trigger is provisional and gather more before pre-planning.
4. **Don't over-attribute to "old tapes."** Only class it as old-tape if the read clearly exceeds what this person/situation supports and echoes an old relationship.
5. **Don't design an intercept that requires calm you won't have.** The intercept must work *while* activated — that's why it's anchored to the earliest warning sign, not to "once you've calmed down."
6. **Don't handle trauma-linked or uncontrollable reactivity here.** If the reaction feels beyond choice or is trauma-connected, route to professional support rather than pre-planning a self-intercept.

## Output Format

```
## The reaction
[input 1], costing [input 5].

## Common trigger (from instances)
[Smallest shared feature across the instances.]

## The interpretation in between
"When [trigger], I read it as [meaning]." Type: [Threat-to-status/competence/autonomy/belonging / Old-tape].

## Interpretation vs. evidence
Supports: [...]. Contradicts: [...]. Verdict: [accurate but oversized / inflated read].

## Earliest warning sign
[The body/thought signal that fires before the behavior.]

## The one intercept (rehearse now)
[Exact words / pause length / physical move, triggered by the warning sign.]

Predicted check: next time [trigger] fires, you catch the [warning sign] and run the intercept at least once. Success is the intercept happening, not the trigger disappearing.
```

## Verification

- [ ] One common trigger extracted from ≥2 instances (or flagged provisional).
- [ ] Interpretation stated as an explicit trigger→meaning sentence and typed.
- [ ] Interpretation tested against evidence; accuracy separated from proportion.
- [ ] Earliest warning sign named.
- [ ] Exactly one rehearsable intercept, anchored to the warning sign.
- [ ] No trait-diagnosis, no fault-assignment, no generic coping menu.
- [ ] Trauma-linked/uncontrollable reactivity routed to professional support.
