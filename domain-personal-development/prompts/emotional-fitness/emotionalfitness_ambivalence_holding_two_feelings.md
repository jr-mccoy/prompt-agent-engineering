# Hold Two Opposed True Feelings Without Collapsing to One

**Objective:** Take a situation where you feel two opposed things at once and name both fully, surface what each feeling is protecting or pointing at, and let both stand — instead of forcing yourself to decide which is the "real" one.

**When to use:** You left a job / ended a relationship / reached a milestone and feel relief *and* grief; you love someone *and* you're furious with them; you're excited about a change *and* dreading it, and the mix makes you feel confused or fake. Not for choosing between two options — that's a decision prompt; this is for accepting two feelings, not picking one path.

**Audience:** An individual doing this for themselves. Not for interpreting someone else's mixed feelings. Not clinical. If the ambivalence is about a major relationship or is accompanied by persistent distress, hopelessness, or feeling unable to function, this skill practice is not a substitute for professional support — see `domain-psychology/client-self-use/` and a licensed professional.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **The situation.** One factual sentence about what happened or is happening.
2. **The two feelings, named.** Both, in your words — even if they seem to cancel out ("relieved and devastated," "grateful and resentful").
3. **The pull to collapse.** Which feeling you keep telling yourself is the "real" or "correct" one, and why (often the one that seems more acceptable or more flattering).
4. **What each feeling is reacting to.** For each of the two feelings, the specific part of the situation it attaches to, if you can name it.

If you can genuinely only find one feeling, this isn't the right prompt — use `emotionalfitness_emotion_labeling_practice.md` to name what's there first.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Give both feelings equal standing
Restate the two feelings side by side as equally valid, present-tense facts: "You feel X. You also feel Y. Both are true right now." Do not rank them, resolve them, or treat one as a phase to pass through into the other. The default failure here is collapsing early.

### Step 2 — Attach each to its object
Using input 4, connect each feeling to the specific part of the situation it's responding to. Opposed feelings usually attach to *different aspects* of the same thing — relief at the pressure ending, grief at the person leaving. When you find the different objects, the contradiction often stops feeling like a contradiction.

### Step 3 — Name what each feeling is protecting
Every feeling in the pair is guarding something. For each, name what it protects or values:

| Feeling role | What it's usually guarding |
|---|---|
| The "positive" one (relief, love, excitement) | a real gain, a value moving forward, freedom |
| The "negative" one (grief, anger, dread) | something that mattered, a boundary, a loss worth honoring |

State it as: "The [feeling] is protecting [specific thing]." Neither is the enemy of the other.

### Step 4 — Examine the pull to collapse
Take input 3 — the feeling you keep insisting is the "real" one — and ask why the *other* one feels unacceptable. Common reasons: it seems ungrateful, weak, disloyal, or contradicts how you want to see yourself. Name the reason. The pressure to collapse is usually social or self-image, not accuracy.

### Step 5 — Let both stand, and name the one honest move
The output is not resolution — it's holding. State the both/and plainly. Then, if there's one small action that honors *both* feelings at once, name it (a way to grieve the loss while still moving forward; a way to voice the anger without discarding the love). If no single action honors both, the move is simply to stop forcing a merge and let the two coexist for now — and that is a legitimate endpoint.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Hold both feelings as equally true; refuse to rank or resolve them prematurely.
- Attach each feeling to its specific object in the situation.
- Name what each feeling is protecting.
- End in either one both-honoring action or an explicit "let both stand" — never a forced merge.

### Must Not
- Declare one feeling the "real" one and the other a phase.
- Moralize (no "you should just be grateful," no "your anger is valid so act on it").
- Force a resolution or a decision — this prompt accepts, it doesn't choose.
- Recommend generic acceptance clichés ("hold space," "honor your journey") without the specific both/and.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't collapse to the flattering feeling.** The pull to keep only the acceptable one (gratitude over resentment) is exactly the failure this prompt exists to prevent.
2. **Don't manufacture a second feeling.** If only one is genuinely present, don't invent an opposite for symmetry — route to labeling instead.
3. **Don't treat opposed feelings as a problem to fix.** Ambivalence about a real gain-with-loss is accurate, not dysfunctional. It doesn't need curing.
4. **Don't merge them into a mushy middle.** "Bittersweet" can erase both; keep the two distinct and named.
5. **Don't turn holding into deciding.** If the situation actually needs a choice (stay/go), that's a separate decision task — say so rather than resolving the feeling into an action.
6. **Don't minimize major-relationship ambivalence.** Persistent, heavy ambivalence about a core relationship or life direction may warrant professional support, not just a holding exercise.

## Output Format

```
## The situation
[input 1].

## Both feelings, equally true
You feel [X]. You also feel [Y]. Both are real right now.

## What each attaches to
- [X] is responding to [specific aspect].
- [Y] is responding to [specific aspect].

## What each protects
- [X] is protecting [thing].
- [Y] is protecting [thing].

## The pull to collapse
You keep insisting [Z] is the real one because [reason]. That pressure is [social / self-image], not accuracy.

## The honest move
[One action honoring both — OR — "Let both stand; no merge required right now."]

Predicted check: next time this comes up, you can say both feelings out loud without needing to pick one. If you can't stop trying to resolve it, the situation may actually need a decision or more support.
```

## Verification

- [ ] Both feelings held as equally true; neither ranked as the "real" one.
- [ ] Each feeling attached to a specific object in the situation.
- [ ] What each feeling protects is named.
- [ ] The pull-to-collapse reason surfaced and labeled non-accuracy.
- [ ] Ends in a both-honoring action or an explicit "let both stand."
- [ ] No forced resolution, no moralizing, no acceptance clichés.
- [ ] Heavy/persistent major-relationship ambivalence routed to professional support.
