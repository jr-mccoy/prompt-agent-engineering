# Distinguish Mentors From Sponsors and Design the Ask

**Objective:** Correctly classify the people who could help your career as potential mentors (give advice) or sponsors (spend their own capital advancing you), then produce one specific ask to one person, paired with the concrete value you offer back.

**When to use:** You have "mentors" but your career isn't moving; you're told to "find a sponsor" and don't know how; or you have relationships with senior people and want to convert one into active advocacy. Not for the actual outreach message — that's `domain-product-management/`. Not for building peer relationships across teams — use `stakeholder_cross_team_alliance.md`.

**Audience:** An individual cultivating their own mentors and sponsors. Not for assigning mentors to others or running a mentorship program, not clinical. If the underlying issue is chronic isolation or belief that no one would help you, that read may be distorted — see `domain-psychology/` if it's persistent.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Your near-term career goal.** The specific thing a sponsor or mentor would help with: a promotion, a role change, a high-visibility project, breaking into a new area. Concrete, within ~12 months.
2. **Candidate people — 5–10.** Real people with some existing relationship to you (any strength). For each: *name/role → their seniority relative to you → do they have decision power or influence over your goal? → current relationship strength (cold / warm / close) → last real interaction.*
3. **What each candidate has actually done for you or others.** Observed evidence: gave you advice, put your name forward, defended someone in a room, made an intro, opened a door. Distinguishes talkers from actors.
4. **What you can genuinely offer.** Skills, effort, information, reach, or work you could do that a senior person would actually value — not "gratitude." Be honest about what's real.
5. **Your goal's decision surface.** Who actually decides the thing in input 1 (the panel, the hiring manager, the calibration room), so you can tell whose advocacy would matter.

If input 2 lists fewer than 4 real people, or input 3 is empty for all of them, refuse and ask for more. Sponsorship is built on existing relationships and observed behavior, not on cold strangers.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Apply the mentor/sponsor distinction as a hard classification

For each candidate, classify their *potential role* using this fixed test:

| Role | Core function | Tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Mentor** | Advises you in private | Gives you their time and judgment |
| **Sponsor** | Advocates for you in rooms you're not in | Spends their own reputation/capital on your behalf |
| **Both** | Advises *and* advocates | Rare; treat as sponsor for the ask |
| **Neither (yet)** | Friendly but no relevant capital or will | Warm but can't or won't move your goal |

The decisive question for sponsor potential: does this person have *capital relevant to input 1's decision surface* (Step below), and have they *ever spent capital on anyone* (input 3)? A kind, wise person with no relevant capital is a mentor, not a sponsor — misclassifying them is the central error.

### Step 2 — Filter sponsor candidates against the decision surface

Cross-check each "Sponsor" or "Both" candidate against input 5. A sponsor only helps if their capital touches the actual decision — a beloved senior person in a different org may have no influence over *your* promotion panel. Downgrade any sponsor candidate whose capital doesn't reach your decision surface to "mentor" or "neither."

### Step 3 — Rank by realistic convertibility

Score the remaining candidates on two axes: **relevance** (capital touches the goal) and **relationship readiness** (warm enough that an ask isn't a cold imposition). The best target is high on both. A high-relevance, cold-relationship person needs relationship-building first — that's a different, slower move; flag it but don't make it the ask.

### Step 4 — Design the reciprocal exchange

Sponsorship is not charity; it runs on the sponsor looking good for backing you and on real value flowing back. For the top target, name:
- **What you're asking for** — specific and proportionate to the relationship (a mentor ask is advice; a sponsor ask is a specific advocacy act: "mention my work to X," "put me forward for Y").
- **Why backing you is safe for them** — the evidence that makes their advocacy low-risk (your track record, a visible win).
- **What flows back** — the concrete value from input 4, offered without making it transactional-feeling.

### Step 5 — Produce one ask

Output **one** ask to **one** person: role-appropriate (don't make a sponsor ask of someone who's only a mentor), inside the decision surface, and paired with the reciprocal value and the safety evidence. Specific and time-bounded — a particular request in a particular upcoming interaction, not "find a sponsor."

State the predicted check: what the person would do or say next if the ask lands.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Classify every candidate as mentor / sponsor / both / neither with the capital-and-behavior test.
- Filter sponsor candidates against the actual decision surface.
- Pair the ask with concrete reciprocal value and safety-for-them evidence.
- Produce exactly one ask to one person, role-appropriate.

### Must Not
- Recommend asking a mentor to do a sponsor's job (advocacy they have no capital for).
- Treat "gratitude" or "I'll make you proud" as reciprocal value — name something real.
- Suggest transactional, quid-pro-quo-flavored framing that would make a sponsor feel used.
- Recommend collecting more mentors as a substitute for one real sponsor.
- Moralize about networking being inauthentic, or cheerlead the user's potential.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't classify a warm, senior advisor as a sponsor by default.** Warmth and seniority aren't capital-relevant-to-your-goal. The test is whether their advocacy touches your decision surface and whether they've ever spent capital — Steps 1–2.
2. **Don't mistake advice-giving for advocacy.** Someone who gives generously of their time may never put their name on the line. That's a mentor; treating them as a stalled sponsor misreads them.
3. **Don't make a sponsor ask of a cold relationship.** High relevance + cold relationship is a relationship-building situation first; a premature ask burns the option. Flag it, don't fire it.
4. **Don't invent reciprocal value.** If input 4 has nothing a senior person would actually value, the honest move is to build something valuable first, not to fake an exchange.
5. **Don't over-diversify.** One real sponsor beats ten mentors. Collecting more advisors when you need advocacy is avoidance dressed as progress.
6. **Don't assume the person won't help.** A distorted "no one would back me" read is common; the evidence in inputs 2–3, not the feeling, decides who's viable.

## Output Format

```
## Goal + decision surface
Goal: [input 1]. Decided by: [input 5].

## Candidate classification
| Person | Role (mentor/sponsor/both/neither) | Capital relevant to decision surface? | Ever spent capital? | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ... | ... | yes/no | yes/no | cold/warm/close |

## Best convertible target
[Person] — high relevance + warm enough. Runner-up needing relationship-building first: [person] (flagged, not the ask).

## The reciprocal exchange
- Ask: [specific, role-appropriate request]
- Why backing you is safe for them: [track record / visible win]
- What flows back: [real value from input 4]

## One ask (this cycle)
[Specific request to specific person, in specific upcoming interaction, by when.]

Predicted check: if it lands, [person] does/says [observable next act] within [timeframe].
```

## Verification

- [ ] Every candidate classified with the capital-and-behavior test, not by warmth or seniority.
- [ ] Sponsor candidates filtered against the actual decision surface; irrelevant ones downgraded.
- [ ] The ask is role-appropriate (no sponsor ask of a mentor-only person).
- [ ] Reciprocal value is concrete and real, not gratitude or flattery.
- [ ] The target relationship is warm enough for the ask; cold-but-relevant is flagged separately.
- [ ] Exactly one ask to one person, specific, time-bounded, with a predicted observable check.
- [ ] No mentor-hoarding as sponsor-avoidance; no transactional framing; no moralizing.
