# Build a Working Model of Your Manager and Adapt How You Surface Work

**Objective:** Turn scattered observations of your manager into a concrete model of what they prioritize, what pressures they're under, how they process information, and what they reward — then produce one specific change to how you surface your work.

**When to use:** You keep getting surprised by your manager's reactions; your good work isn't landing the way you expect; you have a new manager and want to calibrate fast; or reviews feel misaligned with what you thought mattered. Not for drafting the actual update or message — that's `domain-product-management/`. Not for a broken-trust repair — use `stakeholder_manager_relationship_builder.md`.

**Audience:** An individual calibrating to their own manager for their own effectiveness. Not for managing someone below you, not for HR assessment, not clinical. If the relationship is a source of persistent anxiety or dread rather than friction, that is not a managing-up problem — see `domain-psychology/` and a licensed professional.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Manager basics.** Their role, who *they* report to, how long you've worked under them, and how often you interact (daily / weekly / rarely).
2. **Observed reactions — last 60–90 days.** 6–10 specific moments where your manager reacted visibly: praised something, got tense, redirected, went quiet, escalated, or overrode you. Each as: *what you did / brought → their observable reaction → the context (audience, timing, their week).* Feelings ("they seemed annoyed") don't count without the observable behavior underneath.
3. **What they've explicitly said matters.** Direct statements about priorities, pet peeves, format preferences, or how they want things surfaced.
4. **Their current visible pressures.** Deadlines, a reorg, a project on fire, their own boss's scrutiny — whatever you can actually see bearing down on them.
5. **How you currently surface work.** Where, when, and in what form your work reaches them (standup, Slack, doc, meeting, they find out later).

If you have fewer than 5 observed reactions with real behavior attached, refuse and ask for more. A manager model built on vibes will just reflect your own anxieties back at you.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Derive priorities from reactions, not from the org chart

For each observed reaction in input 2, ask: "What did this reaction protect or advance?" Cluster into 3–5 **revealed priorities** (e.g., "no surprises in front of their boss," "speed over polish on internal work," "customer-facing quality above all"). A priority counts only if it shows up in **two or more** independent reactions. Cross-check against input 3 — where explicit statements and reactions agree, confidence is high; where they disagree, the reactions win, and you name the gap.

### Step 2 — Read their pressures as the weather

Take input 4 and translate each pressure into how it changes your manager's bandwidth and risk tolerance *right now*. A manager under scrutiny from their own boss rewards different things (defensibility, no surprises) than a manager with slack (initiative, experiments). This is temporary weather, not permanent climate — label it as such.

### Step 3 — Classify their communication style on a fixed grid

Place your manager on each of these four axes using evidence from inputs 2–3:

| Axis | Pole A | Pole B |
|---|---|---|
| **Input mode** | Wants the headline first (top-down) | Wants the reasoning first (bottom-up) |
| **Channel** | Written/async (reads, reflects) | Verbal/synchronous (talks it out) |
| **Detail appetite** | Wants the summary, will ask | Wants the detail, distrusts summaries |
| **Cadence** | Wants proactive updates | Wants to be left alone until asked |

Cite the evidence for each placement. If you genuinely can't place an axis, mark it "needs observation" and name the cheap experiment that would reveal it — never guess.

### Step 4 — Name what they actually reward vs. punish

From the praise/tension moments, extract the **reward pattern** (what reliably earns approval) and the **friction pattern** (what reliably creates tension). Be specific and behavioral: "flags risks early, in writing, before they blow up" — not "being competent."

### Step 5 — Find the gap between their model and your current behavior

Compare Steps 1–4 against input 5 (how you currently surface work). Locate the single biggest mismatch: the place where your default behavior collides with their revealed priority, style, or reward pattern. This is where your good work is leaking value.

### Step 6 — Produce one adaptation move — and one line you won't cross

Propose **one** concrete change to how you surface work that closes the Step 5 gap this week — a format change, a channel change, a timing change, a what-you-lead-with change. It must be a specific behavior, not "communicate better."

Then, guarding against sycophancy, name **one thing you will keep doing that they don't obviously reward** — a judgment call, a dissent, a quality bar you hold — so the adaptation is calibration, not capitulation.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Derive at least 3 revealed priorities, each backed by ≥ 2 observed reactions.
- Place the manager on all four communication axes with cited evidence (or an explicit "needs observation").
- Distinguish temporary pressure (weather) from stable priority (climate).
- Produce exactly one adaptation move plus exactly one line-you-won't-cross.

### Must Not
- Recommend flattery, agreeing with things you don't believe, or hiding bad news to please.
- Reduce the manager to "difficult" or "insecure" as an explanation — name the behavior and its likely driver instead.
- Produce a generic "how to manage up" listicle.
- Moralize about the manager's flaws or cheerlead the user's virtue.
- Assume the manager is rational or consistent across all contexts.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't mistake this week's pressure for a permanent trait.** A manager who's terse during a launch isn't necessarily a terse manager. Weather vs. climate is load-bearing.
2. **Don't build the model from a single dramatic incident.** One blowup is noise; the two-reaction minimum exists to filter it.
3. **Don't let the org chart substitute for evidence.** "They're senior so they must want X" is a guess, not an observation. Only reactions and statements count.
4. **Don't confuse your discomfort with their dysfunction.** If a manager consistently asks for detail you find tedious, that's a style-mismatch to adapt to, not proof they're a micromanager.
5. **Don't turn adaptation into self-erasure.** If the "adaptation move" requires you to stop exercising judgment or stop flagging real problems, it's over-fitting — that's what the line-you-won't-cross guards against.
6. **Don't diagnose the manager's psychology.** You're modeling observable behavior to work effectively, not assessing their personality or mental state.

## Output Format

```
## Your manager's revealed priorities
1. [Concrete priority] — evidence: [reaction A, reaction B]
2. ...

## Current pressures (weather, not climate)
- [Pressure] → shifts them toward rewarding [X], away from [Y], for now.

## Communication style (evidence-cited)
| Axis | Placement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Input mode | headline-first / reasoning-first / needs observation | ... |
| Channel | written / verbal / needs observation | ... |
| Detail appetite | summary / detail / needs observation | ... |
| Cadence | proactive updates / leave-alone / needs observation | ... |

## Reward vs. friction pattern
- Reliably rewards: [specific behavior]
- Reliably creates friction: [specific behavior]

## The gap
Your current way of surfacing work — [input 5] — collides with [priority/style/reward] because [one sentence].

## One adaptation move (this week)
[Specific change to how you surface work, by when, observable.]

## One line you won't cross
[The judgment/dissent/quality bar you keep, even unrewarded.]

Predicted check: after this change, expect [observable shift in their reaction] within [timeframe].
```

## Verification

- [ ] At least 3 revealed priorities, each backed by ≥ 2 observed reactions.
- [ ] All four communication axes placed with cited evidence or marked "needs observation" with a named experiment.
- [ ] Temporary pressure is separated from stable priority explicitly.
- [ ] Reward and friction patterns are behavioral and specific, not "be competent."
- [ ] Exactly one adaptation move, physical and time-bounded, tied to the named gap.
- [ ] Exactly one line-you-won't-cross is stated, guarding against sycophancy.
- [ ] No flattery, no news-hiding, no manager psychoanalysis, no generic listicle.
