# Reorg Navigation

**Objective:** Get from "the org chart changed and I don't know what it means for
me" to a sober read of your position, a short list of what to protect, a plan for the
first conversations with your new manager, and dated signals that would tell you to
leave — so the stay-or-go call is made on evidence, not in the first week's anxiety.

**When to Use:**
- A reorganisation has been announced or just landed and your reporting line,
  scope, team, or title has changed.
- You have a new manager you did not choose, and you do not know their mandate.
- Your project was merged, cancelled, or handed to another group.
- You are hearing rumours of a second round and want to prepare without panicking.
- **Not this prompt if** you chose and accepted a new role or promotion — that is
  settling in, `prompts/life-transitions/lifetransition_navigating_new_role.md`.
  If you have already decided to look outside, go to `job-search/`; to weigh an
  internal transfer against leaving, `career-transformation/career_internal_vs_external_move.md`.
  If you are writing a promotion case, that is
  `domain-hr-management/performance-reviews/hr_promotion_case_writer.md`. If you are
  the one planning the reorg or a reduction in force, use
  `domain-hr-management/people-ops/hr_reduction_in_force_plan.md`.

**Audience:** An individual employee living through a reorg they did not design.
Not legal advice: if your pay, title, or duties were cut unilaterally, or you have
been offered a separation agreement, get advice from an employment lawyer or your
union representative before signing anything — rules vary by country and state.

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**
1. **What changed, by source** — the announcement text, what your manager or HR
   told you, and what you have heard informally. Keep the three apart.
2. **Your role before and after** — scope, team, reporting line, title, pay,
   key projects, decision rights.
3. **What you know about the new manager** — their background, the group they came
   from, what they have said so far.
4. **Your stated reason for the reorg** (cost cut, strategy shift, merger,
   leadership change) and how confident you are in it.
5. **Your situation** — financial runway, how long you have been there, unvested
   equity or bonus dates, visa or benefits dependence.

**Optional:** What you would want if you could choose; signals from peers about
how they are reading it.

## Method

1. **Sort changes by evidence (RT-05).** Confirmed in writing / told directly /
   rumour. Only the first two drive decisions; rumours become questions to ask.
2. **Read the mandate.** Reorgs serve a goal — cutting cost, consolidating
   duplicate teams, a new strategy, or a new leader building their own team. Infer
   which from the confirmed facts; your new manager will be judged on it, and your
   value to them depends on it.
3. **Place your position** on four levels using observable signals:
   - **Elevated** — more scope, budget, or headcount; your work named as a priority.
   - **Intact** — same work, different box on the chart.
   - **Diminished** — scope, decision rights, or visibility reduced; project moved.
   - **At risk** — role duplicates another; not in early meetings; work unassigned;
     a hiring freeze plus a "review of roles".
   Cite the signals for the level you choose and the one signal that would move it.
4. **Decide what to protect, ranked (DS-06).** Typical assets: your one flagship
   project, a written record of results, two or three cross-team relationships, your
   skills' fit with the new mandate, title and pay. Name what you will let go
   without a fight; spending capital on everything spends it on nothing.
5. **Plan the first two conversations with the new manager.** First: listen for
   their mandate, their first 90-day goals, and how they want updates. Second (within
   about 3 weeks): a short summary of what you own, its results, and where you can
   help their goal — plus one clarifying question about your role. Ask for any role
   change in writing.
6. **Hold the stay-or-go decision (QA-09).** Quitting is hard to reverse; waiting
   four to eight weeks usually is not. Unless an immediate tripwire fires, set a
   review date rather than deciding now. Use the transition's "neutral zone" (in
   William Bridges' sense — the old arrangement has ended, the new one is not yet
   real) to gather evidence.
7. **Set tripwires (DP-13).** Observable, dated signals that move you to an active
   search: pay or title cut; core project not restored by a date; excluded from
   planning for the next cycle; a second round of cuts announced; the new manager
   hires into your role. Also name the signals that would confirm staying.
8. **Quiet preparation regardless.** Update your record of results, reconnect with
   three people outside the team, and check your finances — cheap insurance that
   does not commit you to leaving.

## Output Format

```
# Reorg read — [date]   Review date: [date]

## What changed
| Change | Confirmed / Told / Rumour | Question to ask |

## Likely mandate: [cost / consolidation / strategy / new leader] — because [..]

## My position: [Elevated / Intact / Diminished / At risk]
Signals: [..]   Would move up if: [..]   Would move down if: [..]

## Protect (ranked) / Let go
1. [..]  2. [..]  3. [..]     Let go: [..]

## New-manager conversations
#1 by [date] — listen for: [..]
#2 by [date] — my one-page summary: [..]; my question: [..]

## Tripwires
Leave signals: [signal — by date]
Stay signals: [signal — by date]

## Quiet preparation this month
[..]
```

## Verification

- [ ] Every change is tagged by source; no rumour drives a decision.
- [ ] Position level cites at least two observable signals.
- [ ] Protect list is ranked and includes an explicit "let go".
- [ ] Both manager conversations are dated and have a purpose.
- [ ] Stay-or-go is deferred to a review date unless a tripwire has already fired.
- [ ] Tripwires are observable and dated, for leaving and for staying.
- [ ] Legal-advice line included where pay, title, or separation is involved.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Reading the chart as a verdict.** A new box is not a demotion; check scope
   and decision rights before concluding anything.
2. **Rumour as fact.** Hallway accounts of "they're cutting our team" spread faster
   than decisions. Turn each into a question for someone who would know.
3. **Deciding in week one.** Early weeks are the noisiest. Leaving before the
   new manager has shown their hand forfeits options you cannot recover.
4. **Treating the new manager as the enemy.** They usually inherited the
   decision too. Their mandate is your best lever.
5. **Optimism as a plan.** "It'll probably be fine" without tripwires is how
   people end up in the second round unprepared.
6. **Ignoring the money dates.** A vesting or bonus date two months out changes
   the cost of leaving; name it.

## Example Output

```
# Reorg read — 2026-10-03   Review date: 2026-11-21

## What changed
| Data-platform team merged into Analytics Engineering under Priya | Confirmed (all-hands email) | — |
| I now report to Priya, not Marco | Told (Marco 1:1) | Is my scope the same? |
| My pipeline-migration project "paused" | Told (Priya's staff note) | Paused until when, and why? |
| "Platform roles being reviewed in Q1" | Rumour | Is there a role review, and what is the criterion? |

## Likely mandate: Consolidation — two teams maintained overlapping pipelines;
Priya came from a cost-reduction role at her last company.

## My position: Diminished
Signals: flagship project paused; not invited to the 30 Sep planning session; title
and pay unchanged.
Would move up if: migration restarted under me, or I own the merged pipeline standard.
Would move down if: migration reassigned; Q1 role review confirmed with my role on it.

## Protect (ranked) / Let go
1. Ownership of the pipeline standard for the merged team (fits the consolidation goal).
2. Written results: migration cut nightly job failures from 9% to 1.5%.
3. Relationships with the two analytics leads who depend on the pipelines.
Let go: my old team's on-call rota design; the separate platform roadmap.

## New-manager conversations
#1 by 2026-10-10 — listen for: her 90-day goals, how "paused" is decided, update format.
#2 by 2026-10-24 — one page: what I own, the failure-rate result, an offer to draft
one pipeline standard for both teams. Question: "What would you need to see from me
by January?" Ask for any change to my role in writing.

## Tripwires
Leave signals: migration reassigned (any date); pay or title change; excluded from
the next planning session (by 2026-11-15); role review confirmed with no criterion shared.
Stay signals: given the pipeline standard by 2026-11-21; invited to Q1 planning.

## Quiet preparation this month
Update results doc; coffee with 3 former colleagues; note bonus pays 2027-03-01 —
leaving before then costs about 8% of annual pay.
```

## Techniques Used

- **RT-05 Evidence-Based Reasoning** — confirmed, told, and rumoured changes kept apart.
- **DS-06 Prioritization and Severity Guidance** — a ranked protect list with an explicit let-go.
- **DP-13 Kill Signal Definition** — dated, observable tripwires for leaving and for staying.
- **QA-09 Reversibility Assessment** — defers an irreversible quit while cheap evidence accumulates.
