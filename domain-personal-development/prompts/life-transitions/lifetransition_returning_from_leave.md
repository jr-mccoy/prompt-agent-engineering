# Re-Enter Work After Parental, Medical, or Sabbatical Leave Without Pretending Nothing Changed

**Objective:** Produce a realistic re-onboarding for the first weeks back that recalibrates capacity to what the leave actually changed, resets one boundary, and re-onboards on the work — instead of snapping to the pre-leave pace.

**When to use:** You're returning from extended leave — parental, medical/health, caregiving, or sabbatical — and are planning or living the first weeks back. Useful when you feel pressure (internal or external) to immediately perform at your old level. Not for deciding whether to return at all, and not for the new-role case where the job itself changed (see `lifetransition_navigating_new_role.md`).

**Audience:** An individual re-entering their own work after leave. Not for a manager re-onboarding a returner, not clinical. If you're returning from medical/mental-health leave, honor your clinician's guidance first — persistent symptoms, relapse, or inability to cope on return are medical questions, not productivity ones; see `domain-psychology/` and your treating professional.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Leave facts.** Type (parental / medical / caregiving / sabbatical), length, and any clinician or medical restrictions on return (hours, duties, phased return). For medical leave, these restrictions are binding and override everything below.
2. **What changed during leave.** Two lists: what changed *in you* (energy, health, priorities, new caregiving load, physical limits) and what changed *at work* (team, tools, projects, reorg, your role, who's gone).
3. **Pre-leave baseline.** Your working pattern before leave: rough hours, intensity, always-on habits, the boundaries you did or didn't have.
4. **The return shape.** How you're coming back — full-time day one, phased, reduced — and the first-week expectations (yours and, if known, your employer's).
5. **The pressure.** One sentence on the specific pressure to snap back (a boss's expectation, a project mid-flight, your own identity as "the reliable one," financial need).

If input 1's leave type and any medical restrictions, or input 2, are missing, refuse and ask. Re-entry differs fundamentally by leave type, and medical restrictions are non-negotiable inputs.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Set the capacity floor and ceiling

Establish realistic capacity for the first 2–4 weeks, not the pre-leave baseline:

- If **medical/mental-health** leave: the clinician's restrictions (input 1) are the hard ceiling. State them as fixed. Do not design above them.
- If **parental**: factor the ongoing home load (input 2) and sleep reality — capacity at work is net of what's spent overnight and at home.
- If **sabbatical**: the constraint is re-onboarding and re-immersion, not physical capacity — but resist the urge to prove the time off was "worth it" by overperforming.

Name a realistic first-weeks capacity as a fraction of pre-leave (e.g., ~60–70%), and say what it's based on.

### Step 2 — Map the two change-sets to actions

For input 2, translate each change into a re-onboarding need:

| Change type | Re-onboarding need |
|---|---|
| Changed in you (energy/health/priorities/load) | A boundary or pacing adjustment |
| Changed at work (team/tools/projects/role) | A catch-up action (a briefing, a doc, a person to ask) |

List the top 3 work-side changes that most need catching up on, ranked by how much they gate your effectiveness.

### Step 3 — Reset one boundary

The pre-leave pattern (input 3) usually included boundary gaps that the leave has now made unsustainable (always-on evenings, no lunch, saying yes to everything). Pick the single boundary whose absence would most quickly re-deplete you, and script it concretely — the specific behavior, and if it needs stating to others, the words. One boundary, held, beats five intended.

### Step 4 — Address the pressure (input 5)

Name what the pressure is and whether it's external (a real expectation to negotiate) or internal (an identity as the reliable/tireless one). For external, name the specific conversation to reset expectations. For internal, name the belief and the one-sentence correction: returning at recalibrated capacity is re-onboarding, not failing.

### Step 5 — Design the first-week re-onboarding

Produce a concrete first-week plan: the top catch-up actions from Step 2, at the capacity from Step 1, protected by the boundary from Step 3. Front-load re-immersion (reading, 1:1s, context) over immediate high-stakes delivery. One realistic week, not an ambitious one.

### Step 6 — The one anchoring commitment

Name the single most important thing to protect in week one (the boundary, the capacity ceiling, or the one catch-up that unblocks everything else), with an observable check.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Treat medical/clinician restrictions (input 1) as a hard, non-overridable ceiling.
- Set first-weeks capacity below pre-leave baseline, and justify the fraction.
- Reset exactly one boundary, scripted concretely.
- Front-load re-immersion over high-stakes delivery in week one.

### Must Not
- Design a "hit the ground running" plan that ignores what the leave changed.
- Prescribe above a stated medical restriction under any productivity rationale.
- Recommend generic "communicate with your manager" without a specific scripted reset.
- Frame recalibrated capacity as underperformance or something to apologize for.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't ignore binding medical limits.** For medical/mental-health leave, clinician restrictions govern; no re-onboarding plan may exceed them, however motivated the returner is.
2. **Don't design for pre-leave capacity.** The leave changed something real; planning at the old baseline guarantees re-depletion or, for parental returners, a collision with the home load.
3. **Don't confuse eagerness with readiness.** Wanting to prove yourself (especially post-sabbatical or to counter guilt) is not evidence of capacity. Cap it.
4. **Don't reset five boundaries at once.** Overhauling the whole working pattern in week one fails; pick the highest-leverage one.
5. **Don't miss relapse or non-coping.** If returning from mental-health leave and symptoms are returning, that's a medical matter for the treating professional — not a boundary or pacing tweak. Route to `domain-psychology/` and the clinician.
6. **Don't over-catch-up.** Trying to absorb every change at work in week one competes with the capacity ceiling; rank and take the top three.

## Output Format

```
## Capacity (first 2–4 weeks)
Leave type: [...]. Medical ceiling (if any): [binding restrictions, verbatim].
Realistic capacity: ~[X]% of pre-leave, because [basis — home load / health / re-immersion].

## Changes mapped
Changed in you → boundary/pacing: [...].
Top 3 work-side changes to catch up on (ranked): 1) ... 2) ... 3) ...

## Boundary reset (one)
Boundary: [specific behavior]. If it needs stating: "[scripted words]."

## The pressure (input 5)
Type: [external / internal]. Handling: [expectation-reset conversation | belief correction].

## First-week re-onboarding
[Concrete plan: catch-up actions at the capacity level, protected by the boundary, re-immersion front-loaded.]

## Anchoring commitment
Protect above all in week one: [the one thing].
Predicted check: at end of week one, [observable — the ceiling held, the boundary intact, the top catch-up done].
```

## Verification

- [ ] Medical/clinician restrictions treated as a hard ceiling, stated verbatim.
- [ ] First-weeks capacity set below pre-leave baseline, with a justified fraction.
- [ ] Change-sets split into in-you (boundary) and at-work (catch-up), top 3 ranked.
- [ ] Exactly one boundary reset, scripted concretely.
- [ ] Week-one plan front-loads re-immersion over high-stakes delivery.
- [ ] Pressure classified external/internal and handled specifically.
- [ ] No "hit the ground running," no plan exceeding medical limits.
- [ ] Relapse / non-coping routed to the treating professional and `domain-psychology/`.
