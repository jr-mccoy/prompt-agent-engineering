**Purpose:** Give you a plain-language picture of how a family-law case generally works — from the first filing to a final judgment and beyond — and who the key people are and what they do. The goal is basic literacy: you should leave knowing roughly what each stage is and what the roles mean, so you can ask better questions and follow along when your attorney explains what is happening in your specific case. This is **not** a description of your jurisdiction's procedure — every state and country has its own rules, forms, terminology, and timelines. Read it as an orientation, not a map of your actual case.

**When to use:** You are new to family court and want to understand the general shape of a case before you meet with an attorney; you heard a term in your case (GAL, temporary orders, discovery) and want a plain-language explanation before asking your attorney; you want to build your own question list for a consultation.

**When NOT to use:** You want to know what will happen in your specific case, what you should file next, or what a term means in your jurisdiction → that is legal advice; ask your attorney (see `legalprep_attorney_consultation_question_builder.md`). There is an active safety emergency → Safety Block first. You are looking for authoritative procedural rules → court self-help centers and your state's judiciary website are better sources; confirm everything with counsel.

## Safety Block

Stop and use a different pathway if:
- There is domestic violence, threats, stalking, or a protective/restraining order → National Domestic Violence Hotline 1-800-799-7233 (US). Safety planning and protective filings are urgent; work through counsel and your advocate first.
- A child is being abused or is unsafe in either home → Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline 1-800-422-4453 (US); emergencies 911. Report and route to your attorney immediately.
- You or a child is in crisis → 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US).

This prompt is educational support for basic process literacy. It is not a substitute for legal, safety, or clinical services.

## Scope Boundary — Read First

This is a **plain-language orientation to general concepts**. It is **not legal advice, a description of any specific jurisdiction's procedure, or a substitute for an attorney.** Family-law terminology, procedures, timelines, required forms, and available stages **vary substantially by state and country and change over time.** Nothing here should be relied on as a description of how your case will proceed. Every stage name, role title, and step below is a general concept flagged *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.* Your attorney, your court's self-help center, or your state's judiciary website are the right sources for jurisdiction-specific procedure.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Literacy, not advice.** This map builds vocabulary and orientation — it does not tell you what to do, what will happen, or what the law requires in your state.
2. **Everything is jurisdiction-variable.** Every stage name, role, and timeline is a general concept; your state or country may name, sequence, or skip them differently. Flag every concept *confirm with counsel.*
3. **Neutral throughout.** The stages and roles are described factually; this tool does not characterize what any stage means for your outcome.
4. **Questions are the output.** A well-informed question list for your attorney is as valuable as understanding the concepts themselves.
5. **Child's needs, not the other party's conduct.** Where children are involved, stages like evaluation and mediation are framed around what courts generally look at for children's wellbeing.
6. **Stop at the boundary.** Explain concepts; route all strategy, prediction, and procedure specifics to counsel or official court resources.

## How a Family-Law Case Generally Flows
*These are general concepts. Stages, names, and order vary by state and country — confirm with counsel.*

| Stage | What generally happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Petition / Complaint | One party (the "petitioner") files paperwork to start the case. The other party (the "respondent") is served and must respond by a deadline. | *Confirm forms, fees, and deadlines with your court.* |
| 2. Response / Answer | The other party files their response, which may include their own requests ("counterpetition"). | *Timelines vary by jurisdiction.* |
| 3. Temporary Orders | Early in the case, a court may issue temporary orders about custody, support, or use of property while the case is pending. These are not final. | *Not all cases have a temporary-orders stage — confirm with counsel.* |
| 4. Discovery | Both sides exchange information — financial records, documents, and sometimes depositions (sworn, recorded questioning). | *Rules about what must be shared and how vary significantly.* |
| 5. Evaluation / GAL Appointment | In custody matters, a court may appoint a custody evaluator or a guardian ad litem (GAL) to report on the children's best interests. | *Not all cases have this — confirm with counsel.* |
| 6. Mediation / Settlement Talks | Parties meet (often with a neutral mediator) to try to reach an agreement, avoiding a full trial. Many cases settle here. | *Some jurisdictions require mediation; confirm.* |
| 7. Trial / Hearing | If no agreement is reached, a judge hears both sides and makes decisions. | *Procedure, length, and format vary.* |
| 8. Judgment / Final Order | The court issues a final written order covering all issues (divorce decree, parenting plan, support order). | *Must be followed; modification requires a new legal process.* |
| 9. Post-Judgment | Either party can return to court to modify an order (if circumstances change) or enforce one (if the other party doesn't comply). | *Standards for modification vary by state — confirm with counsel.* |

## Roles Glossary
*All roles and titles vary by jurisdiction — confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction.*

| Role | What they generally do | Who they work for |
|---|---|---|
| Judge | Hears the case; makes binding decisions if no settlement. | The court (neutral) |
| Your attorney | Advises you, files documents on your behalf, represents you in hearings. | You |
| Opposing counsel | The other party's attorney. | The other party |
| Guardian ad litem (GAL) | Investigates and reports to the court on the children's best interests; may interview children, parents, teachers. | The children / the court |
| Custody evaluator | A mental-health or other professional who assesses the family and makes parenting recommendations. | The court |
| Mediator | A neutral third party who helps both sides try to reach an agreement; does not decide anything. | Neither party (neutral) |
| Parenting coordinator | In high-conflict cases, a professional who helps parents implement and interpret a parenting plan going forward. | The court / children |
| Court clerk | Administrative staff who process filings, schedule hearings, and maintain records. | The court |

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "In [state], temporary orders always happen at 30 days" | Explain the general concept; flag *confirm with counsel for your jurisdiction* |
| "A GAL will definitely be appointed in your case" | Explain what a GAL is; flag *confirm with counsel* on whether one will be appointed |
| "Your case should take about 6 months" | Note that timelines vary; route the question to counsel |
| "The mediator will help you get what you want" | Explain that the mediator is neutral and cannot advise either party |
| "Discovery means they'll find everything — hide nothing" | Explain what discovery is in plain terms; do not advise on strategy |
| Present the stage list as universally applicable | Always note that stages, names, and order vary by state and country |
| "You're in Stage 3 — here's what you should do" | Orient, not advise; route all "what should I do" to counsel |
| Describe the other party's role in evaluative terms | Describe all roles neutrally |
