# Reading List Curator — Layered Foundations-to-Frontier Path for a Topic

**Objective:** Curate a reading list that is layered, not flat — sequenced from foundations to the live frontier — so a learner builds understanding in the order the field's ideas actually depend on each other. Four layers: the **foundations** (canonical or timeless works the field rests on), the **synthesis** (surveys, textbooks, the maps that organize the field), the **frontier** (recent essential papers and essays where the field is currently moving), and the **contested** (the contrarian works and live disagreements, so the learner sees the field arguing with itself rather than as settled). The list is sequenced with rest-points and note-prompts between layers, and every item carries what it teaches, where it goes in the order, and how the learner will know they've absorbed it.

**When to use:**
- A learner wants to go deep on a topic and needs a path, not a pile of links.
- The topic has enough literature that selection and sequencing actually matter.
- The learner is willing to read actively (take notes, not just consume).

**When NOT to use:**
- The goal is a hands-on skill, not understanding — use `learning_deliberate_practice_designer.md` or `learning_curriculum_designer.md`.
- The user needs a verified, citation-checked source set for a research deliverable — use `research_field_landscape_map.md` / `research_search_strategy_designer.md`, which enforce source verification.
- The topic is so new or niche that a real foundations layer doesn't exist; say so rather than inventing canon.

**Audience:** Self-directed learners, researchers entering a new area, and professionals who want a structured deep-read of a topic.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Topic.** The subject, scoped as tightly as the learner can.
2. **Current level.** What the learner already knows / has read.
3. **Time budget.** Total reading time, or pace (e.g., one item/week).
4. **Format preference.** Books / papers / essays / video / mixed.
5. **Goal of the deep-read.** Understanding for its own sake, a project, a decision, teaching it, or research.
6. **Any constraints.** Access (paywalls, language), specific works to include or exclude.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Organize into the **four layers**: foundations, synthesis, frontier, contested. Every layer must be represented (or its absence explained — e.g., a brand-new field may have no real foundations).
- **Sequence** the list so each item builds on what precedes it. The order is the product; an unordered list isn't a curriculum.
- For each item, state **what it teaches** (the one or two things the learner gets from it) — not a generic summary.
- Include the **contested layer** deliberately: works that disagree with the consensus, so the learner sees the open questions and doesn't mistake one view for the field.
- Insert **rest-points and note-prompts** between layers: a question to answer or a synthesis to write before moving on, so reading converts to understanding.
- For each item, state **how to know it's absorbed** — a check (can explain the core idea, can apply it, can argue against it).
- Respect the **time budget and format preference**; if the ideal list exceeds the budget, cut from the leaves, not the foundations, and say what was cut.
- Do **not fabricate** titles, authors, or attributions. Where a specific real work is named, it must be a real work; where the learner should find "a recent survey," say that rather than inventing a citation.

### Must Not
- Produce a flat, unordered pile. Sequence is the whole point.
- Skip foundations to get to the exciting frontier. Frontier work read without foundations is misread.
- Present the field as settled by omitting the contested layer.
- Pad with tangential items to look comprehensive. A tight, sequenced list beats an exhaustive one.
- Invent citations or attribute ideas to works that don't exist. Flag uncertainty instead.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Scope and calibrate
Confirm the topic's scope and the learner's current level. Set the entry layer: a beginner starts at foundations; someone with the foundations may start at synthesis or frontier. State where this list begins and why.

### Step 2 — Foundations layer
Identify the canonical or timeless works the field rests on — the ones repeatedly cited as origins or bedrock. For each: what it teaches and why it's foundational. If the field is too new for canon, say so and substitute the earliest organizing works.

### Step 3 — Synthesis layer
Identify the surveys, textbooks, and review pieces that map the field — the works that organize the scattered primary material into a structure. For each: what map it provides.

### Step 4 — Frontier layer
Identify the recent essential papers/essays where the field is currently moving. For each: what new thing it adds and what it assumes the reader already knows (this fixes its place after foundations/synthesis).

### Step 5 — Contested layer
Identify the works that argue against the consensus, the open disputes, the contrarian takes. For each: what it disputes and which prior item it's in tension with. This is what turns a reading list into an education.

### Step 6 — Sequence across layers
Order the full list. Generally foundations → synthesis → frontier → contested, but interleave where a contested piece is best read right after the consensus it challenges. State the order and the logic.

### Step 7 — Insert rest-points and note-prompts
Between layers (and after dense items), add a synthesis task: a question to answer, a one-paragraph summary to write, or a diagram to draw. These convert reading into retained understanding.

### Step 8 — Add absorption checks
For each item, a check: "you've absorbed this when you can [explain the core idea in plain language / apply it to a case / state the strongest objection to it]."

### Step 9 — Fit to budget and verify
Trim to the time budget, cutting leaves not foundations; note cuts. Run the checklist; deliver.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Flat pile.** An unordered list of "good stuff." Without sequence, the learner reads frontier before foundations and misunderstands it. Sequence is mandatory.
2. **Frontier-first.** Jumping to the exciting recent work without the foundations it assumes. The reader will absorb the words without the meaning.
3. **Settled-field illusion.** Omitting the contested layer, leaving the learner thinking one school's view is the whole field. Always include the live disagreements.
4. **Generic "what it teaches."** Summaries that could describe any book on the topic. State the specific one or two things *this* item delivers.
5. **No synthesis tasks.** A list with no note-prompts produces consumption, not understanding. Insert rest-points.
6. **Fabricated citations.** Inventing a plausible-sounding paper or misattributing an idea. Name only real works; otherwise say "find a recent survey on X."
7. **Comprehensiveness padding.** Adding tangential items to seem thorough, diluting the path. Keep it tight; cut leaves.
8. **No absorption check.** No way for the learner to know they've actually got it before moving on. Add a check per item.

```
# Reading Path — [topic]
Entry layer: [foundations / synthesis / frontier], because [learner level].
Budget: [time/pace]. Format: [preference].

## Layer 1 — Foundations
| # | Work (real title/author) | What it teaches | Absorbed when you can... |
|---|--------------------------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | | | |

>>> Rest-point: [synthesis task / question to answer before continuing] <<<

## Layer 2 — Synthesis
| # | Work | Map it provides | Absorbed when you can... |
|---|------|-----------------|--------------------------|

>>> Rest-point: [...] <<<

## Layer 3 — Frontier
| # | Work | What's new in it | Assumes you know | Absorbed when you can... |
|---|------|------------------|------------------|--------------------------|

>>> Rest-point: [...] <<<

## Layer 4 — Contested
| # | Work | What it disputes | In tension with (item #) | Absorbed when you can... |
|---|------|------------------|---------------------------|--------------------------|

## Reading order (across layers)
1. [item] → 2. [item] → ...  (with logic for any interleaving)

## Cut to fit budget
- [leaf item dropped] — reason: [...]

## Uncertainty flags
- [Where I pointed to "a recent survey" rather than naming a specific work, because I won't fabricate a citation.]
```

## Verification

- [ ] All four layers are represented, or a missing layer is explained.
- [ ] The list is sequenced; a reading order with logic is given.
- [ ] Each item states the specific thing it teaches, not a generic summary.
- [ ] The contested layer includes works that disagree with the consensus, tied to what they dispute.
- [ ] Rest-points / note-prompts sit between layers.
- [ ] Each item has an absorption check.
- [ ] The list fits the time budget; cuts are from leaves and stated.
- [ ] No fabricated titles, authors, or attributions; uncertainty is flagged.
- [ ] Foundations precede frontier in the order.
