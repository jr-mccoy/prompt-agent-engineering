# Compensation Banding

**Objective:** Produce a band structure that can be published and defended: levels
defined by **scope of work** rather than years served, ranges anchored to a named market
source at a stated percentile, deliberate overlap between adjacent bands, a written rule
for placement and movement, and an audit for compression and unexplained pay gaps.
Bands without a named market source or a movement rule are refused.

**When to Use:**
- Offers are negotiated case by case and nobody can explain why two people at the same
  level are paid differently.
- You need to publish a range in a job posting and do not have one.
- Pay-transparency legislation now applies to you.
- Someone has asked "what would I need to do to earn more" and there is no answer.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need the **tax** treatment of equity or benefits — that is
  `../../domain-finance/tax-planning/finance_equity_compensation_tax_analysis.md`, which
  covers tax, not band design.
- You are setting a *contractor* rate or your own consulting rate — that is
  `../../domain-finance/corporate-finance-fpa/finance_services_rate_floor_model.md`.
- You are negotiating one specific offer — that is
  `../../domain-negotiation/contexts/negotiation_hiring_offer_employer_side.md`.
- You need performance ratings calibrated across managers — that is
  `../performance-reviews/hr_calibration_facilitator.md`, which this structure consumes
  rather than replaces.

## Context Gathering

1. **The organisation**
   - "Headcount, function mix, locations, and stage."
   - "Current pay for every role, by person, with hire date and last change." (needed
     for the compression and equity audit; anonymise if the requester should not see it)
   - "Is equity part of the package? Bonus? What proportion?"

2. **Market reference**
   - "Which market are you competing in for each function — local, national, global
     remote?"
   - "What data do you have access to: a paid survey, an industry report, offer data
     from your own loops, public postings?"
   - "What percentile do you intend to pay at, and why?"

3. **Levels today**
   - "What levels exist, and how is someone assigned to one?"
   - "Is the answer 'years of experience'? Be honest."

4. **Constraints**
   - "Total compensation budget, and what growth it must absorb."
   - "Any legal obligations: transparency, equal-pay reporting, collective agreements."

If no market data exists, say so in the output rather than inventing a range. A band
with an unsourced number is worse than no band, because it looks authoritative and
cannot be defended when challenged.

---

## Method

### Step 1 — Define levels by scope, not tenure

A level describes **what the person is accountable for**, not how long they have worked.
Tenure-based levels are unfair to fast learners, unenforceable against slow ones, and
provide no answer to "what would I need to do to move up".

For each level state: the scope of work, the degree of autonomy, the blast radius of
their decisions, and who they influence.

| Level | Scope | Autonomy | Blast radius | Influence |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Four to six levels per function is typical for a small organisation. More levels than
you have people is a structure that cannot be calibrated, and inventing eight levels
for a team of twelve produces distinctions nobody can apply.

### Step 2 — Pick the market reference and say what it is

State three things, and state them in the published artifact:

- **The comparator market** — which geography, which industry, which company size.
  Competing with well-funded remote employers for the same engineers is a different
  market from competing locally.
- **The data source** — the named survey, report, or your own offer data. Own offer
  data is legitimate and should be labelled as such, because it reflects who accepted
  rather than the market.
- **The percentile target** — 50th, 65th, 75th. And why: a 50th-percentile target with
  strong equity and unusual flexibility is coherent; a 50th-percentile target with
  nothing else is a retention problem you should state out loud.

If the only available data is public postings, that is a real source with a known bias
(it skews to the ranges companies advertise, not what they pay). Name the bias.

### Step 3 — Build ranges with deliberate overlap

Each band: minimum, midpoint, maximum. Two design decisions:

- **Range width.** Typically 30–50% from minimum to maximum, wider at senior levels
  where the scope varies more. A narrow band forces a promotion to give a raise.
- **Overlap with the adjacent band.** Deliberate, usually 10–25%. Without overlap,
  every pay increase requires a level change, which corrupts levelling into a
  compensation mechanism and makes promotion the only reward available.

State the **compa-ratio** convention you will use — pay divided by band midpoint — so
placement can be discussed in a comparable way across levels.

### Step 4 — Write the placement and movement rule

The part people actually want, and the part most structures omit.

- **Placement within a band on hire.** What puts someone at the minimum, the midpoint,
  or above it? Scope relative to the level, depth in the specific domain, and current
  market conditions are defensible. "How hard they negotiated" is the default in the
  absence of a rule, and it is the mechanism by which pay gaps appear.
- **Movement within a band.** What causes it, how often it is reviewed, and how it
  relates to the performance rating from
  `../performance-reviews/hr_calibration_facilitator.md`.
- **Movement between bands.** Promotion means the scope changed, and it is evidenced by
  the level definitions in step 1 — not by tenure and not by a good year.
- **What happens above the maximum.** Someone at the top of their band who is not ready
  for the next level: state the answer honestly. Usually a lump-sum bonus rather than
  base movement, and a real conversation about the path.

### Step 5 — Audit compression and unexplained gaps

Run four checks on current pay:

| Check | Looking for |
|---|---|
| **New-hire compression** | Recent hires paid at or above longer-tenured people at the same level. Market rates move faster than internal increases; this is the most common and most corrosive finding |
| **Manager–report inversion** | A report paid more than their manager. Sometimes legitimate for a specialist; if unexplained, it is a structural problem |
| **Unexplained gaps at the same level** | Two people, same level, same scope, materially different pay. For each, state the reason. "No stated reason" is the finding |
| **Pattern check** | Whether unexplained gaps correlate with any protected characteristic. If they do, this stops being a design exercise and becomes a legal matter — take advice before acting, and before writing it down informally |

Compression is the check that most often produces immediate work: it is what someone
discovers when a posting publishes a range above their salary, and the resulting
conversation is worse than the correction would have been.

### Step 6 — Decide what to publish, and commit

Publish the level definitions and the ranges. The level definitions are the more
valuable half — they answer "what would I need to do" — and they carry almost none of
the discomfort.

If you will not publish ranges internally, say why in one sentence you would be willing
to say out loud. That test resolves most of these decisions.

## Verification

- [ ] Levels are defined by scope; no level definition references years of experience
- [ ] The comparator market, named data source and percentile target are all stated
- [ ] Any use of own-offer data or public postings is labelled with its bias
- [ ] Bands have a stated width and a deliberate overlap with the adjacent band
- [ ] A written placement rule exists for hire, within-band and between-band movement
- [ ] The above-maximum case has an answer
- [ ] All four audit checks have been run against real current pay
- [ ] Any range without market data is flagged as unsourced rather than presented as a band
- [ ] The publication decision is made deliberately, not by default

**False-positive prevention.** The dominant failure is a band structure that
retrospectively describes current pay. Bands built by clustering what people already
earn encode every existing gap as policy and then defend it as a structure. Build from
the market reference and the level definitions, then compare current pay against it —
the differences are the audit findings, not the bands.

The second failure is tenure-based levels wearing scope language. Test each definition:
could a person satisfy it in their first year? If every level implicitly requires a
number of years, the structure cannot answer the promotion question and will not survive
a challenge.

The third failure is unsourced precision. A band of 62,000–84,000 implies data. If the
data is three public postings and a guess, say so and use a wider range: a stated
uncertainty is defensible where false precision is not.

The fourth is running the pattern check informally. If unexplained pay gaps correlate
with a protected characteristic, that is a legal matter with disclosure and privilege
implications, and an informal document analysing it can itself be discoverable. Stop and
take advice.

**Legally careful, per this domain's standing principles. This is not legal or tax
advice.** Pay-transparency duties, equal-pay obligations, equal-pay audit privilege and
reporting thresholds vary by jurisdiction and change. See
`../../domain-legal/employment-labor/` and take advice before publishing or before acting
on a pattern finding.
