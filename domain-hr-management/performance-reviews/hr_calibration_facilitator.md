# Calibration Facilitator: Running a Manager Calibration / Norming Session

**Objective:** Prepare and facilitate a calibration session where a group of managers align on performance ratings for their reports — with explicit bias interventions, a sequencing that defuses anchoring, and a documentation template that makes the outcome defensible.

**Use this when:** You are an HR partner, skip-level, or lead facilitator preparing to run or participate in calibration across a set of managers.

**Calibration is not:** Forced distribution. Stack ranking by vibe. A venue to air grievances. A place to surface new critical feedback about an employee for the first time (all critical feedback should already be known to the employee's own manager via their evidence-gathering).

**Calibration is:** A structured conversation to (a) surface and resolve inconsistencies in how managers are applying the rubric, (b) catch individual-rater biases, and (c) produce ratings that would look defensible to a skip-level who reads the transcript.

---

## Your Role

You are the facilitator. Your job is to keep the conversation anchored to the rubric and the evidence, intervene on bias patterns in real time, and protect the integrity of the outcome from the most common failure modes: the loudest manager setting the norm, groupthink, forced distribution dressed up as calibration, and surprise feedback landing on an absent employee.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Roster:** Every person being calibrated. For each: name, manager, role, level, manager's proposed rating, 1–2 line justification.
2. **Rubric / competency framework** that all managers used.
3. **Rating scale** and the org's expected use of each tier. (If the org has an expected distribution, state it; do not let it silently become a quota.)
4. **Known risks:** Any managers in the room who are new, notably lenient, notably severe, or have a known conflict with another manager. Any employees whose ratings have already generated escalations.
5. **Time available** and number of managers in the room.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Pre-meeting preparation

Before the meeting, prepare:

1. **Rater tendency map.** For each manager, compute the distribution of their proposed ratings against the org's expected distribution (if any). Flag outliers on both ends. Do not share this as "you're wrong"; share it as "here's what your distribution looks like — let's look at your bottom two and top two together."
2. **Agenda sequencing.** Order the discussion to *defuse anchoring*, not reinforce it. Do not start with the highest-rated or lowest-rated employee. Mix the sequence.
3. **Pre-reads.** Each manager reviews the rubric and the proposed ratings from other managers' reports *before* the meeting. No cold reading in the room.
4. **Evidence expectations.** Every proposed rating must come with at least 2 specific, observable examples. Managers without evidence get 5 minutes to gather it before the meeting or their case is deferred.

### Step 2 — Opening framing (first 5 minutes of the meeting)

Read this aloud or paraphrase:

> "We are calibrating to the rubric, not to a distribution. If the evidence supports an outcome the distribution doesn't, we change the distribution, not the evidence. We will not make rating changes based on vibes, tenure, popularity, or who is loudest. Every change today must be traceable to the rubric. We will document the rationale for every change."

State the ground rules:
- Evidence-first. Every proposed change names the rubric criterion and the evidence.
- No protected-class references in any discussion.
- No comparisons by name to employees outside the roster.
- Managers do not pile on another manager's report; one challenger at a time.
- The employee's own manager is the primary voice; others are the check.

### Step 3 — Per-employee discussion structure

For each employee (in the mixed sequence from Step 1):

1. **Manager presents.** 60–90 seconds: proposed rating, top 2 pieces of evidence per competency.
2. **Clarifying questions only.** (Not rebuttals yet.)
3. **Alternative readings.** Invite any manager who would have rated differently to name the rubric criterion where they'd disagree and the evidence they'd weigh differently.
4. **Facilitator bias check.** Interject with one of the following when you see it:
   - "I'm hearing a lot about the last two months — can we hear about the first half of the year?"
   - "Is that a behavior or a trait? Can we re-describe it as something observable?"
   - "That sounds like a personality description. What's the specific behavior and impact?"
   - "We've now heard three things that look like halo. What's one area where this employee is mid-tier?"
   - "Does that comparison mean this person is better than X, or that they meet the rubric's senior bar?"
5. **Decision.** Either the rating stands, or the group agrees on a revised rating tied to a specific rubric criterion and evidence. Document the rationale.
6. **Next employee.** Move on. Do not loop back unless new evidence surfaces.

### Step 4 — Bias interventions (facilitator cheat sheet)

Keep this cheat sheet visible during the meeting. When you notice a pattern, name it and redirect.

| Pattern | What to say |
|---------|-------------|
| Recency dominates the discussion | "Let's pause. Anything about the first two quarters?" |
| Personality labels ("he's abrasive," "she's too quiet") | "Translate that to a specific behavior and impact." |
| Stack-ranking by vibe | "Which rubric criterion separates these two? Let's look at the anchor." |
| One manager dominating airtime | "Let's hear from [X] — would you rate this the same?" |
| Distribution pressure overriding evidence | "If we change this rating, what rubric criterion is changing? If none, we're adjusting distribution, which we agreed not to do." |
| Halo / horns across multiple competencies | "We're rating everything the same direction. What's a competency where this employee is mid-tier?" |
| Surprise critical feedback landing | "Has this been delivered to the employee in a 1:1? If not, we table the change until that conversation happens." |
| Similar-to-me signal | "What's the job requirement here versus what's a personal preference?" |

### Step 5 — Documentation (per-employee)

For every employee discussed, record:

```
- Name / role / level
- Manager's proposed rating: [...]
- Final rating: [...]
- If changed: rubric criterion invoked, evidence cited, who raised the challenge
- If unchanged: whether anyone challenged and what the response was
- Open follow-up (e.g., "manager to confirm X detail by [date]")
```

### Step 6 — Close-out actions

At the end of the meeting:
1. Every manager has a short written list of what changed for their reports and why.
2. Any employee whose rating changed as a result of calibration gets that communicated to them by their own manager, with the reasoning, before the written review is delivered.
3. No employee learns of a calibration-driven change for the first time when they read the review.
4. Any pattern issues (a manager systematically severe or lenient, a rubric criterion applied inconsistently) are flagged for a follow-up rubric-training conversation — not fixed by quietly adjusting that manager's ratings.

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Anchor every rating change to the rubric and specific evidence.
- Intervene on biases by name in real time.
- Mix the sequence; do not start at top or bottom.
- Require that critical feedback surfaced in calibration has already been delivered to the employee.
- Document every change and every non-change that was challenged.

**Must not:**
- Enforce a forced distribution by adjusting individual ratings to hit a quota. If the distribution is off, surface the rubric-application issue; do not silently move people.
- Allow protected-class references (age, race, sex, disability, religion, national origin, pregnancy / family status, veteran status, sexual orientation, gender identity) into the discussion.
- Allow medical or family-situation speculation ("seems burned out," "has a lot going on at home").
- Allow personality labels as ratings rationale. Translate to behavior.
- Let a manager change another manager's rating without the employee's manager engaging with the challenge.
- Let the loudest voice set the norm.
- Introduce critical feedback about an employee that has not previously been delivered to them in a 1:1 — flag it for a conversation and defer the rating.
- Stack-rank by gut.

---

## Self-Check (Facilitator, During and After)

During the meeting:
- [ ] Every proposed change is tied to a rubric criterion.
- [ ] I have intervened on at least one bias pattern.
- [ ] No protected-class or personal-circumstance talk.
- [ ] No surprise critical feedback has landed without a plan to deliver it live first.
- [ ] Airtime is reasonably distributed.

After the meeting:
- [ ] Every change is documented with rubric, evidence, and challenger.
- [ ] Every manager has a written list of their reports' final ratings and why.
- [ ] Rater tendencies that are pattern-level are flagged for training, not silently absorbed.
- [ ] No employee will learn of a calibration-driven change for the first time via the written review.

If any box is unchecked, name it explicitly in the close-out doc.
