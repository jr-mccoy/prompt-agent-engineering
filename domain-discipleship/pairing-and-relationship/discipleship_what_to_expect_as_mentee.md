# What to Expect as a Mentee

**Objective:** Give the person being discipled a short, plain account they hold themselves — what a healthy
discipling relationship normally looks like, what is never owed to a mentor no matter how it is framed,
what the ordinary and the concerning versions of the same behaviour look like side by side, what to do and
say when something is off, and who to tell — written before any commitment and independent of both the
mentor and the program.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This describes a relationship; it does not assess one, and it does not diagnose
> anybody. If something has already happened that frightened you, involved a minor or a vulnerable adult,
> or crossed into anything sexual, financial, or coercive, that is not a question about expectations — tell
> someone now: a safeguarding lead if there is one, and otherwise an appropriate authority
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service or authority from an official source]`. A discipler is not a
> counselor, a therapist, or a pastor, and nothing in this relationship is a substitute for professional
> help you need.

**When to use:** Before agreeing to be discipled, in the first few weeks, or at any point later when you
find yourself wondering whether something is normal.

**When NOT to use:**
- You and a mentor are writing your mutual agreement together — use
  `discipleship_relationship_covenant.md`, which is the artifact you both sign; this one is yours alone and
  comes before it.
- You are setting up a relationship where there is no program at all — use
  `discipleship_informal_pairing_no_program.md`, which handles the structure; this handles what you
  should expect inside it.
- You were harmed in a previous discipling relationship and are deciding about a new one — use
  `../after-harm/discipleship_harmed_by_previous_discipling.md`.
- You want a plan for your own growth rather than for the relationship — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_personal_growth_plan.md`.

**Audience:** The person being discipled. A mentor or program lead may hand it over, but it is written to
be read alone and kept.

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What has been proposed to you.** Who suggested it, what they said it involves, how often, for how long,
   and whether anything was said about an ending. If nobody has said, that is worth noticing before you
   agree.
2. **What structure sits around it, if any.** A program with a lead and a policy, a church with neither, or
   two people and nothing. This determines who you would tell if something went wrong, and it is better
   answered now than later.
3. **What you want from it.** Even roughly. You are allowed to want something specific, and you are allowed
   to want to try it and see.

**Optional:**

4. **Declared tradition (optional).** Traditions differ on what authority a discipler carries, on confession,
   on submission language, and on what your participation is understood to commit you to — and if the
   community has practical expectations attached to this relationship, you should know them now rather than
   discover them later. Where none is declared, this stays general.
5. **Anything you already feel uneasy about.** Taken seriously as information, not tested.
6. **A previous experience of this**, good or bad.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask the person proposing it. A relationship whose frequency, duration,
subject matter, and ending nobody can state is not yet a relationship you have been asked to agree to — and
asking those questions is ordinary, not suspicious.

## Constraints

### Must

- Write **to the person, in the second person**, and short enough to actually read.
- Give the **ordinary and the concerning version of the same behaviour side by side**, since almost nothing
  here is wrong in itself and the difference is pattern, pressure, and whether it stops when you say so.
- State plainly the things that are **never owed**: your history, your sins, your finances, your passwords or
  accounts, access to your phone, your family's business, an explanation for leaving, or an answer to any
  question at all.
- State that **leaving requires no reason, no meeting, and no permission**, and say it as a fact about the
  relationship rather than as reassurance.
- Give **words to use** — for declining a question, for slowing down, for raising something, and for
  ending — because knowing you may is different from having something to say.
- Name **who you would tell**, worked out in advance, including the case where there is no program and the
  answer is outside the church entirely `[VERIFY]`.
- Name the things that are **never normal at any stage**: anything sexual, financial entanglement, being told
  to keep the relationship secret, being cut off from other people, being told your doubts or your
  reluctance are rebellion, or being told this is between you and them only.
- Say that **a good mentor will welcome this document**, and that a reaction against you having it is itself
  information.

### Must Not

- Assess a specific mentor, or invite the reader to build a case about one.
- Produce a checklist, score, rating, or threshold at which a relationship "counts" as unhealthy.
- Encourage suspicion as a posture, or imply that most discipling relationships go wrong — they do not, and
  a document that reads as a warning will be dismissed by the people who most need it.
- Advise on reporting, confrontation, discipline, or any action against a person — that routes to a
  safeguarding lead, an authority, or `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/`.
- Tell the reader what to conclude about anything that has already happened to them.
- Name any counselor, agency, hotline, service, or church.
- State any legal or safeguarding requirement, age, or threshold — `[VERIFY]` and route.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or use any passage about submission, honouring leaders, covering, or
  church unity to soften what is written here.
- Suggest that raising a concern requires certainty, evidence, or that you be sure first.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note that traditions genuinely differ on the authority a discipler carries, on confession and to
  whom, on whether submission language is part of the vocabulary, and on what your participation commits you
  to in practice — and say that where your community holds a particular view, you are entitled to be told
  plainly what follows from it before you agree, rather than after.
- **Must Not:** rule that any tradition's practice is wrong; and do not allow any tradition's teaching on
  authority, covering, submission, or unity to override the plain statements above about what is never owed
  and about leaving without a reason. Those do not vary by tradition.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Say what this normally is

Describe the ordinary shape in a few lines: someone a bit further on, meeting regularly, asking questions,
reading Scripture together, praying, being consistent. Most of these relationships are good and the
description should sound like the good ones, because a reader who does not recognise their relationship in
the normal picture will not trust the rest.

### Step 2 — Put the pairs side by side

For each area — questions, contact, decisions, other people, privacy, disagreement, ending — write the
ordinary version and the concerning version of the same thing. Almost nothing here is wrong in isolation;
what distinguishes them is pattern, pressure, and whether it stops when the reader says stop.

### Step 3 — List what is never owed

Plainly and without qualification. Include the ones people are most often talked out of: your full history,
your sins in detail, an explanation for declining, and an answer to any question at all.

### Step 4 — State the exits

Leaving needs no reason, no final meeting, and nobody's agreement. Say what to do practically, including the
version where the reader does not want a conversation about it.

### Step 5 — Write the words

Verbatim and short: declining a question, asking to slow down, saying something felt off, and ending it. A
sentence that has been read once is available under pressure in a way that a principle is not.

### Step 6 — Work out who you would tell, now

Name the person and the route before anything is wrong, including the case with no program where the answer
is outside the church `[VERIFY]`. Add: you do not need to be sure, and you do not need proof.

### Step 7 — Name the never-normal list and check the tone

Set out the things that are not a matter of degree at any stage. Then read the whole thing back: does it
sound like a warning about mentors, or like a description of a good relationship with its edges marked? If
the first, rewrite it — a document that makes the reader suspicious of a healthy relationship has failed as
badly as one that misses an unhealthy one.

## Output Format

Produce exactly this structure. Written to the reader. Keep it to one or two pages.

```
# What This Should Look Like

## The Ordinary Version
[Four or five lines. What a good one is like.]

## Same Thing, Two Versions
| | Ordinary | Worth paying attention to |
|---|---|---|
| Questions | [..] | [..] |
| Contact between meetings | [..] | [..] |
| Your decisions | [..] | [..] |
| Your other friendships | [..] | [..] |
| Privacy | [..] | [..] |
| Disagreeing | [..] | [..] |
| Ending it | [..] | [..] |

**What makes the difference:** whether it's a pattern, whether there's pressure, and
whether it stops when you say stop.

## Never Owed
- [..] — including an answer to any question at all, and a reason for saying no.

## Leaving
No reason. No final meeting. Nobody's permission. [How, practically — including the
version where you don't want a conversation about it.]

## Things to Say
| Moment | You can say |
|---|---|
| A question you don't want to answer | "[words]" |
| Going faster than you want | "[words]" |
| Something felt off | "[words]" |
| You want to stop | "[words]" |

## Who You'd Tell
| If something were wrong | You'd go to |
|---|---|
| [With a program] | [role] |
| [Without one] | [kind of person or body — VERIFY] |

**You don't need to be sure. You don't need proof.**

## Not a Matter of Degree
[Anything sexual · money between you · being told to keep this secret · being cut off from
other people · being told your doubts or your reluctance are rebellion · "this stays
between us and no one else."]

These don't have an ordinary version.

## One More Thing
A good mentor will be glad you have this. If someone isn't, that's worth noticing.
```

## Verification

- [ ] The document is addressed to the reader, in the second person, and fits on one or two pages.
- [ ] Ordinary and concerning versions appear side by side, with the distinguishing factor stated.
- [ ] "Never owed" and "leaving needs no reason" appear as plain statements, not as reassurances.
- [ ] Words to say are written verbatim for all four moments.
- [ ] Who to tell is worked out in advance, including the no-program case, with "you don't need proof".
- [ ] No specific mentor is assessed, and no score, checklist threshold, or rating appears anywhere.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Write it as a warning. A document that makes a person suspicious of an ordinary relationship gets
  dismissed — including by the reader who will need it in eighteen months.
- Turn it into a checklist to score a mentor against. The question is never how many boxes were ticked; it
  is whether it stopped when they said stop.
- Treat single instances as signals. A mentor who asks one probing question is a mentor asking a question;
  the pattern, and what happens when you decline, is the information.
- Require certainty before telling someone. "Be sure first" is the sentence that keeps people quiet for
  years, and it is usually offered as fairness.
- Leave the no-program case unanswered. "Speak to your leader" is useless where there is no leader, and
  that is the common case rather than the edge one.
- Add a spiritual qualifier to the never-owed list. Any sentence beginning "of course, we should be willing
  to be known…" undoes the whole section.
- Make it long. A document nobody finishes protects nobody.

✅ **DO:**
- Describe the good version first and make it recognisable, so the rest is read as edges rather than as
  accusation.
- Put the pairs side by side. It is the only format that teaches the distinction rather than asserting it.
- Say plainly what is never owed, and put "an answer to any question at all" in the list.
- Write the four sentences out. The gap between knowing you may decline and having words to decline with is
  where most of the harm happens.
- Answer "who would you tell" before anything is wrong, and include the case with nobody internal.
- Keep the never-normal list short, absolute, and free of qualifiers.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective fixes the artifact as the reader's own and
  independent of both mentor and program, which is what stops it from being written as an orientation
  handout that reassures.
- **RP-02 (Audience-Specific Framing):** everything is second person and sized for one reading, because the
  intended reader is a person deciding whether to agree to something, not a designer building a system.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block rules out the two ways this document fails —
  becoming a warning that gets dismissed, and becoming a scoring checklist that misses pattern and pressure.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the side-by-side table is the whole pedagogical move; the same
  behaviour in two columns teaches a distinction that no amount of prose conveys.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the closing check reads for tone, because a technically complete document
  in the wrong register does not get used.
