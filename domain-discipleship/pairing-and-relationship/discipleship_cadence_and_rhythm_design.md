# Cadence and Rhythm Design

**Objective:** Set the meeting pattern a discipling pair can actually keep — frequency, length, format,
and channel — designed against both people's real constraints rather than a default weekly hour, with a
named reduced pattern for hard seasons and a rule for what happens when meetings begin to slip.

> **Boundary guardrail.** A cadence is a working arrangement, not a commitment either person owes the
> other. Missed meetings are information about fit or capacity, not evidence of anyone's spiritual
> state, and this prompt must never produce a pattern that makes a missed meeting feel like a moral
> failure. Where slipping meetings reflect distress or crisis in either person, that routes out — see
> `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_boundaries_and_referral.md`.

**When to use:** At the start of a pairing, or when a pair's meetings have started slipping and the
rhythm needs rebuilding rather than the relationship ending.

**When NOT to use:**
- The mentee's whole pathway needs rebuilding around constraints — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_life_constraints_adaptation.md`.
- You are designing what happens *in* the meeting — use
  `../session-and-lesson/discipleship_session_plan_builder.md`.
- The relationship should end rather than be re-timed — use
  `discipleship_relationship_ending_or_transition.md`.
- The mentor is overloaded across all their mentees — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_support_and_sustainability.md`.

**Audience:** A mentor and mentee together; a program lead setting default expectations.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Both people's real weeks.** Work patterns, caregiving, travel, health, and which times are
   genuinely available — for both, not just the mentee.
2. **Channel constraints.** Transport, distance, private space for a video call, data and device
   access, and whether either shares a device.
3. **What has already happened.** If meetings have been slipping, what actually got in the way.
4. **The season length.** How long this pairing is set to run.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape whether meetings attach to a service, a midweek
   gathering, or a liturgical rhythm.
6. **Preference for format.** In person, video, phone, walking, over a meal, asynchronous — and whether
   either has a strong preference.
7. **Program expectations.** Any default the program sets, and whether it is a requirement or a
   suggestion.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. Ask about the
*mentor's* constraints explicitly — programs habitually design around the mentee's availability and
then discover the mentor was the limiting factor.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Design against **both people's constraints**, treating the mentor's capacity as equally real.
- Choose the frequency that **can be sustained for the whole season**, not the one that sounds
  committed. Fortnightly kept beats weekly abandoned.
- Define a **reduced pattern** for hard seasons — shorter, less frequent, or asynchronous — agreed in
  advance so it is used rather than invented under stress.
- Specify the **channel** with its constraints named: private space, transport, data, device sharing,
  and safety.
- Set a **slipping rule**: what happens after a stated number of missed or rearranged meetings, who
  raises it, and what the conversation is.
- Name **who initiates** and **who rearranges**, since ambiguity here is the most common cause of drift.
- Include an **asynchronous layer** where it helps — messages between meetings — with its own limits so
  it does not become an always-on expectation on the mentor.
- State what the cadence **is not**: a measure of commitment, and not a promise.

### Must Not

- Default to weekly because it is conventional, without testing it against both stated weeks.
- Set a pattern requiring transport, childcare, private space, or data either person lacks.
- Frame a missed meeting as unfaithfulness, or build in any language of accountability for attendance.
- Create an asynchronous expectation with no boundary — a mentor reachable at all hours will burn out
  and a mentee will learn dependence.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, and do not use passages about meeting together to pressure
  attendance.
- Invent research on meeting frequency or relationship-building cadence.
- Assume the mentor has unlimited capacity because they volunteered.
- Design a rhythm that only works if nothing goes wrong for the whole season.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a tradition ties discipleship rhythm to a service, a fast, a liturgical season, or a
  set gathering, incorporate it as that stream's rhythm and note others are free-standing.
- **Must Not:** treat attaching to a Sunday service as the natural default, or imply that a pair meeting
  outside church structures is less serious.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Map both weeks

Lay out both people's genuinely available windows. Mark the ones that are reliable versus the ones that
merely exist. A window that survives a normal disruption is the only kind worth building on.

### Step 2 — Test the channel options

For each of in person, video, phone, walking, shared meal, and asynchronous, note what it requires and
whether both have it. Private space for a video call is the constraint most often missed — many people
do not have a room they can talk privately in.

### Step 3 — Pick the sustainable frequency

Choose the frequency both can hold across the whole season including its hard weeks. State plainly that
a lower frequency kept is worth more than a higher one abandoned, so neither person reads the choice as
low commitment.

### Step 4 — Design the reduced pattern

Agree now what happens in a hard season: a twenty-minute call instead of an hour, a message exchange
instead of a meeting, a fortnight skipped by agreement. Name who can invoke it — both — and that
invoking it needs no justification.

### Step 5 — Set the asynchronous layer and its limits

Decide what happens between meetings, if anything. Set the boundary explicitly: what the mentor
responds to, roughly when, and what waits for the meeting. Protects both people.

### Step 6 — Write the slipping rule

State the threshold — for example two consecutive rearrangements — and what happens: who raises it,
what the conversation asks (is this the wrong rhythm, the wrong season, or the wrong pairing), and that
it is a design question rather than an accusation.

### Step 7 — Assign initiation and check

Say who books, who rearranges, and how. Then check the design against the four failures: **built on
unreliable windows**, **channel requires something absent**, **no reduced pattern**, and **no slipping
rule**.

## Verification

- [ ] Both people's constraints are mapped; the mentor's capacity is treated as equally real.
- [ ] The chosen frequency is justified against hard weeks, not good ones.
- [ ] A reduced pattern exists and either party may invoke it without justification.
- [ ] The channel choice names private space, transport, data, and device sharing explicitly.
- [ ] A slipping rule exists with a threshold and is framed as a design question.
- [ ] Nothing frames attendance as faithfulness or a missed meeting as failure.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Default to weekly. It is conventional, not correct, and it is the single most common reason pairs
  stop meeting by month three.
- Assume a video call is the low-friction option. It requires a private room, and many people —
  house-sharers, parents of small children, people in unsafe homes — do not have one.
- Design only around the mentee's availability. Mentors are usually the scarcer resource and the
  quieter about it.
- Leave the asynchronous layer undefined. Undefined means always-on, which ends in mentor burnout and
  mentee dependence.
- Treat rearrangement as a commitment problem. Two rearrangements usually mean the rhythm was wrong,
  which is fixable.
- Build a rhythm that only works if the season goes smoothly. It will not.

✅ **DO:**
- Distinguish reliable windows from windows that merely exist. Only the first survives a sick child or
  a late shift.
- Say out loud that fortnightly-kept beats weekly-abandoned, so the lower frequency is not heard as
  lower commitment.
- Agree the reduced pattern before it is needed. Invented under stress, it becomes a cancellation
  instead of an adaptation.
- Bound the between-meetings layer explicitly, including roughly when the mentor replies.
- Set the slipping threshold numerically and name who raises it, so the conversation happens rather
  than the pairing quietly fading.
- Ask about private space directly rather than assuming a video call solves distance.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** both weeks are mapped and channels tested before a
  frequency is chosen, so the rhythm is derived from constraints rather than from convention.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** frequency, length, channel, and asynchronous layer
  are decided separately against reliability, access, privacy, and capacity — dimensions that a single
  "how often shall we meet" question collapses.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the no-weekly-default rule, the mandatory reduced pattern, and
  the bounded asynchronous layer are hard constraints against the three ways pair rhythms reliably fail.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the channel table forces each option to declare its
  requirements against both people, which is how the missing-private-space problem becomes visible.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the four-check design review confirms the rhythm rests on reliable
  windows and that a slipping rule exists before the pattern is adopted.
