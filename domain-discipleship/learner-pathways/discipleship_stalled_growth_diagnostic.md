# Stalled Growth Diagnostic

**Objective:** Work out why a believer's growth has stopped — distinguishing plateau from exhaustion
from isolation from unresolved doubt from circumstance from unaddressed harm from clinical distress —
and route each cause to the response that actually fits it, rather than prescribing more discipline to
all seven.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Several causes of a stall are not formation problems at all. Depression,
> anxiety, trauma, grief, addiction, chronic illness, and the aftermath of abuse present exactly as
> "I've stopped growing," and they do not respond to spiritual effort. Where any of these is in view,
> this prompt routes to a licensed professional, a trusted pastor, or emergency services
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]` and to `domain-psychology/` — and it
> says so early, not as a closing caveat.

**When to use:** Growth has stopped, practices keep collapsing, or faith feels flat and dry, and the
usual advice — try harder, read more, pray more — has not worked.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are looking for a general picture, not a stall — use `discipleship_growth_self_assessment.md`.
- You already know the cause is fit between practices and your week — use
  `discipleship_spiritual_practices_designer.md`.
- You have been away from faith or church for a long period and want to return — use
  `discipleship_returning_believer_reengagement.md`.
- The stall is specifically unresolved intellectual doubt — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/learner-self-study/biblical_learner_honest_questions_doubt_explorer.md`.

**Audience:** The individual believer, or a mentor working through it with them.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What "stalled" means to you.** What has changed, and compared to when.
2. **How long.** Weeks, months, or years — and whether it arrived suddenly or gradually. Sudden and
   gradual point to different causes.
3. **What is still happening.** What you are still doing, even minimally, and what has stopped.
4. **What else changed around the same time.** Job, health, move, loss, relationship, church, family,
   sleep, finances. This is the highest-yield question in the whole diagnostic.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape how a dry season is understood — dark night, wilderness,
   ordinary means, spiritual warfare, backsliding — and which framings are pastorally available.
6. **What you have already tried.** And what happened.
7. **Who knows.** Whether anyone is aware of this.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding — always ask what else
changed at the same time. A stall that began the month a parent died is not a discipline problem, and
without that question it will be treated as one.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Consider **all seven causes** explicitly before concluding anything, and say which were ruled out and
  why.
- Route **clinical, safety, and grief causes out first**, before any formation response is discussed.
- Distinguish **sudden** from **gradual** onset and use it as diagnostic evidence.
- Treat **plateau after real growth** as a normal and expected phase, not a failure — many stalls are
  consolidation.
- Name the **most likely cause and the most likely alternative**, with the evidence for each and what
  would distinguish them.
- Give a response matched to the cause — and where the cause is not formation, say plainly that no
  amount of spiritual effort will address it.
- State what this diagnostic **cannot determine**.

### Must Not

- Default to insufficient discipline, weak commitment, or unconfessed sin as the explanation.
- Diagnose a clinical condition or use clinical labels as descriptions of the person. Describe what was
  said and route it.
- Treat a dry season as evidence about the person's salvation, sincerity, or standing with God.
- Prescribe more practices, more reading, or more accountability as a general answer to a stall.
- Quote Scripture text from memory; addresses only, and only where genuinely apt.
- Invent research, statistics, or stage models of spiritual dryness.
- Name a specific counselor, therapist, service, or hotline — always `[VERIFY]`.
- Attach a score, level, or severity rating to the stall.
- Frame the response with guilt, urgency, or shame.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a tradition has a specific framework for dry seasons — the dark night of the soul,
  wilderness periods, the ordinary means of grace, spiritual warfare, seasons of consolation and
  desolation — name it as that stream's framing and note that others read the same experience
  differently.
- **Must Not:** apply one tradition's interpretation of dryness as the universal explanation, or treat
  a stream's framing of the experience as diagnosis.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Safety and clinical screen first

Before anything else, check what was described against: grief and loss, depression, anxiety, trauma,
addiction, chronic illness or pain, exhaustion past ordinary tiredness, an unsafe home, and thoughts of
self-harm. If any is plausibly present, say so warmly and plainly, route it, and make clear that the
rest of this diagnostic is secondary to that. Do not bury this at the end.

### Step 2 — Establish the shape of the stall

Fix the timeline: when it started, sudden or gradual, and what coincided. A sudden stall usually has an
event behind it. A gradual one usually has a drift, a depletion, or an unexamined question.

### Step 3 — Walk the seven causes

Consider each in turn, with the evidence for and against:

| Cause | What it typically looks like |
|---|---|
| **Plateau after growth** | Practices continue, nothing feels new, no distress — consolidation |
| **Exhaustion / depletion** | Wants to, cannot; everything costs more than it did; often a life-load change |
| **Isolation** | Faith has become private; no one knows; drift accelerated after leaving a community |
| **Unresolved doubt** | An unexamined question quietly making practice feel dishonest |
| **Circumstance** | Time, privacy, health, transport, or safety genuinely removed the conditions |
| **Unaddressed harm** | Church hurt, betrayal, spiritual abuse, or a leader's failure making practice feel unsafe |
| **Clinical distress** | Anhedonia, hopelessness, or dysfunction well beyond the spiritual domain |

### Step 4 — Name the likeliest cause and the runner-up

State the most likely cause with its evidence, then the strongest alternative with its evidence, and
say what would distinguish them — a question to sit with, an observation to make, or a conversation to
have.

### Step 5 — Match the response to the cause

Give the response that fits. Say explicitly where more spiritual effort is the wrong prescription:

- Plateau → normalize, adjust depth or form, wait well
- Exhaustion → reduce load, restore rest, shrink to the floor, seek help for the load
- Isolation → one person, not a programme
- Doubt → examine the question directly, route to the doubt explorer; where a mentor is walking with
  them, route the mentor to
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_posture_in_doubt_and_deconstruction.md`, since the mentor's
  own fear is usually what turns the conversation into an argument
- Circumstance → adapt the pathway, route to the constraints prompt
- Unaddressed harm → acknowledge, do not rush, route to professional support, and where the harm was in
  a previous discipling relationship and they are considering another, to
  `../after-harm/discipleship_harmed_by_previous_discipling.md`
- Clinical → professional help first; formation resumes afterward, not instead

### Step 6 — State the limits and the next step

Say what this cannot determine. Give one concrete next step matched to the likeliest cause, small
enough to do this week.

## Verification

- [ ] The safety and clinical screen runs first and appears first in the output.
- [ ] All seven causes are considered with evidence for and against, not just the likeliest.
- [ ] A runner-up cause is named with a distinguishing test.
- [ ] Where the cause is not formation, the output says plainly that spiritual effort will not fix it.
- [ ] No clinical diagnosis, no named service, no severity score.
- [ ] Plateau is treated as normal, not as failure.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Reach for unconfessed sin as the explanation. It is the traditional first guess and it is usually
  wrong, and it does real harm to someone who is exhausted or grieving.
- Read exhaustion as apathy. The person who "does not feel like it" has often been running on empty
  for a year.
- Treat every dry season as a dark night of the soul. That is one tradition's frame for a specific
  experience, not a universal explanation.
- Prescribe a new practice to someone who has told you they cannot sustain the ones they had.
- Skip the coincidence question because the person did not mention anything. People routinely fail to
  connect a bereavement, a diagnosis, or a job loss to their spiritual life.
- Handle church hurt briskly, or move to reconciliation before acknowledgement. That compounds the harm.

✅ **DO:**
- Put the clinical and safety screen first and say it plainly. Someone in depression who is told to
  pray more receives one more piece of evidence that they are failing.
- Use onset shape as evidence — sudden points to event, gradual points to drift or depletion.
- Name plateau as normal and expected when the evidence fits. It relieves a burden that should never
  have been carried.
- Give the runner-up cause and a way to tell them apart, so the person can keep thinking after the
  conversation ends.
- Say explicitly what will not help. "More discipline will not fix this" is often the most useful
  sentence in the output.
- Offer one small step, not a programme. Someone stalled cannot start a programme.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the clinical and safety screen is Step 1 by design,
  so the diagnostic cannot reach a formation conclusion while an untreated condition is in view.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** seven differentiated causes, each with its own
  presentation and its own response, replace the single-axis effort explanation that this genre
  defaults to.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block bans the traditional wrong answers —
  insufficient discipline, unconfessed sin, weak commitment — as default explanations, which is the
  prompt's central corrective.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** a named runner-up cause with a distinguishing test, plus the
  explicit "what this cannot tell you," keep a plausible diagnosis from hardening into a verdict.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the verification block confirms all seven causes were weighed with
  evidence both ways before the likeliest was named.
