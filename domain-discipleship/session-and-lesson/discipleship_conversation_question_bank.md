# Conversation Question Bank

**Objective:** Build a mentor's working bank of questions for a discipling relationship — organized by
depth tier and by topic, each tier carrying an explicit line past which a question becomes intrusive,
and governed by the rule that depth is earned by time and reciprocity rather than granted by the role.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Better questions produce deeper disclosure, and deeper disclosure raises the
> chance of surfacing something the mentor must route rather than hold — see
> `discipleship_hard_conversation_navigation.md`. A question bank is not a diagnostic instrument, and
> depth of answer is not a measure of the relationship's health. Questions that probe trauma, mental
> health, sexual history, or family harm belong to professionals, not to this bank.

**When to use:** A mentor wants better questions, keeps asking the same three, or has asked something
that landed badly and wants to understand why.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want the meeting's structure — use `discipleship_session_plan_builder.md`.
- The problem is how the mentor listens, not what they ask — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_conversation_skills.md`.
- It is the first meeting — use `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_first_meeting_guide.md`.
- You want discussion questions for a Bible-study group — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/study-methods-teaching/biblical_smallgroup_discussion_guide.md`.

**Audience:** The mentor; program leads producing a shared bank for all pairs.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **How long the relationship has run.** Depth tiers are gated by elapsed time and demonstrated
   reciprocity, not by the mentor's readiness to ask.
2. **What the pair is working on.** The topics the bank needs to cover.
3. **What has already been asked.** Especially anything that landed badly, and how.
4. **The mentee's disposition.** Talkative, reticent, deflecting, over-agreeable — because
   over-agreeable mentees answer intrusive questions rather than declining them, and the bank must
   protect them.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape which topics are customary — confession, giving,
   assurance, spiritual gifts, sacramental practice — and which vocabulary is familiar.
6. **The mentor's own tendency.** Whether they under-ask or over-ask.
7. **Cultural context.** What is ordinary and what is intrusive varies widely, and the bank should ask
   rather than assume.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. Ask what landed badly
— a question that misfired reveals more about the pair's actual boundaries than any amount of
description.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Organize questions into **three depth tiers**, and state what earns access to each.
- Give each tier an explicit **too-far line** — the specific kinds of question that sit just past it and
  why.
- Write questions that are **answerable**: concrete, singular, and not requiring the mentee to have
  already understood themselves.
- Include the **decline line** the mentor offers alongside deeper questions, and the rule that a
  declined question is never returned to in the same conversation.
- Include **questions the mentee can ask the mentor**, since a one-directional bank builds an
  interrogation.
- Cover **reciprocity**: the mentor answers at the depth they ask, first.
- Include a set of **low-stakes questions** for a reticent mentee or a depleted week.
- Note which questions are **likely to surface something that routes out**, so the mentor asks them
  ready.

### Must Not

- Include questions probing trauma, sexual history, mental-health symptoms, family violence, or
  substance use. Those belong to professionals.
- Write questions containing their own answer, or with a single acceptable response.
- Frame declining as evasion, or build in a follow-up that presses a declined question.
- Treat depth of disclosure as progress, or the arrival at tier three as an achievement.
- Quote Scripture text from memory; where a question is anchored in a passage, give the address.
- Invent question frameworks attributed to a named ministry, or cite research on question design.
- Assume topics that are ordinary in one culture are ordinary in another — money, family, marriage,
  and personal failure vary widely.
- Produce so many questions that the bank becomes a script.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a topic is handled distinctively by a stream — confession, assurance of salvation,
  tithing, spiritual gifts, sacramental practice — note it and phrase questions so they work across
  traditions or are marked as stream-specific.
- **Must Not:** assume a tradition's accountability conventions are shared, or import a stream's
  characteristic questions about sin, giving, or experience as though they were universal.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Set the tiers and what earns them

| Tier | What it covers | Earned by |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Open** | Life, week, what they are enjoying, what is hard in ordinary terms | Available from the first meeting |
| **2 — Considered** | What they are learning, wrestling with, hoping for, avoiding | Time, plus the mentor having answered at this depth first |
| **3 — Close** | Fears, failures, questions about God they have not said aloud | Sustained reciprocity and the mentee having led there first |

State plainly: tier three is entered when the mentee opens it, not when the mentor decides it is time.

### Step 2 — Write the tier-one bank

Ten to fifteen questions across the pair's topics. Concrete, singular, easy to answer honestly on a bad
day.

### Step 3 — Write the tier-two bank

Eight to twelve. These invite reflection. Each should be one the mentor has already answered about
themselves, or would.

### Step 4 — Write the tier-three bank, small

Four to six only. These are asked rarely. Each carries the decline line and a note on whether it is
likely to surface something that routes out.

### Step 5 — Draw the too-far lines

For each tier, name the questions that sit just past it and why they are intrusive rather than deep.
This is the most useful part of the bank — it is what prevents an enthusiastic mentor doing harm with
good intentions.

### Step 6 — Write the reverse bank and the decline line

Questions the mentee can ask the mentor. Then the exact decline line the mentor offers with tier-two
and tier-three questions, and the rule: a declined question is not returned to today.

### Step 7 — Add the low-stakes set

Five or six questions for a reticent mentee or a week where nobody has capacity. These are not lesser
questions; they are the ones that keep a relationship alive through a flat season.

## Verification

- [ ] Three tiers with stated earning conditions; tier three is gated on the mentee leading there.
- [ ] Each tier has an explicit too-far line with reasons.
- [ ] No question probes trauma, sexual history, mental-health symptoms, family violence, or substance use.
- [ ] The decline line is written verbatim, with the no-return-today rule stated.
- [ ] A reverse bank exists for the mentee to ask the mentor.
- [ ] A low-stakes set exists and is framed as valuable, not lesser.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Treat reaching tier three as progress. Some good relationships stay at tier two for a year, and
  pushing deeper is how mentors mistake their own curiosity for care.
- Write questions requiring the mentee to have already understood themselves. "Why do you self-sabotage"
  is not a question, it is a diagnosis with a question mark.
- Include accountability questions about sin, giving, or purity as standard. They are stream-specific,
  frequently intrusive, and easily coercive with an over-agreeable mentee.
- Return to a declined question later in the same conversation. Rephrasing it is still asking it, and
  it teaches that declining does not work.
- Build a bank so large it becomes a script. A mentor reading questions is not in a conversation.
- Assume money, family, or personal failure are ordinary topics. What is unremarkable in one culture is
  invasive in another.

✅ **DO:**
- Gate tier three on the mentee having led there, not on elapsed time alone. Time earns tier two;
  invitation earns tier three.
- Write the too-far lines with reasons. A mentor who understands *why* a question is intrusive can
  improvise safely; one who memorized a list cannot.
- Require the mentor to have answered a tier-two question about themselves before asking it.
- Give the mentee questions to ask back, so the relationship is not structurally one-directional.
- Say the decline line aloud when asking, rather than relying on the mentee to know they may decline —
  an over-agreeable mentee will answer anything.
- Keep the low-stakes set and treat it as first-class. It carries the relationship through the weeks
  that would otherwise end it.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** tiers and their earning conditions are set before any
  question is written, so depth is governed by a stated rule rather than by the mentor's sense of
  readiness.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** depth tier crossed with topic, reciprocity, and
  routing likelihood produces a bank that a flat topic-only list cannot — particularly the
  which-questions-may-surface-something dimension.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the too-far-line table pairs each tier with what sits just
  past it, which is the part of the bank that actually prevents harm.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the exclusion of trauma, sexual history, mental-health, family
  violence, and substance-use questions is a hard boundary, as is the no-return-to-a-declined-question
  rule.
- **RP-02 (Audience-Specific Framing):** the reverse bank and the verbatim decline line address the
  power asymmetry directly, since an over-agreeable mentee will answer whatever they are asked.
