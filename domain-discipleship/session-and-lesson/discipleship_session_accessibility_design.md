# Session Accessibility Design

**Objective:** Rebuild a discipleship session or module so that it works for disabled and neurodivergent
participants as its ordinary shape — reducing sensory and social demand, replacing the moves that exclude
without adding anything, giving processing time and alternative routes as standing options everyone gets,
and stating what the design still asks of people so that anyone can decide for themselves whether it will
work.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This prompt **never assesses whether anyone is disabled, neurodivergent, or
> anything else.** It does not screen, identify, suspect, diagnose, or record, and it does not ask a
> participant to disclose a condition in order to receive a design that should have been there anyway.
> Diagnosis and clinical support belong to licensed professionals
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. A mentor is not a therapist, a
> support worker, a special-education professional, or an interpreter of anyone's behaviour, and a
> session redesigned for accessibility does not make them one.

**When to use:** When designing a session, a module, or a small-group format — ideally before anyone has
asked for anything, and specifically when the standard shape assumes sustained eye contact, spontaneous
open sharing, reading aloud, or a room nobody can leave.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are rebuilding one person's whole pathway around their actual life — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_life_constraints_adaptation.md`, which runs the twelve-axis audit with
  and for a named person; this designs the session before anyone in particular is in it.
- The constraint is literacy, reading preference, or an oral learning culture — use
  `../cross-cultural/discipleship_oral_preference_learners.md`, which builds a full oral pathway rather
  than adapting a written one.
- You are building the session itself for the first time — use `discipleship_session_plan_builder.md`, and
  run this over the result.
- You are evaluating published material's accessibility — use
  `../curriculum-architecture/discipleship_material_evaluation.md`.
- You need physical access to a building, transport, or an interpreter — that is real and it is not a
  session design question; it goes to whoever runs the venue `[VERIFY]`.

**Audience:** Session and module designers, mentors preparing to meet, and small-group leaders.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The session or module as it currently stands.** The actual plan, including what is read, what is asked,
   how long it runs, and what happens in the room.
2. **The setting.** Room, café, video call, someone's home; noise, lighting, seating, whether leaving is
   easy and unremarked, whether anyone else is present.
3. **The format's social demands.** What a participant is expected to do in front of others: read aloud,
   pray aloud, answer an open question with no warning, disclose something, make eye contact, stay for a
   fixed period.
4. **The written and spoken load.** How much reading, at what notice, and how much of the session depends
   on holding a spoken exchange in real time.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May involve fixed liturgical forms, sung or spoken responses,
   extemporaneous prayer aloud, physical postures, or a set order — some of which are load-bearing to the
   tradition and are not the designer's to remove. Where a form is fixed, the design works around it
   (position, exit, advance notice, an alternative way to participate) rather than altering it. Where none
   is declared, the design stays form-general.
6. **What has already been asked for**, by anyone, at any time — treated as design information about the
   session, never as information about a person.
7. **What the designer can actually change**, given a venue they may not control.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask about the session and the setting. Do not ask who is coming and
what they have — that is not an input to this prompt, and requesting it converts a design task into a
screening exercise.

## Constraints

### Must

- Design **defaults offered to everyone**, never accommodations arranged for a person — because an option
  that has to be requested is one that has to be disclosed for, and most people will not.
- Work the **six axes** explicitly: sensory load, social demand, structural predictability, processing time,
  written-versus-spoken load, and the ability to leave or pause.
- Give **advance notice as standard** — what will be read, what will be asked, and what the session will
  ask people to do — since unpredictability is a larger barrier than difficulty for many participants.
- Replace **unstructured open questions** with a form that has a way in: named in advance, answerable in
  writing, answerable later, or genuinely passable without comment.
- Make **passing, silence, and leaving unremarkable** — stated once at the start as ordinary for everyone,
  never noticed aloud when used.
- Provide **more than one route to the same thing**, so that participating is not identical to speaking in
  the moment.
- State **what the session still demands** after redesign, plainly, so anyone can decide for themselves
  whether it will work rather than having to try it and find out.
- Where a **form is fixed by tradition**, design around it — seating, exit, advance notice, an alternative
  way to participate — rather than removing it.
- Keep the **formation intact**: the redesigned session must still do what it was for, and where a change
  would cost the substance, say so and find another route.

### Must Not

- Assess, screen for, suspect, infer, record, or discuss whether any participant is disabled or
  neurodivergent, or interpret anyone's behaviour as evidence of a condition.
- Require disclosure of a condition, diagnosis, or need in order to receive any option in the design.
- Name conditions in the session material, or design a version "for autistic participants" that is
  visibly separate from the ordinary one.
- Suggest that a mentor provide therapeutic, clinical, behavioural, or educational support, or use any
  clinical technique.
- Publicly acknowledge, praise, accommodate, or draw attention to any participant's use of an option.
- Reduce the session to the point where it no longer forms anyone, and do not treat lower demand as
  automatically better.
- Frame accessibility as pastoral kindness extended to some people rather than as how the session is built.
- Name any service, agency, therapist, or assessment provider; state any legal accessibility, equality, or
  disability requirement — `[VERIFY]` and route to `domain-legal/`.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or use any passage about weakness, suffering, healing, or bearing with
  one another in connection with a participant's disability.
- Produce any score, rating, or accessibility level for the session or for anyone in it.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on fixed liturgical forms, spoken or sung participation, praying
  aloud extemporaneously, physical postures, silence, and the expected order of a gathering — and where a
  form is load-bearing to the tradition, design the surroundings rather than the form, so that
  participation remains possible without the practice being altered.
- **Must Not:** rule that any tradition's form is inaccessible and should be dropped, or let a tradition's
  expectation of spoken participation or a particular posture be treated as compulsory for an individual
  when a non-conspicuous alternative is available.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Map the session's demands, not its participants

Walk the session in order and write what it asks of a person at each point: to read, to speak
spontaneously, to be looked at, to sit still, to stay, to disclose, to process at the pace of a
conversation. This map is the object of the redesign. Nothing here asks who is coming.

### Step 2 — Reduce the sensory and physical load

Address what the designer controls: seating and where a person can sit without being central, lighting and
noise, whether a door is reachable without crossing the room, background music, phone and screen use, and
the length of the block before a break. Where the venue is not the designer's, list what to ask for and
what to do if the answer is no.

### Step 3 — Lower the social demand without lowering the substance

Take each moment that requires performance in front of others and give it an alternative route that is not
visibly the lesser one: written before spoken, prepared before spontaneous, one-to-one before group,
answering later before answering now. The test is whether someone using the alternative is doing the same
thing rather than a reduced version of it.

### Step 4 — Make the session predictable in advance

Write what participants receive before the meeting: what will be read, what will be asked, what they will
be invited to do, how long it runs, and what happens if they arrive late or leave early. Predictability
removes more barriers than simplification does, and it costs one message.

### Step 5 — Build in processing time and give silence a shape

Put deliberate pauses where questions land, say how long they are, and give silence a stated purpose so it
is not experienced as pressure. Where an answer is wanted, offer the option of bringing it next time as
ordinary rather than as a concession.

### Step 6 — Make passing and leaving unremarkable

Write the one sentence said at the start that establishes passing, silence, and stepping out as ordinary
for everyone. Then write the rule for the leader: when someone uses it, nothing is said, then or later.
An accommodation that is noticed aloud has been withdrawn.

### Step 7 — State the residual demand, then check

Say plainly what the session still requires — that people are in a room with others for an hour, that
there is a conversation, that a mentor will ask questions — so anyone can decide in advance. Then check:
does any option require disclosure to access? Would someone using an alternative be visible as doing so?
Does the session still form anyone? Is there any place where the design asks a mentor to interpret a
participant's behaviour?

## Verification

- [ ] Every option is a default offered to everyone, and none requires disclosure to access.
- [ ] All six axes are addressed with a specific change.
- [ ] Advance notice of content, questions, and length is part of the standard design.
- [ ] Passing, silence, and leaving are established once and never remarked on afterwards.
- [ ] The session still does what it was for, with any substance cost named rather than absorbed.
- [ ] Nothing in the output assesses, screens for, names, or infers any participant's condition.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Build accommodations to be requested. An option that has to be asked for has to be disclosed for, and the
  people who most need it are the least likely to ask a group leader in front of a room.
- Design a separate version "for neurodivergent participants". A visibly different route is a label applied
  in public, and it will not be used.
- Guess who needs it. Deciding that someone is probably autistic and adjusting for them is an assessment,
  it is not the mentor's to make, and it is frequently wrong.
- Praise someone for participating, or thank them for speaking up. It marks the difficulty publicly and
  confirms that the room was watching.
- Strip the session to nothing. Silence, honesty, and being known are demanding by design, and a session
  with no demand forms nobody — the goal is removing the incidental barriers, not the substance.
- Drop a tradition's fixed form because it is hard. The seating, the notice, and the exit are the
  designer's; the liturgy usually is not.
- Leave the residual demand unstated. Someone deciding whether they can manage an hour in a room needs to
  know that it is an hour in a room, in advance.

✅ **DO:**
- Map the session's demands rather than the people, and keep every question about the design.
- Send what is coming beforehand. It is the cheapest change here and the one that removes most barriers.
- Give every performance moment a second route that is doing the same thing, not a reduced version of it.
- Say once at the start that passing and leaving are ordinary, then never mention it again when used.
- Design around fixed forms — position, notice, exit, an alternative way in — rather than altering them.
- State plainly what the session still asks, so people can decide in advance instead of by trying it.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the session's demands are mapped before anything is
  changed, which keeps the redesign anchored to the format rather than drifting toward the participants.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** six axes — sensory, social, predictability, processing,
  modality, exit — are worked separately, so a quiet room does not count as having addressed a session full
  of unstructured open questions.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the prohibition the whole prompt rests
  on — no assessment of anyone, and no option that requires disclosure to reach.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the two-routes table pairs each spoken demand with an
  equivalent alternative on the same row, which is what stops the alternative from becoming a lesser
  version of participation.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the final check tests the two failures that survive good intentions — an
  option gated behind a request, and a design that has quietly stopped forming anyone.
