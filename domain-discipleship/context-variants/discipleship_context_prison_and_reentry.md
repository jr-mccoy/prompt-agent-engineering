# Prison and Re-Entry Program Variant

**Objective:** Design a discipleship program for people in custody and people leaving it — built on the
facility's rules rather than around them, honest about a power asymmetry that no goodwill removes,
disciplined about promising nothing that cannot be delivered, and planned across the release cliff
before the first session inside is run.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs a program, not legal, immigration, housing, or clinical support.
> Appeals, sentencing, parole, licence conditions, immigration status, and any legal question route to
> qualified counsel and, where available, prison legal services `[VERIFY: identify the correct service
> from an official source]`. Housing, benefits, employment, substance-use treatment, and mental-health
> care route to specialist agencies. Nothing here states any facility rule, correctional policy, or law
> — every operational rule comes from the facility itself and the chaplaincy, and where this prompt and
> the facility differ, **the facility governs.** Safeguarding applies fully, including to the volunteer
> and to anyone the participant may have harmed.

**When to use:** You are standing up, rebuilding, or extending a discipleship program in a custodial
setting, a re-entry setting, or across both — and you already have or are seeking authorized access.

**When NOT to use:**
- You have no authorized access. That comes first, from the facility and its chaplaincy, and no design
  substitutes for it.
- You need the generic program design — use
  `../program-operations/discipleship_program_design_blueprint.md`, which this variant extends.
- You need the safeguarding and conduct policy — use
  `../program-operations/discipleship_safeguarding_and_conduct_policy.md`, which this variant is
  subordinate to.
- The need is advocacy, legal support, or resettlement services. Those are different work, done by
  people qualified to do it.

**Audience:** Program leads and chaplaincy-linked coordinators with authorized access. Not a design for
an unsupervised volunteer group.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The facility's rules as the facility states them.** Access, clearance, what may be brought in,
   what may be discussed, contact rules, correspondence rules, and what ends a volunteer's access.
   Supplied by the facility — never assumed, never reconstructed from memory.
2. **The chaplaincy relationship.** Who authorizes, who supervises, who the program reports to, and how
   a concern is escalated.
3. **Which population.** Remand or sentenced, category or security level, sentence lengths, young adults
   or adults, women's or men's estate, and whether participants are approaching release.
4. **What happens at release.** Whether the program follows people out, what the receiving side is, and
   what the actual gap looks like — because the days after release are where programs most often fail
   the people they formed.
5. **The referral landscape on both sides.** Inside and outside, described rather than named from memory.

**Optional:**

6. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground confession, absolution, sacramental practice,
   baptism inside, or particular chaplaincy patterns. Facility rules govern what is possible regardless.
7. **Volunteer pool and their background.** Including whether any volunteer has lived experience of
   custody, which is an asset and carries its own clearance considerations.
8. **Existing material approved for use inside.**

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. If item 1 cannot be supplied, stop: a
program designed against assumed rules will either be refused at the gate or, worse, run until a
volunteer breaks a rule nobody wrote down.

## Constraints

### Must

- Treat the **facility's rules as governing**, restate them in the program document as the facility
  states them, and design entirely inside them.
- Name the **power asymmetry** explicitly: the volunteer leaves and the participant does not; the
  volunteer's report can affect a participant's standing; participation may be perceived as affecting
  parole or privileges. Design the guards that follow.
- State, in participants' hands, that **participation carries no institutional benefit** the program
  can promise, and that the program will not write anything to a board or court unless the facility's
  own process provides for it and the participant asks.
- **Promise nothing that cannot be delivered** — no housing, money, employment, transport, contact after
  release, or ongoing relationship — and record every commitment made, with who is accountable for it.
- Plan the **release cliff** before the inside sessions: what exists on day one, week one, and month
  three; who makes contact; what happens if the receiving side falls through.
- Design **correspondence and contact rules** that match the facility's, including what a volunteer does
  with a letter, a request, or an approach from a participant's family.
- Hold **safeguarding fully**, including the possibility that a participant has harmed others, that a
  volunteer may be manipulated, and that some pairings must not be made.
- Take the **victim's existence seriously**: the program does not adjudicate guilt, does not require
  disclosure of an offence, does not treat remorse as a curriculum outcome, and does not facilitate
  contact with anyone harmed.
- Design for **interruption**: transfers, lockdowns, hearings, and release all end participation without
  notice, so every unit must stand alone.
- Route legal, immigration, housing, benefits, substance-use, and mental-health matters **out**
  `[VERIFY]`.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- State any facility rule, correctional policy, sentencing, parole, or licence condition as fact.
- Offer, imply, or allow a participant to infer that faith participation improves their legal or
  institutional position.
- Ask a participant to disclose their offence, or design any exercise in which disclosure is the point.
- Facilitate, encourage, or carry any contact between a participant and a person they harmed, or that
  person's family.
- Accept or handle money, hold property, carry messages, or contact anyone on a participant's behalf
  outside the facility's own channels.
- Invent recidivism statistics, prison-ministry outcome data, or research on faith and reoffending.
- Give clinical guidance on substance use, trauma, or mental illness. All route out.
- Create a dependency in which the volunteer is the participant's only support after release.
- Name a specific facility, agency, service, or organization.
- Publish, share, or use a participant's story, image, or testimony — inside or outside — for any
  purpose, including fundraising.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on confession and absolution, on baptism inside custody and its
  timing, on sacramental access, and on how repentance and restitution are understood — attributing
  positions to identifiable streams, while the facility's rules constrain what any of them can do in
  practice.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's practice of confession or restitution as universal, or let a
  restitution framing become pressure on a participant regarding legal matters that belong to counsel.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Write the facility's rules down first

Restate the rules as the facility gives them, with the date and source. Then state the standing
principle: where this program and the facility differ, the facility governs, and a volunteer who is
unsure asks the chaplaincy rather than deciding.

### Step 2 — Name the asymmetry and design the guards

Write the power analysis plainly: who leaves, who reports, what participation might be believed to
influence. Then the guards: what a volunteer never says about parole or standing, what is never written
about a participant, how a participant declines without cost, and how the program checks that
participation is genuinely voluntary.

### Step 3 — Build the promise register

Create the register in which every commitment made to a participant is recorded: what was said, by whom,
to whom, and who is accountable. State the rule that a volunteer makes no commitment on the program's
behalf without it entering the register, and that an unkept promise here does more damage than a refused
request.

### Step 4 — Design for interruption

Build the content in standalone units. State what happens on transfer, lockdown, hearing, or sudden
release — including what a participant keeps and how, subject to what the facility permits.

### Step 5 — Plan the release cliff before designing inside

Work backwards from day one after release: what exists, who makes contact and how, what the receiving
community is, and what happens when it falls through. Include the honest statement of what the program
will *not* do, so nobody leaves custody expecting something that was never offered.

### Step 6 — Set the pairing and safeguarding rules

Define who may be paired with whom and who may not, volunteer training and clearance, the two-person and
visibility norms the facility requires, what a volunteer does if approached by a participant's family,
and how a volunteer reports concern about their own influence being used. Route to the safeguarding
policy for everything it already covers.

### Step 7 — Build the content

Lay out the units. For each: the question, Scripture by address, the practice — plus *what this unit
must not ask a participant to disclose*, and *how this unit works if it is the only one they ever
attend*.

### Step 8 — Verify against the facility and stress-test

Check the whole design against the facility's stated rules with the chaplaincy. Then check the standing
failure modes: an implied institutional benefit; a promise that was not kept; the volunteer became the
only support; disclosure of offences became content; the program ended at the gate.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Design from assumed rules. Every facility differs, the rules change, and a program built on assumption
  gets refused at the gate or breaks a rule nobody had written down.
- Let anyone infer that faith participation helps their case. Even unstated, that inference corrupts
  every conversation in the room and makes voluntary participation impossible to assess.
- Promise contact after release without a mechanism and a person accountable for it. The unkept promise
  at the gate is one of the most damaging things this kind of program does.
- Build exercises around disclosing offences. It is not needed for formation, it exposes participants,
  and it turns a mentor into a de facto interrogator.
- Use a participant's story for fundraising or publicity. Consent given inside custody is not freely
  given, and the story travels further than anyone intends.
- Design a sequence that assumes attendance continues. Transfers, lockdowns, and releases end
  participation without notice, and a sequence-dependent design leaves people with fragments.

✅ **DO:**
- Write the facility's rules into the program document, dated and sourced, with the governing principle
  above them.
- Run the promise register from before the first session, and treat a refused request as better than an
  unkept one.
- Plan the release cliff first and design inside content afterwards.
- Make every unit stand alone and state what a participant keeps if this is the only one they attend.
- Keep the victim's existence in view without adjudicating anything, and never facilitate contact.
- Build a receiving community rather than a receiving volunteer, so nobody's post-release support is one
  person's availability.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective names the facility's authority, the asymmetry, the
  promise discipline, and the release cliff as the four things this variant exists to handle.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the absolutes — no implied
  institutional benefit, no offence disclosure, no facilitated contact with anyone harmed, no stated
  correctional rule.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** rules, power, promises, interruption, and release are
  worked as separate axes, which is what the generic program blueprint cannot do for this setting.
- **DS-01 (Framework Application):** the domain's program-design framework is applied intact and then
  constrained by an external rule set that overrides it wherever they conflict.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the design is verified against the facility's own stated rules with the
  chaplaincy before it runs, and the promise register verifies commitments continuously afterwards.
