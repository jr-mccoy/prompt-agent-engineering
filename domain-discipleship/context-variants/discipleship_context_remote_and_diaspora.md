# Remote and Diaspora Program Variant

**Objective:** Design a discipleship program that runs entirely or mostly at a distance — across time
zones, bandwidth limits, languages, and legal environments — holding safeguarding and identity
verification without a shared room to rely on, keeping records deliberately minimal where a participant
may be at risk, and pointing participants toward a local body rather than becoming their substitute for
one.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs a program, not security. It produces no operational security
> guidance, no communications-security instructions, no assessment of what is legal or safe in any
> country, and no evaluation of any platform's safety — those depend on facts the model cannot verify
> and a wrong answer can endanger someone. Where participants may be at legal or physical risk,
> decisions are made with people who know the setting and, where relevant, with organizations that have
> direct presence there `[VERIFY: identify the correct organization from a source you already trust]`.
> Crisis at a distance is the hardest case in this domain: mental-health emergencies, abuse, and danger
> route to local services the participant can actually reach, identified with them in advance rather
> than in the moment.

**When to use:** You are standing up a program where mentors and participants are not in the same place
— diaspora communities, scattered participants, isolated believers, or a program deliberately built
online.

**When NOT to use:**
- The group is co-located and simply uses video sometimes — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/group-leader-facilitation/biblical_groupleader_hybrid_online_format.md`.
- The core difficulty is cultural or linguistic distance rather than physical — use
  `../cross-cultural/discipleship_crosscultural_relationship.md`.
- Participants face genuine hostility for their faith — the witness module carries the graded-risk
  framework, and local judgment governs.
- You are choosing a platform for security reasons. This prompt cannot assess that; local expertise can.

**Audience:** Program leads building remote or diaspora programs, and mentors working across distance.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Where participants actually are.** Time zones, countries, and whether any are in settings where
   identification as a Christian carries legal or physical risk. If the answer to the last is yes or
   unknown, the risk design comes first.
2. **Access reality.** Bandwidth, data cost, device sharing (a phone shared with a family member changes
   everything), privacy at home, and whether video is possible or wise.
3. **Language.** Which languages participants think in, which the material is in, and who translates.
4. **Ages and safeguarding.** Whether any participant is a minor or a vulnerable adult, which at a
   distance requires more structure rather than less.
5. **What local connection exists.** Whether participants have a local congregation, gathering, or
   believer nearby — and what the program does when the honest answer is none.

**Optional:**

6. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground sacramental participation and what its absence at a
   distance means, remote membership, or particular practices. Where a tradition holds that certain
   things cannot be done remotely, that is stated as its position.
7. **Existing platform and its constraints.** As already chosen by people who know the setting.
8. **Legal or data constraints participants have identified themselves.**

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. If item 1 cannot be answered — if nobody
knows whether participants are at risk — design for risk and say so, rather than assuming safety.

## Constraints

### Must

- Design the **risk posture first** where any participant may be exposed: minimal records, minimal
  identifying detail, no participant lists that connect names to faith, and no shared documents that
  aggregate people.
- Keep **data deliberately minimal**: collect only what the program cannot run without, state what is
  kept and for how long, and state what is never recorded — including prayer requests that identify
  someone's faith, location, or family.
- Hold **safeguarding at a distance** with more structure, not less: identity verification appropriate to
  the setting, two-adult presence rules for minors, no unrecorded private one-to-one contact with a
  minor, and a defined escalation route that works across borders `[VERIFY]`.
- Design the **crisis plan in advance and with the participant**: what local services they can actually
  reach, identified together before anything happens, because a mentor thousands of miles away cannot
  find them in the moment.
- Design against **time zones honestly**: whose sleep is being sacrificed, rotated fairly rather than
  imposed on the participant every time.
- Design for **asynchronous formation** as a first-class mode, not a fallback — because live meeting
  windows will often not exist.
- Point participants toward **local embodied community** wherever it exists, and state plainly what a
  remote program cannot provide.
- Address the **sacramental and gathered gap** honestly, noting that traditions differ on what can be
  done remotely.
- Design for **bandwidth and device sharing**: an audio-only or text-only route that is equal rather than
  degraded, and content that survives being read on a shared phone.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Produce security, encryption, evasion, or platform-safety guidance, or recommend or rate any platform.
- Assess or assert what is legal, safe, or tolerated in any country, region, or community.
- Name countries, regions, or groups as hostile, safe, or persecuting, or generalize about how people
  from any nation or religion behave.
- Keep records, lists, photographs, or testimonies that could identify a participant's faith, location,
  household, or contacts where risk exists — and never use any of it for publicity or fundraising.
- Conduct private, unrecorded one-to-one contact with a minor, or accept a remote pairing with a minor
  outside the safeguarding policy.
- Assume video, stable connection, private space, an unshared device, or unlimited data.
- Promise pastoral availability or crisis response that cannot be delivered across the distance.
- Invent research on online discipleship, retention, or remote-ministry outcomes.
- Position the remote program as a replacement for a local congregation where one is reachable.
- Name a specific organization, service, platform, or network.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on remote participation in sacraments and communion, on
  baptism at a distance and who may perform it, on remote membership, on ordination and authority for
  online gatherings, and on whether a gathered body can exist online at all. Attribute positions to
  identifiable streams; let a declared tradition state its own position, including a position that some
  things simply cannot be done remotely.
- **Must Not:** resolve the question of whether an online gathering is a church, or design the program
  so that a participant's own tradition's position on remote sacraments is quietly overridden.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Set the risk posture before anything else

Determine whether any participant may be exposed. If yes or unknown, fix the posture: what is recorded
(as little as possible), what is never recorded, what lists exist (ideally none that connect names to
faith), and who decides — which is the participant and people who know their setting, never the program
at a distance.

### Step 2 — Design the data practice

State precisely what is collected, why, where it lives, who can see it, how long it is kept, and what is
deleted. Then the never-recorded list. Treat prayer requests as identifying data, because in several
settings that is exactly what they are.

### Step 3 — Build safeguarding for a setting with no shared room

Specify identity verification appropriate to the context, the rules for minors and vulnerable adults
(more structure at a distance, never less), the visibility norms for one-to-one contact, and the
escalation route — including who acts when the concern is in another country and the program is not.
Route to the safeguarding policy for everything it already covers, and mark every legal point
`[VERIFY]`.

### Step 4 — Write the crisis plan with each participant

Design the process by which each participant identifies, in advance and for themselves, what local help
they can actually reach. Record the fact that this was done — not necessarily the details. State plainly
what the mentor can and cannot do from a distance, so nobody discovers the limit during an emergency.

### Step 5 — Design the rhythm across time zones

Fix the cadence, including who sacrifices sleep and how that rotates. Then design the asynchronous mode
as the primary structure: what is exchanged, in what form, on what expectation of response, and how a
participant with two hours a week and poor connectivity still participates fully.

### Step 6 — Design for low bandwidth and shared devices

Build the audio-only and text-only routes as equal rather than reduced. State what content looks like on
a shared phone, what a participant should be able to close quickly, and what is never sent
unsolicited to a device someone else may see.

### Step 7 — Connect outward, not inward

Design how participants are pointed toward local embodied community where it exists, and what the
program honestly offers where it does not. State the gap: what a remote program cannot provide, and how
a tradition's position on remote sacraments is respected rather than worked around.

### Step 8 — Verify and stress-test

Check the design with people who know the settings involved. Then the standing failure modes: a list was
kept that should not exist; the program became the participant's only church; crisis planning happened
in the crisis; one participant always lost sleep; video was assumed.

- [ ] Data collection is minimal, with a never-recorded list that includes identifying prayer requests.
- [ ] Safeguarding at a distance is more structured than in person, with a working cross-border

- [ ] The asynchronous, audio-only, and text-only routes are equal participation, not reduced versions.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Keep a participant list linking names to faith. In several settings that document is the single most
  dangerous artefact the program will ever create, and it is usually created for convenience.
- Assume video. Bandwidth, data cost, and a shared room at home make video impossible or unwise for a
  large share of remote participants, and requiring it silently excludes them.
- Plan for crisis during a crisis. A mentor at a distance cannot find local help in the moment; the
  planning must be done in advance and by the person who lives there.
- Recommend or rate a platform. The model cannot assess platform safety, and the participant's local
  context determines the answer.
- Let the program become someone's only church without saying so. Where local community is reachable,
  the program points to it; where it is not, it says plainly what it cannot provide.
- Impose the same meeting time on the same participant forever. Losing sleep every week is a real cost
  and it is usually borne by whoever has least power in the arrangement.

✅ **DO:**
- Set the risk posture first, and treat unknown as risky.
- Treat prayer requests as identifying data and handle them accordingly.
- Design the asynchronous mode as the primary structure, so a poor connection is not a lesser
  membership.
- Make the crisis plan with each participant, and record that it was made rather than its details.
- Rotate the inconvenient hour, and say who is bearing it.
- Hold the sacramental question open, respecting each participant's own tradition's answer rather than
  the program's convenience.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective names distance, risk, minimal data, and local
  connection as the four problems this variant exists to solve, and rules out security guidance.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the risk posture and the data practice precede all
  other design, which is the only ordering that prevents a dangerous artefact being created for
  convenience.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** risk, data, safeguarding, crisis, rhythm, and
  bandwidth are worked as separate axes, so a program that solves the time-zone problem cannot pass
  while failing the record-keeping one.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the absolutes — no security guidance,
  no platform rating, no country characterization, no identifying records where risk exists.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the design is reviewed with people who know the settings, and the crisis
  plan is verified as complete for every participant before the program runs.
