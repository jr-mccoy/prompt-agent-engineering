# Mentor Training Curriculum

**Objective:** Design the training that equips a cohort of lay disciplers to begin — sequencing
sessions so boundaries and referral come first, weighting rehearsal above content, building in feedback
on observed practice, and defining readiness as what a trainee can demonstrate rather than what they
have attended.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Training does not qualify a lay mentor to counsel, diagnose, or handle
> clinical, legal, medical, or financial matters, and the curriculum must state this rather than imply
> competence it does not confer. Safeguarding content is not this prompt's to write from memory: it
> routes to the program's safeguarding lead and to
> `../program-operations/discipleship_safeguarding_and_conduct_policy.md`, with all legal requirements
> marked `[VERIFY]`.

**When to use:** You have a group of people assessed as ready (or ready with support) and need to
prepare them together before they are paired.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are assessing whether individuals should be trained at all — use
  `discipleship_mentor_readiness_assessment.md`.
- You need the boundaries framework itself, which this curriculum teaches — use
  `discipleship_mentor_boundaries_and_referral.md`.
- You want focused skill work for one mentor already active — use
  `discipleship_mentor_conversation_skills.md`.
- You are training Bible-study *teachers* rather than disciplers — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/church-staff-ministry-ops/biblical_churchstaff_teacher_training_plan.md`.

**Audience:** Program leads, discipleship pastors, and trainers preparing a mentor cohort.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The cohort.** How many, their experience of being discipled, and what they already bring —
   including anyone with professional training whose dual-relationship risk needs explicit handling.
2. **Time available.** Total hours, session length, and over what period. Be honest: most volunteer
   training gets four to eight sessions, not a term.
3. **What they are being trained for.** The specific role, the pathway they will walk someone through,
   and the support that exists around them once they start.
4. **Existing materials.** The program's boundaries framework, safeguarding policy, covenant template,
   and curriculum — supplied or described. Training must teach the program's actual documents, not
   generic ones.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May add required content — doctrinal grounding, tradition-specific
   pastoral practice, formal commissioning — and shape who delivers the training.
6. **Known failures.** Where previous mentors have struggled in this context. This is the highest-value
   input for prioritizing scarce session time.
7. **Trainer capacity.** Who is delivering, and whether they can observe practice and give feedback.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. If the program's
boundaries framework and safeguarding policy do not yet exist, say plainly that training cannot be
designed before them, and route to those prompts first.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Place **boundaries, referral, and confidentiality limits in the first session.** A trainee who is
  paired before this has been covered is a risk the curriculum created.
- Allocate **at least half the total time to rehearsal and practice**, not to teaching content.
- Build in **observed practice with feedback** on at least two occasions, and say who observes.
- Define readiness as **demonstrated behaviour**: what the trainee must be able to do, observed by
  someone, before being paired.
- Teach the **program's actual documents** — its covenant, its referral routes, its safeguarding
  policy — not generic equivalents.
- Include a session on **what mentors get wrong**, drawn from the stated known failures where supplied.
- Include an **honest exit**: a trainee may finish training and not be paired, and the curriculum says
  how that is handled and by whom.
- Say what the training **does not qualify anyone to do**.

### Must Not

- Design a content-heavy curriculum that transmits information without rehearsal. It is the default
  failure and it produces mentors who know what to do and cannot do it.
- Imply that completion confers competence in counseling, crisis response, or any clinical matter.
- Write safeguarding content, legal duties, or reporting thresholds from memory. Route and mark
  `[VERIFY]`.
- Invent training research, adult-learning statistics, or claims about mentor effectiveness.
- Quote Scripture text from memory; addresses only.
- Assume trainees can attend a fixed weekly evening, travel, read extensively between sessions, or
  arrange childcare.
- Use attendance as the readiness criterion.
- Name specific published training materials, videos, or programs.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a tradition requires particular content, delivery by an ordained or authorized person,
  or a formal commissioning before mentors begin, name it as that stream's requirement and place it in
  the curriculum accordingly.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's training conventions or authorization structure as the standard
  for lay discipler preparation.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Fix the time and the non-negotiables

State the honest total hours. Then identify what must be covered regardless of how little time exists:
boundaries and referral, confidentiality limits, safeguarding, and the program's covenant. These
consume the first block; everything else competes for what remains.

### Step 2 — Sequence the sessions

Order the sessions so nothing dangerous precedes its safeguard. Boundaries and referral come first,
before any skill that increases a trainee's confidence to go deeper. For each session give its purpose,
content, rehearsal component, and the takeaway.

### Step 3 — Design the rehearsal

For each session, specify what trainees actually practice — role-played first meetings, question
sequences, a handoff script, receiving a hard disclosure, sitting through silence. Say who they practice
with and how feedback is given.

### Step 4 — Build the observation and feedback points

Identify at least two occasions when a trainee is observed and given feedback on something they did,
not something they said they would do. Name the observer and what is being looked for.

### Step 5 — Define demonstrated readiness

Write the readiness criteria as observable behaviours: can state the confidentiality limits unprompted,
can deliver a handoff script without alarming the other person, can ask a question and then stop
talking, can say "I don't know." Attendance is not on the list.

### Step 6 — Design the between-session work and the exit

Keep between-session work small and realistic. Then write the exit: how it is handled if a trainee
finishes without being paired, who says it, and how it is framed as timing and fit rather than failure.

### Step 7 — Check the design

Run the four checks: **content-heavy** (less than half the time is rehearsal), **boundaries late**,
**attendance as readiness**, and **generic materials taught instead of the program's own**.

## Verification

- [ ] Boundaries, referral, and confidentiality limits are in the first session.
- [ ] At least half the total time is rehearsal, and the arithmetic is shown.
- [ ] At least two observation-and-feedback points are named with an observer.
- [ ] Readiness criteria are observable behaviours; attendance is explicitly excluded.
- [ ] The program's own documents are taught rather than generic equivalents.
- [ ] No safeguarding, legal, or reporting content is stated from memory — all `[VERIFY]` and routed.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Build a lecture series. Trainees who have heard about listening but never been observed listening
  will not listen well.
- Put boundaries in the last session because it is administrative. It is the session that makes the
  rest safe to teach.
- Set between-session reading that assumes hours the cohort does not have. It will not be done, and
  the curriculum will silently depend on it.
- Write the safeguarding module. It is jurisdiction- and organization-specific, and a plausible-sounding
  version is worse than none.
- Use completion as the gate. Attendance measures presence, not capability.
- Assume every trainee can rehearse comfortably in front of peers. Some need a smaller setting, and the
  design should say so.

✅ **DO:**
- Show the rehearsal arithmetic in the output, so the split is visible and defensible when time gets cut.
- Sequence so that nothing which raises a trainee's confidence to go deeper precedes the boundaries
  session.
- Have trainees say the handoff scripts out loud in training. The words are hard to produce under
  pressure the first time, and training is the cheap place to find that out.
- Teach the program's actual covenant and referral routes, so what is learned is what will be used.
- Write the not-paired exit before the cohort starts, so it can be handled well rather than improvised.
- State up front what training does not qualify anyone to do — it calibrates trainees against the
  overconfidence that training itself tends to produce.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** time and non-negotiables are fixed before sequencing,
  so boundaries and referral cannot be displaced by content when the schedule tightens.
- **ED-01 (Iterative Scaffolding):** rehearsal, observation, and feedback repeat across sessions with
  decreasing support, so capability is built rather than described.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the session plan carries a rehearsal column on every row,
  making a content-only session structurally visible in the design.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the half-time-rehearsal floor, the boundaries-first rule, and
  the attendance-is-not-readiness rule are hard constraints against this genre's default failures.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the four-check design review runs before the curriculum ships, with
  the rehearsal split shown as arithmetic rather than asserted.
