# Mentor Conversation Skills

**Objective:** Diagnose a specific mentor's actual conversational habits — the ones they have, not the
ones they intend — and build targeted drills for the skills that carry a discipling conversation: open
questions, tolerating silence, reflecting back accurately, resisting the urge to fix, and noticing what
is being avoided.

> **Boundary guardrail.** These are ordinary conversational skills for a lay discipling relationship,
> not therapeutic technique. Better listening does not qualify a mentor to work with trauma, distress,
> or clinical concerns, and a mentor who becomes skilled at drawing people out must become
> proportionately quicker to route out — see
> `discipleship_mentor_boundaries_and_referral.md`. Depth of disclosure is not a measure of the
> relationship's success.

**When to use:** A mentor is active and wants to get better, or notices a specific pattern — talking
too much, jumping to advice, filling every silence, asking questions that already contain the answer.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are designing training for a whole cohort — use `discipleship_mentor_training_curriculum.md`.
- You want questions to ask rather than skills to build — use
  `../session-and-lesson/discipleship_conversation_question_bank.md`.
- You want the structure of the meeting — use `../session-and-lesson/discipleship_session_plan_builder.md`.
- You are facilitating a group rather than a one-to-one — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/group-leader-facilitation/biblical_groupleader_facilitation_dynamics.md`.

**Audience:** An individual mentor working on their own practice, or a trainer coaching one.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What actually happens in your meetings.** As specific as possible: roughly what proportion of the
   talking is yours, how conversations tend to end, and what you do when the other person goes quiet.
2. **A recent conversation that did not go well.** What was said, as close to verbatim as you can
   manage, in a `<conversation>` block. This is the highest-value input by a wide margin.
3. **What you suspect about yourself.** Your own read on your habit — most mentors are roughly right
   about this and under-estimate the degree.

**Optional:**

4. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape conversational norms — whether direct exhortation is
   expected, how confession is handled, whether silence is a familiar practice or an awkward one.
5. **Feedback you have received.** From a mentee, a peer, or a trainer.
6. **The mentee's own style.** Talkative, reticent, deflecting with humour, over-agreeable.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. Ask for the near-verbatim
conversation specifically — general self-description reliably produces a diagnosis of the mentor's
intentions rather than their habits.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Diagnose from the **supplied conversation** where one exists, quoting the specific lines that show
  the habit. Diagnosis without evidence is a horoscope.
- Name **one primary habit** to work on, not a list. Skill change happens one habit at a time.
- Give **drills that fit inside real meetings** — no separate practice time required, since mentors will
  not do homework.
- Provide **replacement language**, not only prohibitions. "Ask an open question" is useless without
  examples of what that sounds like for this mentor in this relationship.
- Distinguish the **five core skills** and assess each: open questions, silence, reflecting back,
  resisting the fix, noticing avoidance.
- Include the **counter-failure**: what over-correcting this habit looks like, since a mentor who is
  told to stop advising sometimes stops contributing anything at all.
- Give a **way to notice in the moment**, not only in review — a signal the mentor can catch mid-sentence.

### Must Not

- Teach therapeutic technique, clinical interviewing, or anything that positions the mentor as a
  counselor.
- Encourage drawing out disclosure for its own sake, or treat depth as a success measure.
- Diagnose the mentor's character or motives from their conversational habits.
- Invent research on listening, communication statistics, or ratios presented as evidence.
- Quote Scripture text from memory; addresses only, where genuinely apt.
- Suggest recording a mentee without their explicit, informed, freely given consent — and note that
  many will find it changes the relationship even when they agree.
- Give more than one primary habit, however many are visible.
- Name specific books, courses, or training programs.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where a conversational norm is tradition-specific — expected exhortation, formal
  confession, spontaneous prayer within conversation, directive spiritual counsel — and work within the
  mentor's own tradition rather than importing another's conversational style.
- **Must Not:** present a non-directive, question-led style as the spiritually superior approach, or
  imply that traditions with directive pastoral norms listen less well.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Read the conversation for habits

Work through `<conversation>` and mark: questions that contain their own answer, advice given before
the problem was fully stated, silences filled, topics changed, and reflections that added rather than
returned. Quote the lines.

### Step 2 — Assess the five skills

| Skill | What good looks like | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Open questions** | Questions the mentor cannot predict the answer to | Leading questions; closed questions; questions containing advice |
| **Silence** | Waiting past the uncomfortable point | Filling within two seconds; rescuing |
| **Reflecting back** | Returning what was said, accurately, in their words | Adding interpretation; summarizing into your own framing |
| **Resisting the fix** | Staying with the problem long enough to understand it | Solving at the first pause; relating it to your own experience |
| **Noticing avoidance** | Registering what is stepped around, without forcing it | Missing it; or naming it too directly, too early |

Assess each from the evidence as **habitual strength**, **inconsistent**, or **habitual gap**.

### Step 3 — Name the one primary habit

Choose the single habit whose change would most improve these conversations. Say why this one and not
the others, and say the others are deliberately deferred.

### Step 4 — Build the in-meeting drill

Design a drill that fits inside a real meeting: a rule for the next three conversations, a counted
behaviour, a phrase to use instead. Make it specific enough to fail at noticeably.

### Step 5 — Write the replacement language

Give five to eight actual sentences this mentor could use, in their own register — not generic
coaching phrases. If they would not say it, it will not survive contact with a real conversation.

### Step 6 — Give the in-the-moment signal

Identify what the mentor can notice mid-conversation: the intake of breath before advising, the reach
for a similar experience of their own, the second of discomfort before filling a silence.

### Step 7 — Name the counter-failure and the review

State what over-correction looks like, so the mentor does not swing into passivity. Set a review point
and what to look at.

## Verification

- [ ] Every diagnosed habit is evidenced by a quoted line from the supplied conversation.
- [ ] Exactly one primary habit is named; the rest are explicitly deferred.
- [ ] The drill fits inside real meetings and requires no separate practice time.
- [ ] Replacement sentences are in the mentor's own register, not generic coaching language.
- [ ] The over-correction failure is named.
- [ ] Nothing teaches therapeutic technique or frames depth of disclosure as success.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Diagnose from self-description alone. Mentors describe their intentions accurately and their habits
  poorly, and the gap between the two is the whole subject.
- Give a list of five habits to fix. The mentor will attempt all five, succeed at none, and conclude
  they are bad at this.
- Supply generic coaching phrases. A mentor who would never say "what I'm hearing is" will not say it,
  and the drill dies.
- Treat a mentee opening up more as proof the skills worked. Sometimes it means the mentor has become
  good at drawing out material they are not equipped to hold.
- Suggest recording without dwelling on consent, and on the fact that consent does not make it neutral.
- Read a habit as a character flaw. Over-advising is usually care under time pressure, not arrogance.

✅ **DO:**
- Ask for near-verbatim dialogue and quote it back. The lines do the diagnostic work that description
  cannot.
- Pick one habit and say plainly which others are being deferred, so the mentor is not privately
  tracking all of them.
- Design the drill as a rule with a countable behaviour — "ask, then say nothing until they speak" —
  so failure is noticeable rather than vague.
- Write replacement sentences in the mentor's own idiom, drawn from how they already speak in the
  transcript.
- Name the in-the-moment signal, since noticing mid-sentence is what changes behaviour; reviewing
  afterwards mostly produces regret.
- Pair improved listening with a reminder that better access means faster routing out, not deeper
  handling.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** transcript analysis precedes skill assessment, which
  precedes habit selection, so the drill targets an evidenced habit rather than a self-reported one.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** five independently assessed skills separate a
  mentor who asks well but cannot wait from one who waits well but reframes everything — distinct
  problems needing distinct drills.
- **ED-01 (Iterative Scaffolding):** one habit, an in-meeting drill, an in-the-moment signal, and a
  review point form a single practice loop sized to what a volunteer will actually sustain.
- **RP-02 (Audience-Specific Framing):** replacement language is written in the mentor's own register
  drawn from their transcript, which is what determines whether the new phrasing survives a real
  conversation.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the verification block enforces evidence for every diagnosed habit and
  the one-habit limit, and requires the over-correction failure to be named.
