# Mentor Support and Sustainability

**Objective:** Design the structure that keeps lay disciplers going — a peer cohort, a check-in cadence,
explicit capacity limits, and named early-warning signs — on the premise that mentor attrition is
usually a failure of program design rather than of the volunteer's commitment.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Support structures are not treatment. A mentor showing signs of depression,
> anxiety, exhaustion beyond ordinary tiredness, or personal crisis needs professional help and a
> reduced load, not a better check-in rhythm — route to a licensed professional and, where there is
> risk, to appropriate services `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`, and
> to `domain-psychology/`. A mentor carrying something heavy from a mentee's disclosure may also need
> supervision beyond peer support.

**When to use:** Setting up a mentoring program, or responding to mentors quietly dropping out,
going flat, or over-extending.

**When NOT to use:**
- A season has ended and you want to review it — use `discipleship_mentor_season_debrief.md`.
- You need more mentors — use `../program-operations/discipleship_mentor_pipeline_and_capacity.md`.
- You are reviewing the whole program's health — use
  `../program-operations/discipleship_program_health_review.md`.
- An individual mentor is burnt out and needs personal recovery — use
  `domain-personal-development/prompts/agency/agency_burnout_recovery.md`.

**Audience:** Program leads designing the support layer; mentors assessing their own sustainability.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The mentor load as it actually is.** How many mentees each carries, meeting frequency, and what
   else the program asks of them beyond the meetings — reporting, training, events, admin.
2. **What is happening now.** Attrition, quiet disengagement, over-extension, or mentors absorbing
   material they should be routing out.
3. **What support exists today.** Honestly, including whether it happens as designed or only on paper.
4. **Who the mentors are.** Their other commitments — jobs, families, caregiving, other church roles.
   A program that treats mentoring as their only volunteering will overload them.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape who provides oversight, whether pastoral supervision is
   formal, and what confidentiality is permitted within a peer cohort.
6. **Known departures.** Why previous mentors stopped, if known — the most direct evidence available.
7. **Program constraints.** Staff time, budget, and geography.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. Ask specifically what
the program asks of mentors *beyond* the meetings — this is where load accumulates unnoticed, and it is
rarely volunteered.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Treat attrition as a **design question first**: what about the structure made stopping the reasonable
  choice.
- Set an **explicit capacity limit** — how many mentees, over what period, with how long between —
  and state it as the program's rule, not the mentor's preference to negotiate.
- Design a **peer cohort** with a stated purpose, size, cadence, and confidentiality boundary, so it
  does not become either a meeting about nothing or a place where mentees are discussed carelessly.
- Provide a route to **supervision beyond peers** for material a peer cohort cannot hold.
- Name **early-warning signs** that are observable by the program, not only self-reported — because the
  mentors most at risk are the least likely to raise it.
- Build in a **legitimate pause and exit**, with no penalty and no requirement to explain.
- Include the **total load audit**: everything the program asks of a mentor, added up.
- State what the support structure **cannot fix**.

### Must Not

- Respond to overload by adding another meeting to a mentor's calendar without removing something.
- Treat a mentor's difficulty as a spiritual problem, a commitment problem, or a call to greater
  sacrifice.
- Design a peer cohort in which mentees' disclosures circulate without a clear, stated boundary.
- Invent burnout statistics, volunteer-retention research, or attrition benchmarks.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, and do not use passages about perseverance, servanthood, or
  laying down one's life to discourage a mentor from stopping.
- Name a specific counselor, service, or program.
- Frame stepping back, pausing, or leaving as letting anyone down.
- Assume mentors can attend an additional evening, travel, or arrange childcare for support meetings.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a tradition provides formal oversight — spiritual direction, elder supervision,
  confessor, ministerial accountability — integrate it and note that other streams rely on peer or
  staff support instead.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's oversight structure as required for safe lay ministry, or
  treat peer-only support as inherently deficient.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Audit the total load

List everything the program asks of a mentor: meetings, preparation, travel, reporting, training,
events, group attendance, admin. Total it honestly per month. Most programs discover the meetings are
the smaller half.

### Step 2 — Diagnose the current attrition

Take what is happening and ask the design question: what made stopping reasonable? Distinguish
**overload**, **isolation**, **carrying something heavy alone**, **unclear expectations**, **no visible
fruit**, and **life change**. Each has a different fix and only one of them is about the mentor.

### Step 3 — Set the capacity limit

State the maximum: mentees at once, season length, and gap between seasons. Make it the program's rule
so that a mentor declining a second mentee is following policy rather than refusing a request.

### Step 4 — Design the peer cohort

Specify purpose, size, cadence, format, who convenes it, and — critically — the confidentiality
boundary: what mentors may bring about their own experience versus what they may not repeat about a
mentee. Make it small, infrequent enough to be sustainable, and useful enough to be missed.

### Step 5 — Provide supervision beyond peers

Name the route for material a peer cohort cannot hold: a disclosure that has stayed with the mentor, a
relationship that has gone wrong, a mentor's own difficulty. Say who provides it and how it is
requested without stigma.

This step designs the *route*; the conversation it delivers is
`discipleship_mentor_case_consultation.md`, which settles the confidentiality frame and de-identification
before anything is disclosed and runs the standing check that consulting is not being used in place of a
referral. A route with no agreed shape to the conversation at the end of it tends to become either a
report on the mentee or a chat that changes nothing.

### Step 6 — Name the early-warning signs

List signs observable by the program — missed check-ins, cancelled meetings, a mentor who stops asking
questions, a mentor who has begun handling something they should have routed — alongside self-reported
ones. Say who watches, and what they do when they see one.

### Step 7 — Write the pause and exit, and state the limits

Write how a mentor pauses or stops: to whom, in what words, with what handover for the mentee, and with
explicit confirmation that no explanation is required. Then state what support cannot fix — a load that
is simply too large, a life season that has changed, or a program design that needs fewer mentees per
mentor.

## Verification

- [ ] The total load audit counts everything beyond the meetings and shows a monthly total.
- [ ] Attrition is diagnosed as a design question, with the mentor-caused pattern separated from the
      five structural ones.
- [ ] A numeric capacity limit is stated as program rule.
- [ ] The peer cohort has an explicit confidentiality boundary covering what may not be repeated.
- [ ] Early-warning signs include program-observable ones, not only self-reported.
- [ ] Pausing and stopping require no explanation, and the output says so.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Add a support meeting without removing something. Support that increases total load is not support.
- Count only the mentoring hours. Preparation, travel, reporting, and events routinely exceed the
  meetings themselves.
- Rely on mentors to raise their own struggle. The ones closest to stopping are the least likely to
  say so, often because they feel they are failing.
- Let the peer cohort become a place where mentees are discussed. Without a stated boundary it will,
  and that is a real breach even among well-meaning people.
- Use perseverance or servanthood passages to make stopping feel like a failure of faith.
- Treat "no visible fruit" as a mentor problem. It is usually a mismatch between the program's implied
  timeline and how formation actually moves.

✅ **DO:**
- Put the load total in front of the program leadership as arithmetic. It is usually the finding.
- Make the capacity limit a rule rather than a suggestion, so declining costs a mentor nothing socially.
- Write the confidentiality boundary in the cohort's terms of reference, and repeat it at each meeting.
- Give the program observable early-warning signs, since self-report arrives too late.
- Provide a supervision route for the mentor carrying a disclosure they cannot put down. Peer support
  is not built for that.
- Say explicitly that stopping requires no explanation — otherwise mentors disappear rather than
  handing over, and the mentee pays for it.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the total-load audit precedes diagnosis and design,
  so support is built against measured load rather than against the assumption that the meetings are
  the work.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** six attrition patterns — five structural, one
  personal — separate causes with different fixes and prevent the default reading of attrition as
  weak commitment.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the capacity limit as program rule, the no-added-load
  constraint, and the ban on perseverance passages as pressure are hard constraints against the ways
  volunteer support quietly becomes another demand.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the load audit table totals hours explicitly, turning an
  impression of busyness into the arithmetic that drives the design decision.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the verification block checks the confidentiality boundary,
  program-observable warning signs, and the no-explanation exit before the structure is adopted.
