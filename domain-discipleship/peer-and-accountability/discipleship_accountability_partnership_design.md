# Accountability Partnership Design

**Objective:** Design a peer-to-peer accountability partnership that helps two people keep what they
have chosen to keep — mutual rather than supervisory, built on each person's own stated commitments,
without monitoring, device checking, record inspection, streak counting, or any mechanism that turns
support into surveillance and honesty into performance.

> **Boundary guardrail.** An accountability partner is a peer, not a counselor, sponsor, confessor, or
> monitor. Compulsive behaviour, addiction, self-harm, disordered eating, abuse, and untreated
> mental-health conditions are not accountability matters: they route to licensed professionals and
> specialist programs `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`, and continuing
> to handle them as accountability delays real help. Where either partner is a minor, the safeguarding
> policy governs and a peer partnership between a minor and an adult is not established.

**When to use:** Two people want to help each other keep something — a practice, a boundary, a change —
and want a structure that does not curdle.

**When NOT to use:**
- One person is discipling the other. That is a mentor relationship, and the covenant is at
  `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_relationship_covenant.md`.
- The behaviour in question is compulsive. Route out first; a partnership is not the intervention.
- The practices themselves need designing — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_spiritual_practices_designer.md` first, then bring them here.
- Someone else wants a partnership arranged *for* two people who have not chosen it. Assigned
  accountability is the version that fails.

**Audience:** Pairs setting up a partnership, and program leads who resource them without arranging
them.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Both people's own commitments, in their own words.** What each has chosen to keep — not what
   someone thinks they should. If either commitment came from someone else, that is the first thing to
   fix.
2. **Whether this is genuinely mutual.** Both bring commitments, both answer, both can call it off. A
   partnership where one person's life is the subject is not a partnership.
3. **What is out of scope.** What each person is not bringing here, stated in advance, so that a
   question about it is out of bounds rather than merely uncomfortable.
4. **Cadence and format.** How often, how long, and by what channel — sized to be sustainable rather
   than intensive.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground mutual confession, a rule of life, examen, or a
   specific accountability practice. Contested specifics stay attributed, and no practice becomes a
   requirement for the other person.
6. **Prior experience.** A previous partnership that went badly, and specifically how — since the design
   should guard against that failure by name.
7. **A third person or group** the pair is connected to, and what they are and are not told.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. If item 2 cannot be answered yes — if only
one person is bringing anything — stop and say so. Asymmetric accountability is the setup for the harm
this design exists to prevent.

## Constraints

### Must

- Build the partnership on **each person's own chosen commitments**, stated in their own words, with
  either person free to change theirs at any point.
- Keep it **mutual**: both bring, both answer, both may decline a question, and both may end it.
- Fix **what is out of scope** before the first conversation, in writing, so that a question about it is
  agreed to be out of bounds.
- Design the interaction as **asking, not checking**: the partner asks, the person answers, and the
  answer is accepted. There is no verification.
- **Ban the surveillance mechanisms explicitly**: no device or browser checking, no shared location, no
  monitoring software reports, no reading anyone's messages, no financial or search-history inspection,
  no reporting to a third party.
- **Ban the metrics**: no streaks, scores, tallies, percentages, or public tracking. A number turns an
  honest answer into a cost.
- Design the response to **"I didn't"** as the design's core test: what the partner says, which is not
  disappointment, a probe, or a plan — and what happens next, which is usually less than either person
  expects.
- Include the **confidentiality limits before anything is shared**: what stays, what cannot, and where
  the second goes.
- Build a **no-explanation exit**: either person may end or pause the partnership without giving a
  reason, and the relationship survives it.
- Set a **review date** at which the partnership is deliberately re-chosen or ended, rather than
  drifting.
- Route compulsion, distress, and safety concerns **out** `[VERIFY]`, with words.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Establish any monitoring, checking, verifying, or reporting mechanism, including software.
- Count, score, chart, publish, or compare either person's consistency.
- Assign a partnership, or make participation in one a condition of a role, a stage, or belonging.
- Set one person's commitments for them, or let a partner add to them.
- Frame the partnership as confession, and never as confession to a person who then holds it over them.
- Treat a failure to keep a commitment as a spiritual verdict, or design any consequence for one.
- Continue to handle compulsive behaviour as accountability once it has been identified.
- Pair a minor with an adult in a peer accountability partnership.
- Invent research on accountability, habit change, or recovery outcomes.
- Name a specific program, app, or service.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on mutual confession, on confession to a priest or an elder, on
  the place of a rule of life, and on whether accountability structures are appropriate at all —
  attributing each to identifiable streams and letting a declared tradition foreground its own.
- **Must Not:** import one tradition's confession practice as an expectation on a partner who holds
  differently, or present peer accountability as a biblical requirement.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Confirm mutuality, or stop

Establish that both people are bringing their own commitments and both will answer. If one person is
the subject, name it: what is actually wanted is either a mentor relationship or professional support,
and this design will not produce a safe version of it.

### Step 2 — Write each person's own commitments

Each writes what they have chosen to keep, in their own words, small enough to be achievable. State that
either may revise theirs at any time without the other's agreement, and that neither may add to the
other's.

### Step 3 — Fix what is out of scope

Each states what they are not bringing here. Write it down. This is what converts an uncomfortable
question into an agreed boundary, and it is the single most protective element of the design.

### Step 4 — Ban the mechanisms, by name

List the surveillance mechanisms explicitly and rule them out: device and browser checking, location
sharing, monitoring reports, message reading, financial or history inspection, third-party reporting.
Then the metrics: streaks, scores, tallies, charts. Naming them individually matters, because each one
arrives sounding reasonable.

### Step 5 — Design the answer to "I didn't"

Write what the partner actually says, verbatim, when the answer is no — and what they do not say. Then
what happens next: usually a question about what got in the way, and nothing else. No plan is imposed,
no disappointment is expressed, and the next meeting is not a follow-up on this one unless the person
asks for it.

### Step 6 — Set the confidentiality limits before the first conversation

Write what stays between them, what cannot (safety, harm to others, anything the program's policy
requires), and where the second goes. Both say it to each other before either discloses anything.

### Step 7 — Build the exit and the review

Write the words for pausing or ending, with no explanation required, and the agreement that the
friendship survives it. Then set the review date at which the partnership is re-chosen or closed
deliberately.

### Step 8 — Verify

Check: is it mutual; are commitments each person's own; is out-of-scope written; is every surveillance
mechanism banned by name; is there no metric anywhere; is "I didn't" answered well; is the exit
explanation-free.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Let one person be the subject. Asymmetric accountability is not a partnership; it is supervision
  without any of supervision's safeguards, and it produces concealment within weeks.
- Add checking "just to help". Every monitoring mechanism arrives sounding supportive, and each one
  converts the relationship from asking to verifying — after which honesty has a cost and stops.
- Keep a streak. The moment a number exists, the honest answer becomes expensive and the partnership
  starts measuring compliance instead of supporting a person.
- Respond to "I didn't" with disappointment, a probe, or a plan. All three teach the person that the
  cheaper answer is yes, and the partnership becomes an elaborate way of lying to a friend.
- Continue handling a compulsion as accountability. It delays real help for months and leaves the
  person convinced the failure was moral.
- Assign partnerships. Chosen partnerships sometimes work; assigned ones reliably produce two people
  performing a structure neither wanted.

✅ **DO:**
- Confirm mutuality first and be willing to say this is not the right structure.
- Write out-of-scope before anything else is discussed. It is the element that makes honesty safe.
- Ban the mechanisms by name rather than in principle — each one has to be refused individually,
  because each arrives with a reasonable justification attached.
- Write the "I didn't" response verbatim and rehearse it. It is the whole design in one sentence.
- Say the confidentiality limits to each other before either discloses anything.
- Set a review date so the partnership is deliberately re-chosen rather than quietly enduring past its
  usefulness.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective names the surveillance-and-shame failure as the
  thing being designed against, so a partnership that adds checking has failed rather than improvised.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the load-bearing element — every surveillance mechanism and
  every metric is banned by name, because each is individually plausible and only collectively fatal.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** mutuality, scope, mechanism, response, and exit are
  worked separately, so a partnership that is mutual but metric-driven cannot pass.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the mutuality check runs before design and can return "this is not a
  peer partnership", and the verification list checks the mechanisms that reintroduce surveillance.
- **RP-02 (Audience Framing):** the design is written to two peers rather than to a supervisor and a
  subject, and every element reinforces symmetry.
