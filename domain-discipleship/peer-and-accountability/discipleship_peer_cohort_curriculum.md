# Mentor Peer-Cohort Curriculum

**Objective:** Design the ongoing curriculum for a cohort of disciplers meeting as peers — what they
work on across a season, in what order, with what practices — so that mentors are themselves being
formed rather than only being resourced, and with a confidentiality rule that keeps their mentees out of
the room and a stated line between peer support and supervision.

> **Boundary guardrail.** A peer cohort is not clinical supervision, therapy, or a case conference. It
> does not review a mentee's situation, assess anyone's spiritual state, or make decisions about
> another person's care. Where a mentor is carrying something that needs professional support — their
> own mental health, burnout that has become a health matter, or the weight of a disclosure — that
> routes to licensed help `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. Where a
> safeguarding concern exists, it goes to the safeguarding lead immediately and never into a peer
> discussion.

**When to use:** You are setting up or rebuilding a standing peer group for the disciplers in a program,
and want more than an unstructured monthly check-in.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want the initial training for new mentors — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_training_curriculum.md`.
- The problem is mentor burnout and capacity — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_support_and_sustainability.md`, which this cohort delivers
  on rather than replaces.
- You want the facilitation of a single cohort meeting — use
  `discipleship_peer_cohort_facilitation.md`.
- You need actual supervision with authority to intervene. That is a different structure and should be
  named as such.

**Audience:** Program leads and cohort facilitators designing the mentors' own formation.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Who is in the cohort.** How many, how experienced, whether they are peers or include the person
   who oversees them — which changes what can honestly be said.
2. **What the cohort is for**, stated in one sentence: formation, support, skill, accountability, or a
   defined combination. A cohort that is all four without saying so becomes none of them.
3. **The confidentiality rule.** What may be said about a mentee, in what form, and what may not — settled
   before the first meeting, because it will otherwise be settled by whatever the first person shares.
4. **Cadence and season length.** How often, for how long, and whether the cohort re-forms.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground practices the cohort holds together — fixed prayer,
   confession, examen, a rule of life. Contested specifics stay attributed.
6. **What has surfaced before.** Recurring difficulties in this program that the curriculum should
   address.
7. **Who facilitates**, and whether that person holds any authority over the members.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. Item 3 in particular — a cohort without a
settled confidentiality rule will discuss mentees within two meetings, and the mentees never agreed to
it.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Fix the **confidentiality rule first**, in writing: mentees are not discussed by name or by
  identifiable detail; a mentor may bring *their own* difficulty, and the material stays with them
  rather than becoming a case.
- Name the **line between peer support and supervision**, and say which this is — including what the
  cohort cannot do and where anything requiring authority goes.
- Design for **the mentor's own formation**, not only their skill: prayer, Scripture, character, and
  what discipling others is doing to them.
- Include **the standing risks of the role** as recurring content: dependence, over-reach, savior
  dynamics, boundary drift, and the mentor's own need to be needed.
- Build in **practice and rehearsal**, not only discussion — the skills that fail in the room are
  behavioural.
- Make the cohort a place where **admitting difficulty is normal**, with the facilitator modelling it
  first and no mechanism that could make disclosure costly.
- Design **honest attendance expectations** that a mentor with a job and a family can meet, and a way to
  miss without it being remarked on.
- Include the **exit**: how a mentor steps back from the cohort or from mentoring altogether without
  it being treated as failure.
- Route the mentor's own mental health, burnout, and any safeguarding concern **out** `[VERIFY]`.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Discuss a mentee by name, by identifiable detail, or in a form the mentee would recognize as
  themselves.
- Function as a case conference, an assessment body, or a route by which information about mentees
  reaches program decisions.
- Score, rank, review, or report members' performance, or let the cohort feed a mentor's evaluation.
- Require disclosure of a member's personal struggles, sin, or spiritual state as a condition of
  belonging.
- Invent supervision research, mentoring outcome data, or burnout statistics.
- Provide clinical guidance, or treat the cohort as a substitute for professional support.
- Handle a safeguarding matter in the room. It goes to the safeguarding lead, immediately and outside
  the cohort.
- Let a member's authority over others — a manager, an overseer, a pastor — go unnamed where it changes
  what can safely be said.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on confession among peers, on spiritual direction and its
  authority, on accountability structures, and on the place of a rule of life — attributing each to
  identifiable streams and letting a declared tradition foreground its own practice.
- **Must Not:** import a confession or accountability practice from one tradition as an expectation on
  members who hold differently, or present peer confession as a requirement of the role.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Write the confidentiality rule and the supervision line

Settle both before designing anything: what may be said about a mentee (in almost all cases, nothing
identifiable), what a mentor may bring about themselves, and what the cohort is not — supervision,
assessment, or a channel to program decisions. Write the sentence a facilitator says when a member
starts describing their mentee.

### Step 2 — Fix the cohort's purpose in one sentence

State what it is for. If it is more than one thing, state the proportions — how much of a meeting is
formation, support, skill, and accountability — because unstated proportions default to whoever speaks
first.

### Step 3 — Build the season's arc

Design the sequence across the season: what is worked in what order, building rather than rotating.
Include both the mentor's own formation strand and the role-skill strand, and state their dependency —
skill content lands differently on a mentor who is depleted, which is why the formation strand comes
first.

### Step 4 — Put the standing risks on the calendar

Schedule the recurring content the role requires: dependence, over-reach, savior dynamics, boundary
drift, the need to be needed, and the mentor's own unmet need surfacing in the relationship. These are
not one-time training topics; they recur because the drift recurs.

### Step 5 — Design rehearsal into every session

Specify what is practised rather than discussed: the handoff words, the hard-conversation opening, the
confidentiality sentence, saying "I don't know", ending a relationship well. A cohort that only talks
produces mentors who can describe good practice.

### Step 6 — Make difficulty ordinary

Design the mechanisms that make admitting struggle normal: the facilitator goes first, there is a
standing question everyone answers, nothing said is recorded, and no member's disclosure can affect
their standing. Then state what would make this fail — usually the presence of someone with authority
over members, unnamed.

### Step 7 — Set attendance and exit honestly

Fix a cadence a working mentor can meet, a way to miss without comment, and the exit route from the
cohort or from mentoring — including stepping back for a season, which is a normal outcome rather than
a failure.

### Step 8 — Verify

Check: is the confidentiality rule written and rehearsed; is the supervision line stated; does the
formation strand exist rather than only skill; are the standing risks scheduled; is there rehearsal in
every session; can a member leave without cost.

## Verification

- [ ] The confidentiality rule is written before the curriculum, with the facilitator's intervening
      sentence rehearsed.
- [ ] The line between peer support and supervision is stated, with where authority-requiring matters go.
- [ ] The formation strand exists alongside the skill strand, and its precedence is stated.
- [ ] The standing risks of the role are scheduled to recur, not taught once.
- [ ] Every session has something rehearsed rather than only discussed.
- [ ] Nothing in the cohort can affect a member's standing, evaluation, or program decisions.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Let mentees into the room. It happens within two meetings unless the rule is written, and the mentee
  never agreed to be discussed by six people.
- Run the cohort as a case conference. It feels responsible, it produces decisions about people who are
  not present, and it converts peer support into informal supervision without the safeguards of either.
- Put a member's overseer in the cohort without naming it. Every disclosure is then filtered, and the
  cohort becomes a performance in which nobody is struggling.
- Teach the standing risks once. Dependence and over-reach are drifts, not knowledge gaps, and they
  return every season regardless of who was trained.
- Design a cadence that only an unusually free person can meet. The mentors who most need the cohort are
  the ones with the least discretionary time.
- Treat stepping back from mentoring as a failure. A mentor who withdraws honestly has done the
  program a service, and the cohort should be where that is possible.

✅ **DO:**
- Write the confidentiality rule first and rehearse the facilitator's sentence for interrupting.
- State the cohort's proportions, so it does not become whichever of its four purposes shouts loudest.
- Put the mentor's own formation before the skill content in the arc.
- Schedule the standing risks to recur, with the question that surfaces each.
- Rehearse something every session — the words are what fail under pressure, not the concepts.
- Design the exit as a normal route, including stepping back for a season.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective makes the mentors' own formation the deliverable
  and names the confidentiality rule and supervision line as constitutive, not incidental.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the confidentiality rule and purpose are settled before
  any content is designed, which is the only ordering that keeps mentees out of the room.
- **ED-01 (Iterative Scaffolding):** the season arc builds rather than rotates, with the formation strand
  preceding the skill strand because depleted mentors cannot absorb technique.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the absolutes — no identifiable
  mentee discussion, no case conference, no performance review, no safeguarding matter in the room.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the confidentiality rule, supervision line, formation strand, and
  exit route are each verified before the cohort runs.
