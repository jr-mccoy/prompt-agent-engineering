# Digital Life Module

**Objective:** Design a module that forms a disciple's judgment about their digital life — what their
attention is being shaped by, what they perform online and for whom, how outrage and platform incentives
work on them, and what they say and to whom — producing durable practice rather than a set of screen
rules that will be abandoned in three weeks.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs formation, not treatment or safety response. Compulsive
> pornography use, compulsive gambling, disordered eating driven by content, self-harm content
> exposure, online grooming, harassment, extortion, and image-based abuse all route to licensed
> professionals, platform reporting channels, and where relevant to authorities
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. A mentor does not become anyone's
> content monitor, does not receive accountability-software reports on another adult, and does not
> handle a safety incident as a formation matter. Where minors are involved, the safeguarding policy
> governs.

**When to use:** You are building the digital-life module in a curriculum, or a mentee's phone,
platforms, or online conduct have become the recurring live issue.

**When NOT to use:**
- The live issue is compulsion — route to specialist help first; formation follows treatment, not
  instead of it.
- The live issue is sexual formation more broadly — use
  `discipleship_module_sexuality_and_singleness.md`, which carries the STRONG-GUARD framework.
- You want the practice rhythms designed — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_spiritual_practices_designer.md`.
- The participant is being harassed, extorted, or targeted. That is a safety matter, not a module.

**Audience:** Curriculum designers, mentors, and small-group leaders building the module for adults or
(with safeguarding in place) for youth tracks.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Where this module sits.** Stage, prerequisites, session count.
2. **The actual digital life in the room.** Which platforms, what devices, whether work requires
   constant availability, whether participants have public-facing accounts, and whether anyone's
   income depends on a platform. A module that assumes optional use is useless to someone whose job is
   online.
3. **Ages.** Adults, mixed, or minors. Minors change consent, content, and oversight entirely, and
   the safeguarding policy governs.
4. **The referral landscape.** Local and program-level specialist help, described rather than named
   from memory.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground fasting practice, media abstinence,
   simplicity commitments, Sabbath framing, or specific teaching on speech and witness. Contested
   specifics stay attributed.
6. **Community norms already in force.** Existing covenants, filtering expectations, or leadership
   social-media policies.
7. **Recent incident.** A public conflict online involving the community changes what can be safely
   discussed as material.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. Do not assume optional or recreational use;
for many participants the device is the job, the family connection, and the only access to community.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Form **judgment**, not compliance: participants should finish able to decide, not able to recite rules.
- Cover, at minimum: **attention, formation by feeds, performance and audience, outrage and conflict,
  sexual content, and speech** — with a practice attached to each.
- Treat the platform's **design incentives** as part of the subject: the participant is not simply
  weak-willed, they are inside a system built to hold them.
- Distinguish **use, heavy use, and compulsion**, and state that the third is not a discipline problem
  and routes out.
- Include the participant whose **livelihood or family connection is online**, with content that does
  not treat disconnection as the goal.
- Include **what a disciple says online** — speech, argument, public rebuke, and the community's witness
  — as formation territory, not only consumption.
- Attach practices that are **measurable by the participant themselves**, reported by no one, and that
  never require a mentor to inspect a device, receive reports, or hold passwords.
- Reference Scripture **by address only**, translation named, noting that its application to digital
  life is an inference the module is making rather than a text about phones.
- Route compulsion, harassment, extortion, image-based abuse, and grooming **out** `[VERIFY]`.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Invent statistics or research about screen time, attention spans, dopamine, social-media harm,
  pornography effects, or brain changes. This topic is saturated with confident false numbers, and the
  module adds none.
- Prescribe a screen-time figure, app list, filtering product, or device rule as the spiritual standard.
- Set up mentor-held accountability software, device inspection, password sharing, or location
  monitoring between adults.
- Frame ordinary use as sin, or a participant's relationship with technology as evidence of their
  spiritual state.
- Give clinical guidance on compulsion, addiction, anxiety, or content-driven disorders. All route out.
- State law on privacy, data, defamation, age limits, or online speech. Mark `[VERIFY]` and route to
  `domain-legal/`.
- Use a participant's browsing, posting, or usage as group material.
- Name a specific app, filter, platform policy, or organization as recommended.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on media abstinence and fasting, on simplicity commitments, on
  Sabbath's shape as it applies to devices, on public speech and rebuke, and on how communal
  accountability practices are understood. Attribute positions to identifiable streams; let a declared
  tradition foreground its own practice.
- **Must Not:** present one stream's media discipline as the biblical standard, or let an accountability
  practice from one tradition become an expectation applied to everyone in the group.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Establish the honest starting position

Describe the actual digital life in the room, including the participant whose income or family
connection runs through a platform. Then state the module's stance: the goal is not less screen time; it
is a person who knows what is forming them and can choose.

### Step 2 — Name the system, not just the user

Set out how platform incentives work — engagement, variable reward, outrage amplification, social
comparison, and the economics behind them — as observable design facts described without invented
research. Participants who understand the system stop treating their own behaviour as a mystery of the
will.

### Step 3 — Work the six territories

| Territory | The formation question under it |
|---|---|
| Attention | What has first claim on my attention, and what actually gets it? |
| Feeds | Who is forming my sense of what is normal, urgent, and true? |
| Performance | Who am I online, who is the audience, and what does maintaining them cost? |
| Outrage | What makes me angry online, who benefits from that, and what do I do with it? |
| Sexual content | What am I consuming, what is it forming, and where does this stop being a formation question? |
| Speech | What do I say, to whom, and would I say it to their face? |

For each: Scripture by address (with the inference named), the distortion common in this group, and the
practice.

### Step 4 — Draw the compulsion line

Write the plain-language distinction between use, heavy use, and compulsion, with the observable
signals of the third. State clearly that compulsion routes to specialist help, that willpower content
is the wrong response to it, and that the mentor's role is referral and continued relationship — not
monitoring. Give the mentor's words.

### Step 5 — Design practices that need no observer

Build practices participants can run, notice, and adjust alone: a fast with a defined length and a
defined return, an audit of what they actually looked at, a friction change to their environment, a
speech rule they set themselves. No practice may require a mentor to inspect, receive, or verify
anything.

### Step 6 — Build the sessions

Lay out sessions against the count. For each: the question, Scripture by address with the inference
flagged, territory, practice — plus *the participant this session could shame*, and the wording that
prevents it. Exegesis routes to `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/`.

### Step 7 — Write the safety and referral edges

Enumerate: compulsion, harassment and pile-ons, extortion and sextortion, image-based abuse, grooming
(and any concern involving a minor), and content encountered that indicates risk to someone else.
Destination kind `[VERIFY]`, mentor's words, and how the relationship continues. Note that some of these
are urgent and none of them wait for the next session.

### Step 8 — Stress-test

Check against the standing failure modes: the module became screen rules; it produced shame without
capability; it made the mentor a monitor; it assumed optional use; it repeated technology folklore as
fact. Early signal and fix.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Quote a screen-time statistic, a dopamine claim, or an attention-span figure. The widely repeated ones
  are mostly wrong, and a module that leans on them collapses the first time a participant checks.
- Issue a rule set. Rules produce compliance for about three weeks and shame thereafter; judgment
  survives the phone changing.
- Make the mentor an accountability monitor between adults. It converts formation into surveillance and
  reliably ends in either concealment or dependence.
- Treat compulsion with willpower content. It is the wrong response, it delays real help, and it teaches
  the participant that their failure is spiritual.
- Assume participants could simply leave. For many the platform is work, family, immigration paperwork,
  or the only accessible community.
- Use someone's disclosed browsing or posting as group material, ever.

✅ **DO:**
- Teach the system's design first. Participants who see the mechanism stop treating their own behaviour
  as inexplicable weakness.
- Include speech and public conduct. Most digital-life modules cover only consumption, and the damage a
  disciple does online is usually done by posting.
- Build practices with a defined length and a defined return — a fast with no return date is a rule
  wearing a practice's clothes.
- Draw the compulsion line explicitly and rehearse the mentor's words for it.
- Mark the urgent safety items as urgent, in the table, where anyone delivering the module will see it.
- Say out loud that Scripture is not about phones, and that the module is making an inference — it keeps
  the teaching honest and the participant's trust intact.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective fixes judgment rather than compliance as the
  deliverable, which is what separates this module from the screen-rule material it will be compared to.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the system's design is taught before any territory, so
  practices are built on an understanding of the mechanism rather than on willpower.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** six territories — including speech and performance —
  cover the producing side of digital life that consumption-only modules omit entirely.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the domain's hardest constraints here:
  no invented technology research, no prescribed rules, and no mentor-held monitoring between adults.
- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** Scripture by address with the digital-life inference named, and a
  blanket prohibition on statistics in a topic where fabricated numbers are the norm.
