# Forgiveness and Reconciliation Module

**Objective:** Design a module that forms a disciple's understanding and practice of forgiveness while
keeping three separable things separate — forgiveness, reconciliation, and the restoration of trust —
so that no participant is taught that releasing a debt obliges them to return to a relationship, and so
that safety governs the whole module rather than appearing as a caveat at the end.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs formation, not the aftermath of abuse. Domestic abuse, sexual
> abuse, child abuse, coercive control, stalking, and ongoing danger route immediately to licensed
> professionals and appropriate authorities `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official
> source]`. A lay mentor never mediates between a person who has been harmed and the person who harmed
> them, never advises a participant to remain in or return to a relationship, and never treats a
> reconciliation refusal as a spiritual problem. Trauma, complicated grief, and intrusive distress route
> to `domain-psychology/` and a licensed professional.

**When to use:** You are building the forgiveness module in a curriculum, or a discipleship
relationship has arrived at a real injury and you need more than a single conversation's structure.

**When NOT to use:**
- Something has just been disclosed — use `../session-and-lesson/discipleship_hard_conversation_navigation.md`.
- The live topic is everyday conflict and anger rather than an injury already done — use
  `discipleship_module_anger_and_conflict.md`.
- The participant is in or leaving an abusive situation. Route out; a module is not the response.
- You need a church-discipline, restoration-of-a-leader, or membership process. That is polity and
  routes to `domain-biblical-studies/theology-research/biblical_theology_church_government_polity.md`.

**Audience:** Curriculum designers and experienced mentors. A first-time mentor should deliver this
module with supervision.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Where this module sits.** Stage, prerequisites, session count.
2. **What kind of injuries are actually in view.** Ordinary relational wounds, family estrangement,
   betrayal, injuries inside the church community, or harm with a safety dimension. The design differs
   sharply, and assuming the mild case is how this module causes harm.
3. **Whether any participant is likely to be in an ongoing harmful situation.** If unknown, design as
   though yes.
4. **The referral landscape.** What professional and safeguarding help actually exists locally and
   through the program, described rather than named from memory.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground confession practice, absolution, sacramental
   reconciliation, a communal restoration process, or a particular reading of the forgiveness passages.
   Contested specifics stay attributed and alternatives stay visible.
6. **Community history.** Unresolved conflict, a past scandal, or a leadership injury inside the group
   changes what can be safely opened.
7. **Existing material.** Any forgiveness curriculum already in use.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. Do not guess item 2. A module built for
ordinary wounds and delivered to someone carrying abuse will tell them, in effect, that their refusal to
reconcile is a spiritual failure — which is the most damaging thing this module can do.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Hold **three distinct things apart** throughout, and define each before any session:
  forgiveness (what the injured person releases), reconciliation (a restored relationship, requiring
  both parties), and restoration of trust (earned over time through changed behaviour, and never owed).
- State plainly that **reconciliation is not always possible, wise, or safe**, and that declining it can
  be the right and faithful choice.
- Make **safety prior**: the safety screen sits before the content, and a safety concern ends the
  module's involvement and begins a referral.
- Include the participant who is the one **who caused harm**, with content on confession, restitution,
  changed behaviour, and accepting that trust is not owed on demand.
- Treat forgiveness as **a process with a timeline the injured person owns**, not an event to be
  completed by a session's end.
- Address the **unresolvable case**: the person who has died, will not engage, denies the harm, or is
  unsafe to contact.
- Address **self-forgiveness and being forgiven**, which participants raise constantly and modules
  routinely omit.
- Reference Scripture **by address only**, translation named, and note where a passage's application to
  abuse is contested rather than importing it.
- Give the mentor **explicit words** for the moment a participant says they cannot or will not forgive.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Require, urge, schedule, or set a deadline for forgiveness, or treat a participant's timeline as
  disobedience.
- Ask, arrange, or facilitate contact between an injured person and the person who harmed them.
- Present reconciliation as the goal of forgiveness, or restored trust as its evidence.
- Apply a passage about interpersonal conflict between equals to a situation involving abuse, coercion,
  or a power imbalance without flagging that the application is contested and often harmful.
- Frame ongoing pain, anger, or refusal as bitterness, unforgiveness, or a spiritual condition.
- Invent forgiveness research, health outcomes of forgiving, or reconciliation statistics.
- Give clinical guidance on trauma, PTSD, or grief. All route out.
- State any law on abuse, protective orders, reporting, or family matters. Mark `[VERIFY]` and route to
  `domain-legal/`.
- Name a specific counselor, service, agency, or mediation organization.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ — confession to a priest, to a leader, or to one another;
  absolution and its authority; sacramental reconciliation; communal restoration and discipline
  processes; whether forgiveness is conditional on repentance; and how the "seventy times seven" and
  Matthew 18 material is read. Attribute each to identifiable streams; let a declared tradition
  foreground its own practice.
- **Must Not:** present one stream's account of forgiveness — particularly the conditional/unconditional
  question — as the settled biblical position, or use a tradition's restoration process as though it
  were universal church practice.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Define the three things, separately, before anything else

Write plain-language definitions of forgiveness, reconciliation, and restoration of trust, each with an
example and a counter-example. Then write the sentence the module returns to whenever they get confused
— because they will, in nearly every session.

### Step 2 — Put the safety screen first

Design the screen that runs before the module opens: what would make participation harmful, how someone
signals it privately, and what happens then. State that a participant with a live safety concern is
routed out of the module and toward help, not carried through it with adjustments. Destination kinds
`[VERIFY]`, mentor's words, and how the relationship continues.

### Step 3 — Build the injured person's strand

Design content for the person who was harmed: naming the injury accurately, grief, anger as legitimate,
what forgiveness releases and what it does not, the timeline as theirs, and the explicit permission not
to reconcile. Include the unresolvable cases — death, denial, refusal, danger.

### Step 4 — Build the strand for the one who caused harm

Design content for the participant on the other side: honest naming without minimizing or
over-dramatizing, confession, restitution where possible, changed behaviour, and accepting that trust
is earned slowly and may never be restored. State plainly that seeking forgiveness does not entitle
anyone to receive it, and that pressing for it is a further injury.

### Step 5 — Map the contested ground

Build the contested table: forgiveness as conditional on repentance or not; confession's form and
recipient; absolution; communal restoration; whether reconciliation is owed within a church community;
and the disputed application of the standard passages to abuse. Positions, streams, handling.

### Step 6 — Build the sessions

Lay out sessions against the count. For each: the question, Scripture by address, which strand it
serves, the practice, and two mandatory columns — *the pressure this session could create* and *the
wording that prevents it*. Exegesis routes to `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/`.

### Step 7 — Write the mentor's scripts for the hard moments

Write verbatim words for: "I can't forgive them"; "I won't forgive them"; "they say they've changed and
everyone wants me to move on"; "I forgave and I still feel the same"; and "am I sinning by staying
away?" Each script must protect the participant's timeline and their choice.

### Step 8 — Stress-test

Check against the standing failure modes: forgiveness and reconciliation were collapsed; the module
created pressure toward contact; anger was pathologized; a passage was applied to abuse without a flag;
the person who caused harm was centred. Early signal and fix for each.

## Verification

- [ ] Forgiveness, reconciliation, and restoration of trust are defined separately and never used
      interchangeably anywhere in the module.
- [ ] The module states that declining reconciliation can be right and faithful, without qualification.
- [ ] The safety screen precedes the content, and a safety concern routes the participant out.
- [ ] No session, practice, or script urges, schedules, or sets a deadline for forgiveness.
- [ ] Every application of a conflict passage to an abuse situation is flagged as contested and harmful.
- [ ] Both strands exist, and the strand for the person who caused harm states that forgiveness is not
      owed to them.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Use "forgiveness" and "reconciliation" in the same breath as though the second follows the first. That
  single conflation causes more harm than every other error in this module combined.
- Treat a participant's continuing anger as evidence they have not forgiven. Anger at a real injury is
  accurate, and pathologizing it teaches people to hide it.
- Import Matthew 18 into an abuse situation. The passage is about conflict between people with
  comparable standing; applying it where there is coercion has produced documented harm, and the
  application is contested precisely for that reason.
- Facilitate a meeting. Mediation between an injured person and the person who harmed them is
  specialist work, and a mentor who attempts it is out of their role.
- Design the module around the person seeking forgiveness. They are usually the one asking for the
  module, and centring them turns the whole thing into pressure on the injured person.
- Promise that forgiveness will produce peace, health, or freedom by a particular point. It sets people
  up to conclude they did it wrong.

✅ **DO:**
- Give the module a single returning sentence that re-separates the three things, and use it every time
  they blur.
- Run the safety screen before anything opens, and make routing out the response — not a modified
  version of the module.
- Write both strands, and put in Strand B the sentence that seeking forgiveness does not entitle anyone
  to receive it.
- Cover the unresolvable cases explicitly; most participants are in one of them.
- Rehearse the five hard-moment scripts. The gap between knowing to protect someone's timeline and
  being able to say it out loud is where the pressure enters.
- Include self-forgiveness and being forgiven. Participants ask, and a module that omits them sends
  people to worse sources.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective names the separation of three commonly collapsed
  concepts as the module's central job, so a design that blurs them has visibly failed its brief.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** definitions and the safety screen precede all content,
  which is the only ordering that prevents the module from being built around the person seeking
  forgiveness.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the absolutes — no urging, no
  scheduling, no facilitated contact, no unflagged application of conflict passages to abuse.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** two strands crossed with the three concepts and the
  unresolvable cases covers positions a single-strand module structurally cannot reach.
- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** Scripture by address with a named translation, no invented
  forgiveness research or health claims, and every service and legal point marked `[VERIFY]`.
