# Participant Onboarding Design

**Objective:** Design what a discipleship pairing program asks each side at intake — what a person
seeking discipleship is actually asking for, what a prospective mentor is offering — collecting only
what a real matching or screening decision needs, with consent and confidentiality stated before any
question invites disclosure.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Intake is not assessment of anyone's faith, and it is not a screening
> interview about someone's problems. A seeker owes no account of their spiritual state or their
> struggles to be admitted. Where intake surfaces distress, crisis, or a safeguarding concern, it routes
> immediately rather than being recorded and processed
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. Data-protection and consent
> requirements are `[VERIFY]` with qualified counsel via `domain-legal/`.

**When to use:** Designing or fixing the intake for a pairing program — particularly when the form has
grown long, when matching decisions are being made on guesses, or when people are dropping out at
signup.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are designing how matches are made — use
  `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_pairing_criteria_design.md` first; onboarding collects what
  it needs.
- You are assessing a specific mentor's readiness — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_readiness_assessment.md`.
- You want a personal self-reflection tool — use
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_growth_self_assessment.md`.
- You are designing the whole program — use `discipleship_program_design_blueprint.md`.

**Audience:** Program leads and platform designers building the intake for both sides.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The matching criteria.** What matching actually uses — every intake question must map to one of
   these or to screening. Questions that map to neither are removed.
2. **The screening requirements.** From the safeguarding policy, for the mentor side.
3. **How intake happens.** Form, conversation, or both — and who sees the responses.
4. **Who participates.** Whether minors or vulnerable adults are involved, which changes consent
   substantially and adds requirements not stated here.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May add intake elements — membership standing, a reference from a
   leader, doctrinal affirmation — applied as that stream's requirement.
6. **Current drop-off.** Where people abandon the existing intake, which is usually the finding.
7. **Platform constraints.** What can be collected, stored, and retrieved.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask clarifying questions before proceeding. If the matching
criteria do not exist yet, stop — intake designed before criteria collects what seems interesting rather
than what is needed, and it is always too long.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- **Justify every question** by the specific decision it informs — matching, screening, or safety.
  Questions justified by none are removed, and the output says which were removed.
- Place **consent and confidentiality before any question inviting disclosure**: who sees this, how long
  it is kept `[VERIFY]`, and what happens to it if no match is made.
- Ask the seeker **what they are asking for** in their own words, rather than requiring them to
  self-categorize into program terminology.
- Ask the mentor **what they are actually offering** — capacity, experience, and what they are not
  equipped for — rather than only what they are willing to do.
- Include an explicit **"what I'd rather not be matched with"** on both sides, since it prevents more
  mismatches than any positive preference.
- Keep both forms **short enough to complete in one sitting**, and state the estimated time honestly.
- Make **optional questions visibly optional**, and require nothing that is not needed for a decision.
- Say what happens **if no match is available**, at the point of intake rather than in silence
  afterwards.

### Must Not

- Ask for a testimony, conversion account, spiritual history, or account of struggles as a condition of
  entry.
- Collect information the program cannot act on, does not store securely, or does not need.
- Ask about mental health, medication, trauma, abuse history, sexual history, or substance use.
- State a data-protection, consent, retention, or minors requirement — `[VERIFY]` and route.
- Require a seeker to demonstrate readiness, commitment, or spiritual maturity to be accepted.
- Quote Scripture text from memory.
- Invent intake benchmarks, completion-rate research, or form-design statistics.
- Design a form that takes more than the stated time, or that asks the seeker more than the mentor.
- Leave someone who completed intake without communication.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** where a tradition requires membership standing, a leader's reference, or doctrinal
  affirmation at intake, include it as that stream's requirement, label it, and note others do not
  require it.
- **Must Not:** ask doctrinal-position questions as a general screening device, or make a
  tradition-specific affirmation a condition of receiving discipleship where the program does not
  actually require it.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Derive from the criteria

List every matching criterion and screening requirement. Each becomes at most one intake question. Then
list any question the current form asks that maps to neither — those are removed, and the output names
them.

### Step 2 — Write the consent block

Before any question: who sees this, what it is used for, how long it is kept `[VERIFY]`, what happens if
no match is made, and how to withdraw. In plain language, at the top, not in a linked policy.

### Step 3 — Design the seeker side

Ask in their own words what they are looking for. Cover availability, channel, language and access
constraints, and what they would rather not be matched with. Do not ask them to summarize their
spiritual state or their problems.

### Step 4 — Design the mentor side

Ask capacity honestly — how many, for how long, from when. Ask what they have walked through that might
be relevant, what they are not equipped for, and what they would rather not be matched with. Attach
screening separately, since screening is not matching.

### Step 5 — Mark what is optional and time it

Make every non-decision question optional and visibly so. Time both forms by reading them aloud and
state the honest estimate. If the seeker's form is longer than the mentor's, rebalance — the asymmetry
signals who the program thinks is being evaluated.

### Step 6 — Write the no-match and waiting communication

What the person is told at intake about timing, what they hear if no match is available, and when. This
is written now so that silence is never the default.

### Step 7 — Check the design

Run four checks: **every question maps to a decision**, **consent precedes disclosure**, **nothing
clinical or intrusive is asked**, and **the seeker's form is not longer than the mentor's**.

- [ ] Every question maps to matching, screening, or safety; removed questions are listed.
- [ ] Consent and confidentiality appear before the first question, in plain language.
- [ ] No question asks about mental health, medication, trauma, abuse, sexual history, or substance use.
- [ ] Both sides have a "what I'd rather not be matched with" question.
- [ ] The seeker's form is no longer than the mentor's.
- [ ] The no-match communication is written, with timing.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Ask for a testimony at intake. It reads as a test of whether someone qualifies for help, and it is
  the fastest way to lose exactly the people the program exists for.
- Collect what is merely interesting. Every additional question costs completions, and unused data is a
  liability rather than an asset.
- Ask what someone is struggling with. That belongs in the relationship, offered freely, not in a form
  read by an administrator.
- Make the seeker's form longer than the mentor's. It tells the seeker they are the one being assessed.
- Ask only what mentors are *willing* to do. Willingness runs far ahead of capacity, and the gap is
  where over-commitment starts.
- Leave people who completed intake in silence. Most programs' worst experience is the unanswered
  application.

✅ **DO:**
- Derive every question from a criterion, and publish the removed list so the form does not silently
  regrow.
- Put consent at the top in plain words, not behind a link. It is read there and nowhere else.
- Ask both sides what they would rather *not* be matched with — it is the single most useful matching
  input and it is almost always omitted.
- Ask mentors what they are not equipped for, and treat the answer as a safety input as much as a
  matching one.
- Time the forms by reading them aloud, and state the estimate honestly on the form.
- Write the no-match message before launch, so nobody has to improvise it under the discomfort of
  having nothing to offer.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** questions are derived from matching criteria before
  any form is drafted, which is what prevents the accumulating intake form that collects what seems
  interesting.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** intake is designed across matching, screening,
  safety, consent, and completion-cost axes — the last two being what most program intake forms ignore
  entirely.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the no-testimony, no-clinical-questions, and
  seeker-form-not-longer rules are hard constraints against the ways intake signals that the person
  seeking help is the one on trial.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the maps-to column on every question makes an unjustified
  question structurally visible, and the removed-questions table keeps the form from regrowing.
- **NE-14 (Multi-Audience Documentation Targeting):** two distinct intakes are designed for two
  audiences with different needs, with an explicit balance check between them.
