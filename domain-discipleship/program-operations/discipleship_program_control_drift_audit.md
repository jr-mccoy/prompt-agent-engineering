# Program Control Drift Audit

**Objective:** Audit a discipleship program's own design artifacts for the four mechanisms by which
formation quietly becomes control — the cost of leaving, what participants are not told, what happens to
someone who disagrees, and whether anyone can hold the leadership to anything — and produce specific
changes to specific documents, without assessing any person, any leader's motives, or whether the church
as a whole is healthy.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This audits **artifacts you own** — your covenant, your onboarding, your
> expectations, your structures — and it produces design changes. It does not assess a person, a leader's
> character or intent, a congregation, or a church's theology, and it does not decide whether a group is
> high-control. If the live question is *"is the group I am in controlling me"*, that is a personal
> assessment and belongs at
> `domain-psy-ops/personal-defense/psyops_high_control_group_dynamics_assessment.md`, which works on
> structure rather than belief and is more rigorous at it than this domain could be. Where the audit
> surfaces something a person has actually been harmed by, that stops being an audit finding and becomes
> a safeguarding matter `[VERIFY: identify the correct authority from an official source]`.

**When to use:** Annually, before scaling, after a wave of quiet departures, when a participant says
something that lands uncomfortably, or whenever a leader wants to check that the sixth of this domain's
load-bearing conventions — no spiritual coercion — actually holds in the documents rather than in the
intentions.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want to know whether the program is working — use `discipleship_program_health_review.md`, which
  covers stalled pairs, attrition, and load, and deliberately does not audit for coercion.
- You are asking whether a group you belong to is high-control — that is `domain-psy-ops/`, above, and it
  is a different question with a different method.
- Someone has been harmed — use `../after-harm/`, and report before auditing.
- You are evaluating third-party material rather than your own structures — use
  `../curriculum-architecture/discipleship_material_evaluation.md`.
- The question is church governance, discipline, or polity — out of scope for this domain; see
  `domain-biblical-studies/theology-research/biblical_theology_church_government_polity.md`.

**Audience:** The program lead, the leadership team, or a trustee doing an independent read. Best run by
someone who did not write the artifacts.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The actual artifacts, as participants receive them.** Covenant, onboarding material, expectations,
   cadence, accountability structures, multiplication asks, any commitment anyone signs. The documents as
   given, not as described — the gap between the two is frequently the finding.
2. **What is expected of a participant beyond meeting.** Attendance elsewhere, giving, service,
   multiplication, disclosure, availability. Written and unwritten both, since the unwritten expectations
   are the ones that cannot be declined.
3. **How someone leaves, in practice.** What they do, who they tell, what changes afterwards socially and
   in the community, and whether anyone has actually left recently and what happened.
4. **Who holds the leadership accountable, and how a participant would raise something about them.** Named
   and real. "The elders" is only an answer if a participant could actually reach them.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May legitimately include submission to leaders, church discipline, a
   membership covenant with obligations, confession, and accountability structures — and this audit is not
   a judgement on any of them. Where a tradition holds such practices, the audit tests whether they are
   **disclosed in advance, applied consistently, and appealable** rather than whether they should exist.
   Where none is declared, the audit stays governance-general.
6. **Exit data.** Who has left over two years and what, if anything, was said. Usually the most informative
   input and the least often collected.
7. **What participants have said**, including offhand remarks that were not treated as feedback.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask for the documents as issued. If the artifacts cannot be produced
because they are not written down, that is the first finding: an unwritten expectation cannot be declined,
appealed, or pointed at, and writing them is the first change.

## Constraints

### Must

- Audit **artifacts and structures only**, and phrase every finding as a property of a document or a
  process rather than of a person.
- Work the **four mechanisms explicitly**: exit cost, information asymmetry, penalty for dissent, and
  unaccountable authority — each against named artifacts.
- For every finding, produce **the specific change to the specific document**, with wording where wording
  is the problem.
- Test whether every expectation is **disclosed before commitment**, since the same requirement is
  legitimate when stated in advance and coercive when it arrives afterwards.
- Test whether **declining is genuinely available**: what happens socially, relationally, and in the
  community to someone who says no to multiplication, to disclosure, to more.
- Test the **appeal route**, including for a decision made by the leader, and count "there is none" as a
  finding rather than an observation.
- Distinguish **a tradition's disclosed practice from drift**: a stated obligation, applied consistently
  and appealable, is not a finding; the same obligation unstated, applied selectively, or unappealable is.
- Include **what the audit cannot see** — that documents can be fine while practice is not, that people
  rarely tell a leader the truth about exit cost, and what would have to be asked of whom to find out.
- Route anything that surfaces as **actual harm** out of the audit and into safeguarding, immediately.

### Must Not

- Assess, characterize, or attribute motive to any leader, participant, or the church.
- Conclude that the program, group, or church is high-control, healthy, safe, or abusive — this audit
  produces changes, never a verdict.
- Produce a score, index, rating, tier, or percentage for the program, for coercion, or for anything else.
- Adjudicate whether a tradition's practice of submission, membership obligation, church discipline, or
  confession is itself legitimate.
- Treat commitment, expectation, structure, or accountability as coercive in themselves — a program with
  no expectations forms nobody, and an audit that concludes otherwise is useless.
- Become a personal assessment tool for participants, or be given to participants as a way to evaluate
  their own leaders — that is `domain-psy-ops/` and it belongs there.
- Name any agency, service, or authority; state any legal, employment, or safeguarding requirement —
  `[VERIFY]` and route.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or use any passage to justify or to condemn an authority structure.
- Recommend disciplinary action, removal, or any consequence for a person.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** name that traditions differ substantially and legitimately on submission to leaders, membership
  covenants and their obligations, church discipline and its process, confession, tithing expectations, and
  the authority of an elder or overseer — and audit each for **disclosure in advance, consistency of
  application, and the existence of an appeal**, which are the questions that apply across all of them.
- **Must Not:** treat any tradition's authority structure as inherently coercive or inherently safe, rule
  on whether a practice should exist, or allow a tradition's teaching on unity, submission, or honouring
  leaders to be used as the reason a finding cannot be raised.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Read the artifacts as a participant receives them

Work from the documents as issued, in the order a participant meets them. Where a practice exists but is
not written anywhere, record it as unwritten — that is a finding in itself, because an expectation nobody
can point at is one nobody can decline.

### Step 2 — Audit exit cost

For each way of leaving — leaving a pair, leaving the program, leaving the church — establish what it
actually costs: what is said, what changes socially, whether a reason is required, whether anyone follows
up and how many times, and what happened to the last person who did it. Cost is measured in what happens
afterwards, not in whether the document says people may leave.

### Step 3 — Audit information asymmetry

List everything a participant only discovers after committing: expectations, obligations, what is shared
with leaders, what their mentor may pass on, what membership or continuation depends on. Every item on that
list is a change to onboarding, and the standing test is whether the same requirement stated in advance
would have changed anyone's decision.

### Step 4 — Audit the penalty for dissent

Establish what happens to someone who disagrees theologically, declines to multiply, declines to disclose,
or criticizes the program: what changes in their standing, their role, their invitations, their pairing.
Then ask the sharper version — has anyone disagreed recently, and if nobody has, what does that indicate.

### Step 5 — Audit accountability upward

Trace how a participant raises something about the leader, whether that route reaches anyone outside the
leader's influence, whether it has ever been used, and what happened. Then check whether the leadership is
subject to the same conduct expectations as the mentors are.

### Step 6 — Separate disclosed practice from drift

For each finding, ask three questions: is it disclosed before commitment, is it applied consistently, is it
appealable. A tradition's stated obligation that passes all three is not a finding. The same obligation
failing any of them is, and the change is to the failing property rather than to the practice.

### Step 7 — Write the changes, state the blind spots, and check

Produce document-by-document changes with wording. Then write what the audit cannot see and what would have
to be asked of whom — including that the people best placed to answer about exit cost have already left.
Then check: is any finding about a person? Is there a verdict anywhere? Is any legitimate disclosed practice
recorded as a fault?

## Verification

- [ ] Every finding is a property of a document or a process, never of a person.
- [ ] All four mechanisms are audited against named artifacts.
- [ ] Each finding carries a specific change to a specific document.
- [ ] Disclosed, consistent, appealable practices are recorded as fine rather than as findings.
- [ ] No score, rating, or overall verdict on the program appears anywhere.
- [ ] A blind-spot section states what the audit cannot see and who would have to be asked.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Conclude that the program is or is not high-control. That is a verdict, it is not this audit's to reach,
  and it will be quoted long after the changes have been forgotten.
- Treat expectation as coercion. A program that asks nothing forms nobody, and an audit that flags
  commitment itself gives every real finding an easy answer.
- Flag a tradition's disclosed practice as a fault. A membership covenant with stated obligations, applied
  consistently and appealable, passes; the audit's business is disclosure, consistency, and appeal, not
  the practice.
- Make it about the leader. The moment a finding names a person's character, the audit becomes a
  disciplinary document and the changes stop being implementable.
- Accept "people can leave any time" from the document. Exit cost lives in what happens afterwards, and the
  document is the last place it appears.
- Treat an absence of dissent as a good sign. It is the finding, and it needs the question asked directly.
- Skip the audit because relationships are good. Drift arrives one reasonable-sounding expectation at a
  time, and every one of them is added by someone with good intentions.

✅ **DO:**
- Work from the artifacts as participants actually receive them, in the order they meet them.
- Test every expectation against "was this disclosed before they committed", which resolves most cases
  without any judgement about the practice.
- Measure exit cost by what happened to the last person who left.
- Give every finding a document and a change, with wording where the wording is the problem.
- Ask whether the leadership is under the same conduct expectations as the mentors, and record the answer.
- Say what the audit cannot see, and name who would have to ask — because the people with the clearest view
  of exit cost are no longer there to be asked by the leader.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective fixes the deliverable as document changes and
  explicitly excludes a verdict, which is what prevents the audit from producing the one output it must
  never produce.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** exit cost, information asymmetry, penalty for dissent,
  and upward accountability are four independent axes, so a program can pass three and still be told
  precisely where it fails.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block separates this audit from the personal
  assessment it resembles — no verdict, no person, no score, no adjudication of a tradition's practice.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the disclosed / consistent / appealable table resolves the
  hardest judgement in the audit into three columns, which is what keeps legitimate tradition practice from
  being logged as coercion.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the final check scans for the finding that names a person and the verdict
  that crept into the summary — the two failures that make an otherwise useful audit unusable.
