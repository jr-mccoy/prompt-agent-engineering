# College and Young Adult Track

**Objective:** Design a discipleship track for the years between leaving home and settling — built on
the assumption that participants will move within three or four years, that they now make their own
decisions and did not before, that their hard questions are genuine intellectual work rather than a
crisis to be contained, and that the track's real success measure is whether formation survives the
handoff to wherever they go next.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs formation, not care or advice. Mental-health concerns, disordered
> eating, substance use, sexual assault, financial crisis, and immigration or visa jeopardy all route to
> licensed professionals and appropriate services `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an
> official source]`. A mentor in this life stage is often the most available adult in a young person's
> life, which makes over-reach easy and referral more important, not less. Career, academic, and
> immigration decisions belong to the participant.

**When to use:** You are building a track for students, apprentices, early-career adults, or anyone in
the first years of independent life, where turnover is high and questions are live.

**When NOT to use:**
- The participants are minors — use `discipleship_track_youth_and_teen.md`, which is safeguarding-gated.
- You are designing the whole campus program, with its access rules and institutional constraints — use
  `../context-variants/discipleship_context_campus_ministry.md`.
- You need a Bible study for this age group — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/ministry-contexts/biblical_ministry_college_young_adult_study.md`.
- A participant's questions have become genuine distress rather than inquiry. Care and referral first.

**Audience:** Campus workers, young-adult ministry leads, curriculum designers, and mentors working with
this age group.

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The actual population.** Students, working, apprenticed, unemployed, or mixed — and whether they
   are local, domestic movers, or international. These groups have almost nothing in common
   practically.
2. **The expected residence window.** How long a typical participant will be present. Everything about
   the track's shape follows from this number, and designing a five-year pathway for a three-year window
   guarantees nobody finishes.
3. **What they are arriving from.** Church-raised and now free, newly interested, returning, or
   arriving from another country and culture entirely.
4. **The pressures actually in play.** Money, work hours, academic load, isolation, housing insecurity,
   visa conditions, or family expectation from a distance.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground confirmation, membership, a campus fellowship's
   distinctives, or specific practices. Contested specifics stay attributed.
6. **Institutional constraints.** Where a campus or employer governs access, publicity, or meetings.
7. **Existing material or fellowship structure.** Anything already running that this track sits inside.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding — particularly item 2. A track without a
stated residence window will be designed at the wrong length, and the failure only becomes visible when
a cohort leaves halfway through.

## Constraints

### Must

- Design the track to **complete within the residence window**, with a defined finished state, or to be
  explicitly modular so an incomplete pass still delivered something whole.
- Build the **handoff** as a first-class deliverable: what a participant takes with them, how they find
  a community where they are going, and who — if anyone — stays in contact.
- Treat **hard questions and deconstruction as intellectual work**: real reading, real answers where
  they exist, honest "this is disputed" where they do not, and no framing of questioning as drift.
- Respect **newly held autonomy**: participants now decide, and the track must never recruit their
  guardians, employers, or peers into pressure.
- Design for **irregular attendance** — shift work, exam periods, placements — with a way back in that
  costs nothing.
- Include **belonging and loneliness** as formation territory. For most participants this is the
  presenting need, and the track that ignores it loses them regardless of its content.
- Include the **international or culturally distant participant**, whose questions, practices, and family
  situation differ substantially and who is often the most isolated person present.
- Build **peer discipling** into the design, because this population is disproportionately capable of it
  and because the mentor supply will not scale with the turnover.
- Route mental health, assault, substance use, financial crisis, and visa jeopardy **out** `[VERIFY]`.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Treat doubt, deconstruction, or leaving as a failure of the participant or the track, or design
  retention mechanics to prevent it.
- Use belonging as leverage — making community, roles, or friendship conditional on attendance,
  agreement, or a decision.
- Pressure participants on career, relationship, or academic decisions, or present a life choice as a
  matter of obedience with one right answer.
- Invent retention statistics, faith-and-university research, or generational claims about young adults.
- Give clinical, financial, immigration, or legal guidance. All route out.
- Design activities that would jeopardize a participant's visa, academic standing, or employment.
- Assume family support, financial slack, or discretionary time.
- Name a specific counselor, service, campus body, or organization.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on membership and its timing at this age, on confirmation, on
  the authority of a campus fellowship relative to a local congregation, on how doubt is pastorally
  handled, and on the relationship between a young adult's independence and their home church.
  Attribute positions to identifiable streams; let a declared tradition foreground its own.
- **Must Not:** present one stream's handling of doubt as the biblical one, or design the track so that
  a participant's home tradition is implicitly corrected by the fellowship's.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Fix the window and design backwards from the exit

State the residence window. Then design the exit first: what a participant leaves with, where they are
in their formation, and what they can do. Only then work backwards to the entry. Tracks designed
forwards from arrival reliably run past the point at which everyone leaves.

### Step 2 — Make the track survive irregular attendance

Decide the structure: modular units each of which stands alone, or a sequence with defined re-entry
points. State how someone returns after six weeks away — because they will — and make the return
uncostly and unremarked.

### Step 3 — Build the questions strand properly

Design the strand for doubt and hard questions: what is genuinely disputed and among whom, what has
answers and what the honest answers are, and what the track does not know. Route the exegetical and
apologetic work to `domain-biblical-studies/`. State plainly that a participant may work through this
strand and conclude differently, and that they remain welcome.

### Step 4 — Build belonging deliberately

Design the relational structure — who a participant knows, how, and how quickly — as content rather than
as atmosphere. Include the international and culturally distant participant explicitly, and the person
who arrives knowing nobody in week nine rather than week one.

### Step 5 — Design the peer layer

Specify how participants disciple each other: what they are equipped to do, what they are explicitly not
(counselors, authorities, or monitors), the training they receive, and the referral route they use.
Route the reproduction design to `../curriculum-architecture/discipleship_multiplication_design.md`.

### Step 6 — Build the sessions

Lay out modules and sessions against the window. For each: the question, Scripture by address, the
practice, whether it stands alone — and *what this session assumes about the participant's time, money,
or family*, so the assumptions are visible and can be corrected.

### Step 7 — Design the handoff

Build the leaving process: a conversation before they go, what they carry, how they look for a community
where they are going, what the track will and will not do afterwards, and an honest statement that
contact usually fades and that this is not betrayal. Where a tradition has a transfer or commendation
practice, name it as that tradition's.

### Step 8 — Stress-test

Check against the standing failure modes: the track was longer than the window; belonging became
leverage; doubt was contained rather than engaged; the international participant was an afterthought;
the handoff did not exist. Early signal and fix.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Design a pathway longer than the years people stay. It is the single most common design error in this
  life stage, and the cost is that nobody ever finishes anything.
- Treat questioning as a retention problem. Participants can tell when a conversation is really about
  keeping them, and it is the fastest way to lose the thoughtful ones.
- Make friendship contingent. This population is often lonely enough to comply, which is exactly why
  using belonging as leverage is a serious abuse of the position.
- Assume the academic calendar. Apprentices, shift workers, and the unemployed are in this population
  too, and a track built on term dates excludes them silently.
- Leave the international participant to find their own way in. They are frequently the most isolated
  person present and the least likely to say so.
- Build the whole thing around a mentor supply that cannot match the turnover. The peer layer is not a
  compromise here; it is the structure that fits the population.

✅ **DO:**
- Design from the exit backwards, and make the handoff a real conversation with real content.
- Build modules that stand alone, so a term away does not end someone's participation.
- Put the "may conclude differently and remain welcome" sentence into the track document itself.
- State each session's assumptions about time and money, then check them against the actual population.
- Equip the peer layer properly — including what peers are explicitly not — and give them a referral
  route they can use without shame.
- Say out loud that contact usually fades after someone moves, so nobody experiences an ordinary
  transition as abandonment.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective makes surviving the handoff the success measure,
  which reframes the track from a program to be completed into formation that must travel.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the window is fixed and the exit designed before the
  entry, which is the only ordering that prevents the standard over-length design error.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** population, window, pressures, and belonging are
  worked as separate axes, so a track built for full-time students does not get delivered to shift
  workers by default.
- **ED-01 (Iterative Scaffolding):** modules are designed to stand alone while still building, which is
  what makes the track survive irregular attendance without losing progression.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** the questions strand is required to distinguish the genuinely
  disputed from the answerable and to state what the track does not know.
