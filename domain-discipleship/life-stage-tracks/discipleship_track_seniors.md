# Seniors Track

**Objective:** Design a discipleship track for older adults built on the assumption that they are still
being formed and still have something to give — designing honestly around accumulated loss, changing
capacity, and dependency without condescension, making accessibility structural rather than
accommodating, and holding an elder-abuse and isolation screen that does not turn pastoral attention
into surveillance.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This designs formation, not care. Cognitive decline, depression, grief that is
> not resolving, medication questions, mobility and falls, and end-of-life decisions route to qualified
> professionals `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. Elder abuse —
> physical, emotional, financial, or neglect — and coercion by a family member or carer route
> immediately to the safeguarding lead and appropriate authorities. A mentor does not assess capacity,
> witness documents, take financial responsibility, or accept gifts, bequests, or access to accounts,
> and the track states this plainly.

**When to use:** You are building a track for older adults and want it to be discipleship rather than a
social program with a devotional attached.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want a Bible study for older adults — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/ministry-contexts/biblical_ministry_seniors_study_designer.md`.
- The presenting need is a specific bereavement — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/ministry-contexts/biblical_ministry_grief_and_loss_scripture_guide.md`.
- A participant's capacity has changed such that consent is in question. That is a care and safeguarding
  matter first, and it routes out.
- The real constraint is access, transport, or health limiting participation in any pathway — start with
  `../learner-pathways/discipleship_life_constraints_adaptation.md`.

**Audience:** Program leads, curriculum designers, and mentors — including peer mentors of the same
generation, who are frequently the right people and are routinely overlooked.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **Who the participants are.** Age range, health and mobility spread, living situation (own home,
   with family, sheltered, residential care), and whether some participants are themselves carers —
   often for a spouse.
2. **Access reality.** Transport, hearing, vision, fatigue, cognitive load, digital access and comfort,
   and whether meetings can be reached at all in winter or in the evening.
3. **What this community currently offers older adults.** Whether they are treated as recipients of
   ministry, as volunteers to be deployed, or as disciples — and what the track is correcting.
4. **The referral landscape.** Local and program-level help for health, bereavement, safeguarding, and
   social care — described rather than named from memory.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground last rites and anointing, the communion of saints,
   prayer for the dying, a particular practice around confession, or specific end-of-life pastoral
   patterns. Contested specifics stay attributed.
6. **Bereavement load in the group.** Recent and accumulated losses change what can be opened and when.
7. **Existing material.** Anything already running for this group.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. Do not guess item 2 — access, not interest,
is the usual reason older adults are absent, and a track that treats absence as disengagement is
diagnosing the wrong problem.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Design the track as **formation, not occupation**: participants are still being formed, and the track
  must have real content rather than company with a devotional attached.
- Design for **giving as well as receiving**: what participants have to offer — discipling others,
  praying, teaching, remembering — is content, not a volunteering ask.
- Make **accessibility structural**: print size, audio, pace, session length, seating, transport,
  hearing loops, and a non-digital route that is equal rather than lesser.
- Treat **accumulated loss** as central formation territory — bereavement, capacity, role, home,
  independence, and the loss of peers — without rushing to resolution.
- Include **dependency** honestly: receiving help, losing autonomy, and being cared for by one's own
  children, which participants find harder than most curricula admit.
- Include **the carer**, who is often the person in the room with the least discretionary time and the
  most invisible need.
- Hold an **elder-abuse and isolation screen** that is proportionate and does not become monitoring:
  what would be noticed, who it goes to, and the participant's own agency in it.
- State the **mentor's financial and legal limits** in the track: no gifts, no bequests, no account
  access, no witnessing of documents, no involvement in financial decisions.
- Design for **cognitive change** with dignity: what changes, what the track does, and how participation
  continues rather than ending.
- Reference Scripture **by address only**, translation named.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Treat older participants as a ministry object, a legacy-preservation project, or a volunteer pool.
- Use "finishing well" language in a way that implies the participant's formation is concluded or that
  their remaining task is to hand things over.
- Assume digital access, or make the non-digital route a degraded version.
- Assess cognitive capacity, mental state, or fitness to decide, or advise on care arrangements.
- Give clinical guidance on dementia, depression, medication, falls, or end-of-life care. All route out.
- Involve a mentor in wills, powers of attorney, property, gifts, or financial decisions of any kind.
- Invent statistics about ageing, faith in later life, or cognitive decline.
- State law on capacity, safeguarding of adults, power of attorney, or inheritance. Mark `[VERIFY]` and
  route to `domain-legal/` and qualified counsel.
- Name a specific service, care provider, charity, or organization.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on anointing and last rites, on prayer for the dead and the
  communion of saints, on confession near the end of life, on healing expectation in illness, and on
  what is understood to happen at death. Attribute positions to identifiable streams; let a declared
  tradition foreground its own practice.
- **Must Not:** present one tradition's end-of-life practice as universal, or introduce a practice a
  participant's own tradition does not hold at a moment when they are least able to decline it.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Fix the frame

State the track's premise in the participants' hands: you are still being formed, and you still have
something to give. Then name what the track is correcting — whether this community has treated older
adults as recipients, as volunteers, or as background.

### Step 2 — Design access before content

Work every access dimension: session length and time of day, transport, print and audio, hearing,
seating, pace, and the non-digital route. State how a participant who cannot leave home participates
fully — not partially. Access decisions made after content are always compromises; made first they are
design.

### Step 3 — Work the five territories

| Territory | The formation question under it |
|---|---|
| Loss | What have I lost, and what does faith look like carrying it? |
| Dependency | What is happening in me as I need more help than I give? |
| Memory | What has God actually done, and what do I do with knowing it? |
| Giving | What is mine to give now, and to whom? |
| The end | What do I believe about dying, and what have I never said out loud? |

For each: Scripture by address, the distortion common here, and the practice.

### Step 4 — Design the giving strand as real work

Specify what participants disciple, teach, pray, or remember — with training, structure, and a real
recipient. Route reproduction design to `../curriculum-architecture/discipleship_multiplication_design.md`.
Peer discipling within the generation belongs here and is usually the strongest option available.

### Step 5 — Build the carer strand

Design for the participant caring for a spouse or a family member: what they can attend, what they
cannot, what a five-minute practice looks like, and what the track offers when they can only receive.
State plainly that respite and support route out `[VERIFY]`.

### Step 6 — Build the screen without building surveillance

Design the elder-abuse and isolation screen: what would be noticed (financial control, restricted
contact, unexplained change, a family member answering for them), who it goes to, and how the
participant's own agency is preserved throughout. Write the mentor's limits — gifts, bequests, accounts,
documents — into the same section.

### Step 7 — Build the sessions

Lay out sessions. For each: the question, Scripture by address, territory, practice — and two mandatory
columns: *the access requirement of this session*, and *how this session lands on someone recently
bereaved*, with the opt-out.

### Step 8 — Design for cognitive change and stress-test

State what the track does when a participant's cognition changes: how participation continues, what is
simplified, who is consulted, and how dignity is held. Then check against the standing failure modes:
the track became a social program; participants were treated as recipients only; access was an
afterthought; the carer was invisible; care became surveillance.

## Verification

- [ ] Access is designed before content, and the at-home route is full participation rather than partial.
- [ ] The giving strand has real recipients, training, and structure — and is not a volunteering ask.
- [ ] The carer strand exists, with a practice that fits a person with almost no discretionary time.
- [ ] The mentor's financial and legal limits are stated in the track itself.
- [ ] The screen names what would be noticed and preserves the participant's agency throughout.
- [ ] No capacity assessment, clinical guidance, or statement of law appears anywhere.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Design a social program and call it discipleship. Participants know the difference, and the ones who
  want formation quietly stop coming.
- Use "finishing well" as the organizing idea. It frames the participant's remaining years as a tidy-up,
  and it is heard exactly that way.
- Treat the at-home participant as receiving a reduced version. Design the at-home route as the full
  route and the in-person elements as additions.
- Deploy participants as volunteers and call it giving. The distinction is whether the giving is formed,
  supported, and chosen, or whether a rota needed filling.
- Let pastoral attention become monitoring. An older adult whose contacts all report on them has lost
  something real, and the screen must be designed to avoid producing that.
- Let a mentor near a will, a power of attorney, a gift, or a bank account. The limit is absolute and
  belongs in the track document where everyone can see it.

✅ **DO:**
- Put the frame — still being formed, still giving — in the participants' hands in writing.
- Settle every access dimension before a single session is designed.
- Build the giving strand with peer discipling inside the generation as the first option, since it is
  usually the strongest and is habitually overlooked.
- Include dependency as its own territory. It is frequently the hardest thing in the room and almost
  never on the syllabus.
- Design what happens when cognition changes, in advance, so participation continues with dignity rather
  than ending in embarrassment.
- State the mentor's financial limits in the track, not only in a policy document.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective fixes formation-and-giving as the deliverable,
  ruling out the social-program-with-a-devotional that this track is usually mistaken for.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** access is designed before content, which is the only
  ordering that produces genuine access rather than accommodation bolted on afterwards.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** five territories including dependency and the end,
  crossed with the carer strand, reach the material that seniors' ministry most often avoids.
- **RP-02 (Audience Framing):** the track is written to people still being formed rather than to
  recipients of care, and the frame is stated in the participants' own hands.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the access design, the giving strand's reality, the mentor's financial
  limits, and the agency-preserving screen are each verified before the track runs.
