# Cross-Cultural Discipling

**Objective:** Design a discipling relationship where mentor and disciple do not share a culture or a
first language — built entirely on what this specific person says about their own world rather than on
any generalization about their nationality, ethnicity, region, or religious background, with the
mentor's own culture surfaced as a culture rather than as the neutral default, and with the power the
arrangement carries stated plainly.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This prompt **refuses to generate or accept generalizations about how people
> of a nationality, ethnicity, region, or religious background think, believe, or behave.** Such claims
> are unreliable, they flatten the person in front of you, and acting on them does harm that the mentor
> will not see. Everything this design uses about the disciple's culture comes from the disciple. Where
> immigration status, asylum process, family safety, or legal jeopardy is in play, those route to
> qualified advice and specialist services `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official
> source]` — a mentor's guess in these areas can cost someone their status or their safety.

**When to use:** You are about to disciple, or are already discipling, someone whose culture or first
language you do not share — in a diaspora congregation, a migrant community, an international setting,
or a mixed local church.

**When NOT to use:**
- The primary difficulty is physical distance and time zones — use
  `../context-variants/discipleship_context_remote_and_diaspora.md`.
- The question is how a passage's own ancient cultural setting works — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/biblical_historical_cultural_context.md`.
- The disciple's learning is primarily oral rather than text-based — use
  `discipleship_oral_preference_learners.md`, which this prompt often pairs with.
- The problem is the material itself in translation — use
  `discipleship_translated_material_pitfalls.md`.

**Audience:** Mentors, program leads, and pairing coordinators working across cultural and linguistic
difference.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What the disciple has said about their own context.** Not their nationality — what *they* have
   said: about family, obligation, authority, privacy, directness, time, hospitality, shame and honour
   if they raise it, and what faith looked like where they come from. If little has been said, that is
   the starting point rather than a gap to be filled by assumption.
2. **Language reality.** Which language each person thinks in, which they are meeting in, the fluency
   gap in both directions, and whether an interpreter is involved.
3. **The mentor's own culture, named as a culture.** Their assumptions about time, disclosure,
   directness, individual decision-making, and what a "good" discipling conversation looks like — all of
   which are cultural, not neutral.
4. **The power in the arrangement.** Who has status, language advantage, legal security, money, or
   institutional standing — and in which direction.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** Both people's, which may differ. Where the disciple comes from a
   tradition the mentor does not know, that tradition is theirs to describe and the mentor's job is to
   learn it rather than correct it.
6. **Interpreter or bilingual companion**, and their relationship to both parties.
7. **Migration circumstances**, only as the disciple chooses to share them — never asked for as
   background.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. If item 1 is thin, say so plainly: the
correct first move is a set of questions for the disciple, not a description of their culture supplied
by anyone else.

## Constraints

### Must

- Build **every cultural understanding from what this person says**, and mark anything unverified as a
  question to ask rather than a fact to use.
- Surface the **mentor's own culture explicitly** — time, directness, disclosure, decision-making,
  emotional expression, and what they assume a discipling relationship is — so it stops functioning as
  the invisible standard.
- Name the **power asymmetry** in whichever direction it runs, including language advantage, which is
  usually the largest and least discussed.
- Design for **the disciple's language**, not the mentor's convenience: the disciple prays, reads, and
  thinks in their first language wherever possible, and Scripture is engaged in it.
- Give the disciple a **standing, low-cost way to say "that doesn't work here"** or "I don't understand"
  — with words, since a fluency gap plus a status gap makes objecting very hard.
- Distinguish **the gospel from the mentor's cultural expression of it** in each session, and say which
  practices are the mentor's habits rather than requirements.
- Design the **interpreter's role** where one exists: what they do, what they do not, confidentiality,
  and the fact that a bilingual family member is usually the wrong choice.
- Treat the disciple as the **authority on their own life, family, and community**, including on what a
  faithful decision costs them there.
- Expect the relationship to **change the mentor**, and design a way for that to be visible.
- Reference Scripture **by address only**, translation named — with the translation in the disciple's
  language identified separately.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording.
- Generate, repeat, accept, or act on any generalization about how people of a nationality, ethnicity,
  region, or religious background think, believe, decide, or behave — including a favourable one.
- Use a "cultural dimensions" framework, national-character typology, or honour-shame/guilt-innocence
  mapping as a description of a person or a group. Where such vocabulary is used at all, it is used
  only as a question the disciple may answer about themselves.
- Treat the mentor's cultural forms — punctuality, directness, personal disclosure, individual
  decision-making, extemporaneous prayer — as spiritual maturity.
- Ask about immigration status, asylum process, or migration history, or advise on any of them.
- Use a bilingual family member, especially a child, as the interpreter for a pastoral conversation.
- Invent research on culture, migration, cross-cultural ministry, or language acquisition.
- Position the mentor as the one with the faith and the disciple as the one receiving it. In many
  pairings the disciple's own tradition is older, deeper, and has cost more.
- Correct a practice from the disciple's own Christian tradition as though it were error.
- Name a specific service, agency, or organization.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** treat the disciple's own tradition — which may be a global-majority-church tradition the
  mentor has never encountered — as a tradition with its own integrity, described by the disciple and
  attributed accordingly; note where it and the mentor's differ, without ranking them.
- **Must Not:** present the mentor's tradition as the standard against which the disciple's is measured,
  or treat unfamiliar practice as syncretism, immaturity, or error without the disciple's own account of
  what it means.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Write the mentor's culture down first

Before anything about the disciple, the mentor describes their own assumptions: what they consider
punctual, how directly they expect to be told things, how much personal disclosure they think is normal,
whether decisions are made alone or with family, what they assume prayer sounds like. This list is the
mentor's culture, and until it is written it will be mistaken for the way things are.

### Step 2 — Build the question set, not the profile

Produce the questions to ask this person about their own world — family and obligation, authority and
respect, time, privacy, hospitality, how disagreement is expressed, what faith looked like at home, and
what they wish people here understood. State explicitly that no answer is assumed in advance, and that
the questions are asked over months, not in an interview.

### Step 3 — Name the power, including language

Write the asymmetries: status, language, legal security, money, institutional standing, and who needs
whom. Then the guards: what the mentor does not offer, what they do not ask, and how they check whether
agreement is real agreement or the polite response of someone with less power in a second language.

### Step 4 — Design the language of the relationship

Decide where each language is used: prayer, Scripture, hard conversations, and ordinary talk. Default the
disciple's first language for prayer and Scripture wherever possible. Where an interpreter is involved,
define their role, their confidentiality, and the rule against family interpreters — especially children.

### Step 5 — Separate the gospel from the mentor's habits

For each area of practice — prayer style, Bible reading pattern, giving, gathering, decision-making,
emotional expression — state what is the gospel's and what is the mentor's culture. Where the mentor is
unsure, say so; that uncertainty is honest and useful.

### Step 6 — Build the correction channel

Design the disciple's low-cost route to say "that doesn't work here", "I don't understand", or "that is
not what I meant". Write the actual words, in both languages if possible, and the mentor's response —
which is thanks, not defence. Then design the mentor's own check: how they would find out they had got
something wrong if the disciple never said so.

### Step 7 — Design the relationship's shape

Set cadence, format, hospitality, and who else is involved — noting that in many contexts a one-to-one
private meeting between two people is unusual, and that family or community presence may be normal
rather than an obstacle. Ask; do not assume either way.

### Step 8 — Verify

Check: does anything in this design rest on a generalization; is the mentor's culture written down; is
the power named including language; does the disciple have real words for objecting; is the interpreter
rule in place; is the disciple treated as the authority on their own life.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Read about a culture and arrive with a profile. The person in front of you may match none of it, and
  they will spend the relationship correcting a picture they never drew.
- Use an honour-shame or cultural-dimensions framework as a description of a person. As a question they
  may answer about themselves it is occasionally useful; as an assumption it is a stereotype with
  academic furniture.
- Mistake the mentor's cultural forms for maturity. Punctuality, extemporaneous prayer, and comfortable
  self-disclosure are habits, and treating them as formation teaches the disciple that becoming mature
  means becoming like the mentor.
- Use a bilingual child to interpret a pastoral conversation. It exposes the child, distorts the family,
  and makes honest disclosure impossible.
- Assume a private one-to-one meeting is the natural format. In many contexts it is unusual, and
  insisting on it can look improper to the disciple's community.
- Ask about immigration status. It is not the mentor's business, the disciple may not be able to decline
  safely, and a well-meant follow-up question can put someone at risk.

✅ **DO:**
- Write the mentor's own culture down first. It is the single most effective move in this whole design.
- Build a question set instead of a profile, and ask it over months.
- Default prayer and Scripture to the disciple's first language, and name the translation they use.
- Give the disciple real words for objecting, and treat their use as a gift.
- Design the mentor's own check for what they would never be told.
- Expect to be changed by the relationship, and make that visible — a mentor who learns nothing has been
  discipling a version of themselves.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective binds the deliverable to what this person says,
  and the no-generalizations refusal is stated in the same breath as the design's purpose.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the load-bearing element — the Must Not block encodes the
  refusal of group generalizations, typologies, and cultural-dimension profiling as testable constraints.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** the mentor's culture, the disciple's account, power,
  and language are worked as four separate axes, which prevents the whole design collapsing into "learn
  about their culture."
- **RP-02 (Audience Framing):** the design is written so the disciple is the authority on their own
  life, and the mentor's perspective is explicitly relocated from default to particular.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** unverified cultural understanding is recorded as a question to
  ask rather than a fact to use, and the mentor is required to mark where they are unsure.
