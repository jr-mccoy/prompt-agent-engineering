# Translated Material Pitfalls

**Objective:** Audit discipleship material that has crossed a language — or is about to — for the
failures that survive a technically correct translation: metaphors that carry a different meaning,
examples that assume a context the reader does not have, terms with no equivalent, an implied reader who
does not exist there, and the authority a printed translated text acquires that the original never had.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This prompt does not evaluate translation quality, accuracy, or fluency — it
> cannot read the target language reliably and any such judgment would be fabricated. It identifies
> **what to check with a competent bilingual reader**, and the checking is done by people, not by this
> prompt. Bible translation itself is entirely out of scope and routes to
> `domain-biblical-studies/`; nothing here comments on which Scripture translation a community should
> use, which is a matter for that community and its own scholarship.

**When to use:** Material is being translated for a diaspora congregation, a partner community, or a
mission context — or translated material is already in use and something about it is not landing.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want the material's substance evaluated regardless of language — use
  `../curriculum-architecture/discipleship_material_evaluation.md`.
- The question is which Scripture translation to use, or why translations differ — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/biblical_translation_comparison.md`.
- The question is a specific idiom or figure of speech in the biblical text — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/original-languages/biblical_language_idiom_and_figures_of_speech_analysis.md`.
- You need to assess whether a translation is accurate. This prompt cannot, and says so.

**Audience:** Curriculum designers, translation coordinators, and ministry leads preparing or reviewing
material for another language community — working alongside competent bilingual readers.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **The material itself**, supplied as text — source language and, where it exists, the translation.
   Nothing is assessed that is not supplied.
2. **The receiving community**, described by someone from it: who reads or hears this, in what setting,
   with what shared reference points.
3. **Who is available to check.** The bilingual readers, ideally from the receiving community rather
   than only from the sending one, who will do the actual verification this audit produces work for.
4. **How the material is used.** Read alone, read aloud, taught from, memorized, or given out — because
   an error in a memorized text behaves very differently from one in a handout.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** Both the sending and receiving traditions, which may differ — and
   where they do, translated material can quietly import one tradition's distinctives into another.
6. **Prior feedback** from the receiving community about what has not worked.
7. **Existing glossary or terminology decisions** already made for this language.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. If item 3 cannot be answered — if there is
no bilingual reader from the receiving community available — say plainly that the audit can identify
what to check but that nothing can be resolved without them, and that publishing before that check is
the risk being taken.

## Constraints

### Must

- State clearly, at the top of the output, that **this audit does not assess translation quality** and
  that every finding is a question for a competent bilingual reader.
- Find the **metaphors and images** that carry different weight elsewhere — agricultural, military,
  athletic, legal, financial, weather, family, and body metaphors are the usual offenders.
- Find the **embedded assumptions in examples**: household shape, employment, money, transport, weather,
  schooling, gender roles, healthcare, and privacy that the illustration takes for granted.
- Find the **terms with no clean equivalent** — including grace, covenant, accountability, boundaries,
  intentionality, authenticity, community, and self-care, several of which are recent and
  culture-specific even in the source language.
- Identify the **implied reader**: their assumed literacy, leisure, autonomy, income, and family
  authority — and say who in the receiving community that excludes.
- Name what the material **imports** doctrinally, practically, or culturally without saying so — including
  one tradition's distinctives into another's context.
- Note where **register and directness** shift in translation: material that is warm in one language can
  read as presumptuous, patronizing, or shockingly blunt in another.
- Treat the **authority effect** as a finding: printed, translated material is often received as more
  authoritative than the original was, so an unmarked opinion becomes a rule.
- Produce a **checking brief for bilingual readers**, item by item, with the specific question to ask
  about each.
- Where a fix is proposed, prefer **local rewriting over literal translation**, and say who has the
  authority to make that call — which is the receiving community.

### Must Not

- Assess, rate, or comment on the accuracy, fluency, or quality of any translation.
- Assert what a word, phrase, or metaphor means in a target language, or that a language lacks a term.
  Every such claim is a question for a bilingual reader, marked as one.
- Make any generalization about the receiving culture, nationality, or ethnicity.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or comment on which Scripture translation a community should use.
- Invent linguistic claims, translation research, language statistics, or facts about any language's
  structure or vocabulary.
- Recommend a specific translation service, tool, publisher, or organization.
- Treat the sending community's version as the correct original that the receiving community must match.
- Propose a rewrite of material in a language the audit cannot read.
- Assume the receiving community lacks its own material, theologians, or writers.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** flag where the material carries the sending tradition's distinctives — on practices,
  authority, sacraments, spiritual gifts, giving, and church structure — into a receiving context that
  may hold differently, and name the import as an import.
- **Must Not:** treat the sending tradition's framing as neutral content that merely needs translating,
  or assume the receiving community's tradition is the one that must adjust.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Declare the audit's limits

Write the limitation at the top: this audit finds what to check, it does not check it; nothing here
assesses translation quality; every finding is a question for a bilingual reader from the receiving
community.

### Step 2 — Sweep for metaphors and images

Go through the material and list every metaphor and image, with what it relies on to work. For each, the
question for the bilingual reader: does this image exist there, does it carry the same weight, and does
it carry an unintended one.

### Step 3 — Sweep for embedded assumptions in examples

List every illustration and example, and what it assumes: a household of a certain shape, a job of a
certain kind, a car, a bank account, a weekend, a private room, a school system, a doctor. For each,
what happens to the example where those do not hold.

### Step 4 — List the hard terms

Identify the terms likely to have no clean equivalent, including the ones that feel basic in the source
language. For each: what the material means by it, the question for the bilingual reader, and whether a
loanword, a phrase, or a local term is likely to be needed — with the decision belonging to the
receiving community.

### Step 5 — Describe the implied reader and who is excluded

Write the reader the material assumes: their literacy, time, autonomy, income, privacy, and family
position. Then say who in the receiving community that is not, and what the material does to them.

### Step 6 — Name the imports

Identify what travels along with the content: the sending tradition's practices and distinctives, its
psychological vocabulary, its assumptions about individual decision-making, and its implicit model of
what a mature Christian looks like. Mark each as an import for the receiving community to accept, adapt,
or decline.

### Step 7 — Assess register and authority

Note where tone may shift — warmth reading as familiarity, directness as rudeness, encouragement as
condescension — as questions for the reader. Then state the authority effect: which parts of this
material would become rules once printed in translation, and what should be marked as suggestion.

### Step 8 — Produce the checking brief

Assemble every finding into an item-by-item brief for the bilingual readers: the location, the concern,
the question to ask, and who decides the outcome. State plainly that publishing before this brief is
worked through is a choice with consequences.

## Verification

- [ ] The audit's limits are stated at the top, and every finding is framed as a question rather than a
      judgment.
- [ ] No claim about what any word or phrase means in the target language is asserted as fact.
- [ ] No generalization about the receiving culture, nationality, or ethnicity appears anywhere.
- [ ] Every import from the sending tradition is named as an import, for the receiving community to
      accept, adapt, or decline.
- [ ] The implied reader is described, with who in the receiving community that excludes.
- [ ] The checking brief is item-by-item and identifies the receiving community as the decider.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Judge a translation's quality. The model cannot read the target language reliably, and a confident
  assessment here would be fabrication with real consequences for people's material.
- Assert that a language has no word for something. It is a frequently repeated claim, it is usually
  wrong, and it is nearly always condescending.
- Assume the sending version is the original that must be matched. Frequently the right outcome is
  locally written material rather than translated material, and the audit should be able to say so.
- Translate psychological vocabulary as though it were doctrine. Boundaries, authenticity, self-care, and
  intentionality are recent and culture-specific even in the source language, and they arrive in
  translation carrying an authority they never earned.
- Leave imports unmarked. Once printed, a tradition's distinctive reads as Christian teaching to a
  reader with no way to tell the difference.
- Publish without the bilingual check because the schedule is tight. The material will be in use for
  years and the errors are copied forward.

✅ **DO:**
- Frame every finding as a question and name who answers it.
- Sweep metaphors and examples separately — they fail in different ways, and examples fail more quietly.
- Describe the implied reader explicitly; it is usually the fastest route to seeing who the material
  excludes.
- Mark the authority effect and identify which parts must be labelled as suggestion before printing.
- Prefer local rewriting where the audit shows the material is carrying too much, and say that the
  receiving community decides.
- Assume the receiving community has its own writers and theologians, and design the check to involve
  them rather than to inform them.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective separates *finding what to check* from *checking
  it*, which is the only honest position available to a prompt that cannot read the target language.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the sweeps run metaphors → examples → terms → implied
  reader → imports → register, so each failure class is found on its own pass rather than being missed
  behind a louder one.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** six independent failure classes are analysed
  separately, because material can be word-perfect on five and unusable on the sixth.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block encodes the refusals — no quality assessment,
  no claims about the target language, no cultural generalizations, no invented linguistic facts.
- **QA-04 (Uncertainty Acknowledgment):** every finding is expressed as a question for a bilingual
  reader with the decision assigned to the receiving community, rather than as a conclusion.
