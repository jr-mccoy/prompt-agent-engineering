# Oral-Preference Learners

**Objective:** Design discipleship for people who learn, remember, and reason primarily through hearing,
telling, and repeating rather than through reading — building an oral pathway that is a genuine
equivalent rather than a simplified version, and removing the assumption, built into almost all
discipleship material, that a mature disciple is a reader.

> **Boundary guardrail.** Oral preference is not a deficit, and this prompt does not assess, diagnose,
> or infer anything about a person's literacy, education, intelligence, or cognitive ability. People
> learn orally for many reasons — culture, schooling, language, dyslexia, sight, or simple preference —
> and none of them is the mentor's to determine or to name. Where someone raises a learning difficulty
> or a sight condition themselves, that routes to appropriate professional assessment `[VERIFY: identify
> the correct service from an official source]`, never to a mentor's judgment.

**When to use:** You are discipling someone who does not read easily or by preference, working in an
oral-preference community, or building material that must work without reading.

**When NOT to use:**
- The material itself is the problem across a language — use
  `discipleship_translated_material_pitfalls.md`.
- The wider question is discipling across cultural difference — use
  `discipleship_crosscultural_relationship.md`, which this pairs with.
- You want the narrative craft of a biblical text worked — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/exegesis-interpretation/biblical_narrative_analysis.md`.
- You want a memorization plan — use
  `domain-biblical-studies/study-methods-teaching/biblical_memorization_retention_plan.md`.

**Audience:** Mentors, curriculum designers, and program leads building oral pathways.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What the person has said about how they learn.** Their own account — what they remember, how they
   prefer to receive something, whether they read at all and in which language, and what has worked
   before. Never inferred from their background, occupation, or accent.
2. **The oral forms already present in their community.** Story, proverb, song, recitation, drama,
   call-and-response, testimony — whatever is actually used and valued there.
3. **What the pathway must cover.** The formation content this oral route has to carry, so it is built
   as an equivalent rather than a subset.
4. **Group or individual.** Oral formation is usually communal, and a design built for solitary study
   will fail even if the content is right.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May foreground liturgy, recited creeds, fixed prayers, chant, or a
   catechism in question-and-answer form — all of which are oral instruments a tradition already owns.
6. **Audio access.** Whether recorded Scripture and materials in the right language are available, and
   whether devices and data are.
7. **Literacy the person is developing**, only where they have raised it and want it supported.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask before proceeding. Do not infer oral preference from anyone's
nationality, class, or occupation — ask the person, and if they have not said, that question comes
first.

---

## Constraints

### Must

- Treat the oral pathway as **equal, not remedial**: same formation outcomes, same depth, different
  medium — and say this explicitly, since the assumption of inferiority is the main harm here.
- Build on **the community's own oral forms** rather than importing a generic storytelling method.
- Design **Scripture engagement without reading**: passages delivered by address and by hearing, learned
  by telling back, with the translation and audio source named — and never with the mentor supplying
  remembered wording as the text.
- Use **narrative and dialogue as the primary carriers**, with doctrine emerging through story sequence
  and question rather than through propositions to be retained.
- Build in **telling back** as the core practice: the disciple retells, and the retelling is where
  understanding is formed and checked.
- Design for **community memory**: what the group holds together, how a story stays accurate across
  retellings, and who corrects drift without shaming anyone.
- Keep every practice **doable without literacy at any point**, including any record-keeping,
  reflection, or planning the pathway requires.
- Where the tradition has **oral instruments** — liturgy, creed, fixed prayer, chant, catechism —
  use them as the assets they are.
- Provide **audio** where possible, in the language the person thinks in, with the source named
  `[VERIFY]`.
- Never make literacy a **gate** to any stage, role, practice, or rite.

### Must Not

- Quote Scripture text from memory or assert verse wording — including when telling a story aloud, where
  the risk of drift is highest.
- Assess, infer, diagnose, or comment on anyone's literacy, education, intelligence, or cognitive
  ability.
- Infer oral preference from nationality, ethnicity, class, occupation, or accent.
- Design the oral route as a simplified, shortened, or "basics only" version of the reading route.
- Require reading, writing, note-taking, journalling, or form-filling at any point.
- Present reading Scripture privately as the mark of a serious disciple, or treat a non-reader's
  formation as provisional.
- Invent oral-learning research, orality statistics, or claims about how oral cultures process
  information.
- Correct someone's retelling publicly in a way that shames them.
- Supply story content that fills gaps in a biblical narrative with invented detail presented as the
  text.
- Name a specific audio product, app, or organization.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note that traditions differ on the place of recited liturgy and creed, on fixed versus
  extemporaneous prayer, on chant and song as instruction, and on catechism in question-and-answer form
  — attributing each to identifiable streams and letting a declared tradition use its own instruments.
- **Must Not:** present one stream's oral practice as the biblical model, or treat a tradition's
  recitation practice as rote in a pejorative sense.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Establish the medium without assessing the person

Record what the person has said about how they learn, in their words. State explicitly that the pathway
is being built for a preference and a strength, not around a deficiency, and that nothing here assesses
their literacy or ability.

### Step 2 — Inventory the community's oral forms

List what is already used and valued: story, proverb, song, recited prayer, drama, call-and-response,
testimony, chant. These are the pathway's building materials; an imported method that ignores them will
feel foreign and be abandoned.

### Step 3 — Build the story set

Select the biblical narratives that carry the formation content, in a sequence that builds. For each:
the address, what it carries, the questions it opens, and the point at which drift is most likely in
retelling. State that the wording comes from a named translation or audio source, never from the
mentor's memory.

### Step 4 — Design the telling-back loop

Make retelling the core practice: the disciple hears, tells back, is asked questions, tells it again to
someone else. Specify how accuracy is held — checked against the named source, corrected gently and
usually privately — and how the group holds the story together over time.

### Step 5 — Let doctrine emerge through sequence and question

Rather than teaching propositions, order the stories so that what the tradition holds emerges from them,
and design the questions that draw it out. Where the tradition has a creed or catechism, use its recited
form as the anchor and let the stories fill it.

### Step 6 — Build the non-literate equivalents for everything else

Take every element of the standard pathway that assumes writing — reflection, planning, tracking,
covenanting, review — and design its oral form: spoken commitment before witnesses, remembered rhythms,
a companion who asks, and markers held in the community rather than on paper.

### Step 7 — Provide audio and name the sources

Specify what audio exists in the right language, how it is accessed, and what to do when it does not
exist. Every source is named for the user to verify `[VERIFY]`; none is asserted from memory.

### Step 8 — Verify

Check: is the pathway equal in outcome rather than reduced; does anything require reading; is every
Scripture wording sourced rather than remembered; are the community's own forms used; is literacy
nowhere a gate; is nothing assessed about the person.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Build the oral route as the simple version. It is the most common form of this mistake and the
  disciple can tell immediately, because the content thins while the language gets warmer.
- Tell a Bible story from memory. Spoken delivery is exactly where invented detail enters and then
  propagates through every retelling as though it were the text.
- Infer that someone learns orally from where they are from or what they do. Ask them; people are
  frequently assumed to be non-readers when they simply prefer to listen, and the assumption is
  insulting.
- Import a storytelling method over a community that already has richer forms. Proverb, song, and recited
  prayer are frequently stronger instruments than anything a curriculum brings in.
- Require a journal, a worksheet, or a form anywhere in the pathway. One unnoticed worksheet at week
  eleven undoes the whole design.
- Correct a retelling in front of the group. The person stops telling, and the practice that carries the
  formation stops with them.

✅ **DO:**
- State in the pathway itself that this route is equal and not remedial.
- Build on the forms the community already has and values.
- Name the source of every wording, and use audio in the language the person thinks in wherever it
  exists.
- Make telling-back the core practice, including telling it to someone else — which is also how the
  pathway reproduces.
- Design the oral equivalent of every written element, including the covenant, spoken before witnesses.
- Show the outcomes side by side with the reading route, so equality is demonstrated rather than
  claimed.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective fixes equivalence rather than accommodation as the
  deliverable, which is the difference between an oral pathway and a simplified one.
- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the community's forms are inventoried before the story
  set is built, so the pathway is assembled from materials that are already trusted there.
- **ED-01 (Iterative Scaffolding):** the story sequence builds, and the telling-back loop is the
  scaffold — hear, tell, question, tell again to someone else — which is also the reproduction
  mechanism.
- **RP-02 (Audience Framing):** the design is written to a person whose preference is a strength, with
  the assessment of literacy explicitly excluded from the mentor's role.
- **QA-05 (Citation Requirements):** every Scripture wording is traced to a named translation or audio
  source, applied at the highest-drift point in the domain — a story told aloud from memory.
