# When the Mentor Got It Wrong

**Objective:** Take something a discipler did that hurt the person they were discipling and work it
through to repair — first testing whether "mistake" is honestly the right word, then producing the
apology in the mentee's terms rather than the mentor's, a change they can actually observe, and an ending
for the conversation that leaves the mentee free to forgive, not forgive, continue, or leave, with none of
those owed to anyone.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This is for ordinary rupture between two people — a confidence repeated, advice
> that overstepped, a disclosure mishandled, a promise dropped, a moment of impatience, a boundary the
> mentor crossed without noticing. It is **not** for conduct: any romantic or sexual contact, financial
> entanglement, coercion, threat, isolation of the mentee, anything involving a minor or vulnerable adult,
> or anything a safeguarding policy names. Those are not mistakes to repair privately, they are matters to
> report — to the safeguarding lead and, where relevant, to an authority
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct authority from an official source]` — and a self-managed apology in
> those cases obstructs a process and increases harm. If there is any doubt about which of the two this
> is, it is reported, not repaired.

**When to use:** After a mentor has done something that hurt their mentee, once the immediate moment has
passed and before the relationship quietly reshapes itself around the unspoken thing.

**When NOT to use:**
- Any conduct or safeguarding line may have been crossed — use
  `../program-operations/discipleship_safeguarding_and_conduct_policy.md` and report. This prompt refuses
  that case by design.
- The season is over and the mentor is reviewing it privately — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_season_debrief.md`, which handles the honest mistake
  retrospectively and for the mentor alone.
- The problem is the shape of the relationship rather than a specific act — use
  `discipleship_dependency_and_over_attachment.md`.
- The relationship should end because of what happened — use
  `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_relationship_ending_or_transition.md`, and let the mentee
  choose it rather than being offered it as the apology.
- The mentee is the one who caused harm — that is not this prompt, and it is not a mentor's to
  adjudicate.

**Audience:** The mentor. A program lead or supervisor may work it through with them; the mentee is never
handed this document.

---

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What the mentor actually did.** The act, in plain terms, with what they said or did not do. Not how
   it was received, not what they meant — what happened. If the account keeps returning to intention, that
   is the first thing to work on, because an apology built on intention will restate the injury.
2. **What it cost the mentee, as best the mentor can tell.** Trust, standing, privacy, safety, time,
   a relationship with someone else. Named specifically, and marked as the mentor's guess where it is one.
3. **Whether anyone else was affected or now knows.** A confidence repeated has a second injury attached to
   it, and the repair has to reach there too.
4. **Whether the mentee has said anything about it.** Directly, indirectly, or by going quiet. Silence is
   information and is not consent.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape whether confession is made to a person, an elder, or a
   priest, whether restitution has a defined form, and what repentance is understood to involve — which
   affects what the mentor does alongside the apology, though never what the mentee owes in response.
   Where none is declared, the repair stays practice-general.
6. **What the relationship was like before.** How much trust there was to spend, and whether this is the
   first time.
7. **Whether the mentor has somewhere to take their own reaction.** Shame is the main reason apologies get
   delayed or turned into a performance, and the mentee is not the place for it.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask. If the mentor cannot describe the act without describing what
they meant by it, work that first. If there is any uncertainty about whether this is conduct rather than
rupture, stop and route to the safeguarding policy — that uncertainty is itself the answer.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Run the conduct gate first.** Decide explicitly whether this is a rupture to repair or a matter to
  report, and stop the prompt where it is the latter.
- Write the apology as **the act and its effect, with no explanation attached** — no "I was tired", no
  "what I meant was", no account of pressure the mentor was under.
- Keep the apology **free of any request**: no asking for forgiveness, no asking for reassurance, no
  asking how the mentee feels about the mentor now, no asking them to say it is all right.
- Name a **change the mentee can observe**, not a change of heart, and say what it will look like from
  where they are standing.
- Make the **outcomes genuinely open**: forgive, not forgive, continue, pause, hand over, or end — all
  listed, all equally available, none framed as the mature one.
- Give the mentee a **route that is not the mentor** — a program lead, another leader, someone outside —
  and say it out loud, since a person who was hurt by the mentor should not have to raise it only with
  them.
- Address the **second injury** where a confidence travelled: what the mentor does about the third party,
  with the mentee deciding what is said there.
- State what the mentor does **if the mentee does not respond**, which is not to follow up until they do.

### Must Not

- Produce any apology containing "but", "if", "however", or any clause that relocates the injury to the
  mentee's perception — including "I'm sorry you felt", "I'm sorry it came across", and "I'm sorry if".
- Ask for, expect, hint at, time-bound, or in any way set up forgiveness or reconciliation as the outcome,
  or describe withholding it as unforgiveness, bitterness, or immaturity.
- Turn the apology into a disclosure of the mentor's own distress, guilt, or self-criticism, or arrange
  anything in which the mentee ends up comforting the mentor.
- Advise repairing privately anything that a safeguarding policy would require to be reported, or treat
  reporting as an option to consider after the conversation.
- Use confession, repentance, grace, or restoration language to shorten the mentee's process or to imply
  a response is owed.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or use any passage on forgiveness, reconciliation, or restoration to
  produce an expected response.
- Name any counselor, agency, or service; state any legal or reporting duty — `[VERIFY]` and route to the
  safeguarding lead and `domain-legal/`.
- Score the repair, rate the apology, or stage the relationship's recovery.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions differ on confession and to whom, on whether repentance has a defined
  form or restitution a required shape, on the role of an elder or priest in repair, and on how quickly
  reconciliation is expected within a community — and let a declared tradition carry out its own practice
  for the mentor's part, distinct from anything the mentee is asked for.
- **Must Not:** allow any tradition's teaching on forgiveness, reconciliation, restoration, or unity to
  place an expectation on the mentee, set a timeframe for their response, or characterize their choosing
  not to continue as a spiritual failure. What the mentor owes and what the mentee owes are not
  symmetrical, and only the first is this prompt's business.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Decide whether "mistake" is the right word

Test the act against the conduct list: anything romantic or sexual, financial, coercive, isolating, or
involving a minor or vulnerable adult, and anything the program's safeguarding policy names. If it is
there, or if it is unclear, stop. The output is the reporting route `[VERIFY]` and nothing else. Framing a
reportable act as a rupture to be repaired between two people is the most damaging thing this prompt could
produce, and it usually happens with good intentions and genuine remorse.

### Step 2 — Say what happened without saying why

Write the act plainly. Then remove every clause that explains it. What remains is the only version the
mentee can receive, because an explanation offered at this point asks them to hold the mentor's reasons
before they have finished holding the injury.

### Step 3 — Name the cost from where they stand

Set out what it cost them, in their terms, marking what is the mentor's guess. Include the second injury
if a confidence travelled, and the standing loss if it happened in front of anyone.

### Step 4 — Draft the apology, then strip it

Write it. Then cut: every "but", every explanation, every request, every reference to the mentor's own
feelings about it. What is left is usually three or four sentences, and it will feel to the mentor like
not enough. That feeling is the point — the length was never for the mentee.

### Step 5 — Name the observable change

Say what will be different in a way the mentee can check without taking the mentor's word for it: what the
mentor will not do again, what they will do instead, what they are putting in place, and who else now
knows to hold them to it.

### Step 6 — Open the outcomes and hand over the route

List every outcome as equally available, in the mentor's own words to the mentee. Then name the person
other than the mentor the mentee can take this to, and say it in the conversation rather than leaving it
implied. Then write what the mentor says about time: that there is no answer needed today and none needed
at all.

### Step 7 — Plan the silence, then check the whole thing

Decide in advance what happens if there is no reply: one message, then nothing, and no interpretation of
the silence. Then check the output — does the apology contain a single explanatory clause? Does anything
ask for a response? Would the mentee, reading it, know that leaving is an ordinary option? Is anything here
something a safeguarding policy should have received instead?

## Output Format

Produce exactly this structure. The mentor holds this; the mentee receives only the words.

```
# Repair — [what happened, in five words]

## Gate: Is This Reportable?
| Conduct category | Present? |
|---|---|
| Romantic or sexual contact of any kind | [no] |
| Financial entanglement | [no] |
| Coercion, threat, or isolation of the mentee | [no] |
| Minor or vulnerable adult involved | [no] |
| Anything the safeguarding policy names | [no] |

**Any "yes" or "unsure" stops here → report. [VERIFY: correct authority and safeguarding lead]**

## What I Did
[The act. No reasons. Two or three sentences.]

## What It Cost Them
| Cost | How it shows | Mentor's guess or their words |
|---|---|---|

**Second injury (if a confidence travelled):** [who else knows, and what is owed there]

## The Apology — Said, Not Sent Ahead
> "[Three to five sentences. No 'but'. No explanation. No request.]"

## What Will Be Different
| What I won't do again | What I'll do instead | How you'll be able to tell |
|---|---|---|

## What Happens Now Is Yours
> "[The mentor's words offering all of it: carry on, pause, move to someone else, stop.
> Nothing owed. No answer needed today, or at all.]"

**Someone other than me you can take this to:** [role — and it is said out loud, not implied]

## If There Is No Reply
[One message, then nothing. What the mentor does not do. What the silence is not taken to mean.]

## Where the Mentor Takes Their Own Reaction
[Role. Not the mentee, and not in the conversation.]
```

## Verification

- [ ] The conduct gate is run first and stops the prompt on any yes or unsure.
- [ ] The apology contains no explanation, no "but", no "if", and no reference to what the mentor meant.
- [ ] Nothing in the mentor's words asks for forgiveness, reassurance, or any response.
- [ ] The change named is observable by the mentee without relying on the mentor's report of it.
- [ ] Ending or leaving is offered in the same tone as continuing, with nothing marking one as better.
- [ ] A route to someone other than the mentor is named and said aloud, not left implicit.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Repair privately what should be reported. Sincere remorse is not a substitute for a process, and an
  apology offered instead of a report can compromise one — this is the single most serious failure
  available here.
- Explain. "I'm sorry — I'd had a terrible week" hands the mentee the mentor's week to carry, and every
  version of it converts an apology into a defence.
- Write "I'm sorry you felt that way". It relocates the problem into the mentee's perception, and it is
  heard exactly that way even when it is not meant that way.
- Ask for forgiveness. Asking makes the response a transaction and puts the mentee under an obligation at
  the moment they have least reason to be under one.
- Bring the mentor's shame into the room. A mentee who ends up reassuring the mentor has been asked to do
  the mentor's work, on top of the original injury.
- Offer ending as the dramatic option and continuing as the gracious one. Tone does this even when words
  do not.
- Follow up until there is an answer. Repeated contact after an apology is pressure, however gently it is
  phrased.

✅ **DO:**
- Run the gate first, every time, and let "unsure" count as a yes.
- Say the act and stop. The apology being shorter than the mentor wants is a sign it is theirs to give
  rather than theirs to feel better about.
- Name what will be observably different, so the mentee can verify rather than trust.
- Say the alternative route out loud. A person hurt by the mentor should not have to work out on their own
  who else they could tell.
- Give time with no end on it, and say so.
- Take the mentor's own reaction somewhere else — a supervisor, a cohort, a friend outside — and arrange
  that before the conversation, not after.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-02 (Structured Sequential Instructions):** the gate runs before the apology is drafted, which is
  the ordering that keeps a reportable act from being resolved between two people out of genuine remorse.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block bans the specific linguistic forms that make
  apologies fail — "but", "if", "you felt" — rather than advising against them generally.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** the act, its cost, the observable change, and the
  outcomes are worked separately, so a well-phrased apology cannot stand in for a change or for the
  mentee's freedom to leave.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the conduct gate is a table with a yes/no per row, because a
  prose judgement about whether something is reportable is exactly where a motivated reading gets in.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the final check scans the drafted words for a single explanatory clause
  or implied request — the two errors that survive every earlier step.
