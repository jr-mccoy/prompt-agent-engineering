# Dependency and Over-Attachment

**Objective:** Work out whether a live discipling relationship has closed in on itself — the mentee
routing decisions, feelings, and access to God through the mentor, or the mentor needing to be needed —
and produce the specific changes that reopen it: decisions handed back, other people restored, contact
resized, and the words for saying all of it, with ending the relationship as one available outcome
rather than the assumed one.

> **Boundary guardrail.** This addresses relationship shape, not anyone's mental health. It is not
> therapy, attachment assessment, or a diagnosis of either person. If what is actually present is
> depression, an eating disorder, self-harm, suicidality, addiction, an abusive situation at home, or
> distress the mentee cannot manage between meetings, that is not dependency and does not get a
> dependency remedy — it routes to a licensed professional now
> `[VERIFY: identify the correct service from an official source]`. And if any conduct line has been
> crossed — romantic or sexual contact, financial entanglement, coercion, isolation imposed on the
> mentee, or anything involving a minor or vulnerable adult — this prompt stops and the safeguarding
> policy governs instead.

**When to use:** When a discipling relationship has become the mentee's main or only source of counsel,
reassurance, or spiritual confidence — or when a mentor notices they are the one who cannot let go — and
nothing has crossed a conduct line, so there is something to repair rather than to report.

**When NOT to use:**
- Any conduct or safeguarding line has been crossed — use
  `../program-operations/discipleship_safeguarding_and_conduct_policy.md`, immediately.
- The relationship should simply end — use
  `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_relationship_ending_or_transition.md`, which covers the five
  ending types and does it better than an unwinding plan would.
- You are setting standing scope for a mentor before anything has gone wrong — use
  `../mentor-equipping/discipleship_mentor_boundaries_and_referral.md`.
- Meetings are simply too frequent or badly shaped — use
  `../pairing-and-relationship/discipleship_cadence_and_rhythm_design.md`, and change the rhythm before
  concluding anything about dependency.
- The mentee was harmed and the relationship is over — use
  `discipleship_harmed_by_previous_discipling.md`.

**Audience:** The mentor, the program lead, or the pair together. Where the mentor is the dependent one,
the honest reader is the program lead or the mentor's own supervisor.

## Inputs / Context

**Required:**

1. **What is actually happening, in behaviour.** Contact frequency and who initiates it, what is being
   asked for, what decisions are being brought, what happens between meetings, and what changed and when.
   Behaviour, not impressions — "she messages most evenings and asks what she should do" is usable;
   "she's too attached" is not.
2. **Which direction it runs.** Mentee toward mentor, mentor toward mentee, or both. This is the question
   most often answered wrongly, because the mentor is usually the one asking, and the answer determines
   the entire remedy.
3. **What else the mentee has.** Other friendships, family, church community, professional support, and
   whether these have thinned since the relationship began. A relationship is not the problem when it is
   someone's only one; the absence of everything else is.
4. **What the relationship was for.** The original scope and duration, and whether either person can
   still state it.

**Optional:**

5. **Declared tradition (optional).** May shape how spiritual authority, submission, covering, and
   confession are understood, and whether directive counsel from a discipler is normal or unusual — which
   changes what counts as ordinary practice and what counts as drift. Where a tradition treats directive
   guidance as normal, the output describes its own practice plainly and still names the mentee's own
   decisions as the thing to be protected. Where none is declared, the analysis stays practice-general.
6. **Whether anyone else has noticed.** A cohort, a spouse, a friend, a program lead.
7. **The mentor's own season.** Loneliness, a recent loss, an empty diary, or a role they are about to
   lose all make being needed harder to give up.

**If any required input is missing:** Ask, and be specific about behaviour. If the only available
description is a feeling about the relationship, do not proceed to a plan — a dependency finding built on
an impression will land on the mentee as an accusation they cannot answer.

## Constraints

### Must

- **Screen out the three alternatives first**: a safeguarding matter, a clinical matter, and a plain
  cadence problem. Each is far more common than dependency and each has a different remedy.
- Establish **direction before remedy**, and hold open that the mentor may be the dependent party — the
  case least likely to be self-reported and most likely to be misread as the mentee's problem.
- Work the diagnosis on **observable behaviour only**, and state what would have to be true for the
  finding to be wrong.
- Produce **changes to structure, not to feelings**: what is decided by whom, what the contact pattern
  is, what gets routed elsewhere, and who else is in the mentee's life.
- Name **who the mentee's decisions belong to** and how they are handed back — including the mentor's
  standing answer when asked to decide something.
- Include a **re-widening plan**: the specific other people and settings that come back, since dependency
  is closed by adding, not only by subtracting.
- Give **verbatim words** for raising it, for the mentor declining to decide something, and for resizing
  contact — without any of them implying the mentee did something wrong.
- Treat **ending as one of three outcomes** — adjust, hand over, or end — and say what distinguishes them.
- State what the mentor **owes by way of ownership**, because most dependency is co-created and a plan
  that lands entirely on the mentee is inaccurate as well as unkind.

### Must Not

- Diagnose attachment style, personality, trauma, or any clinical condition in either person.
- Frame the mentee's need as immaturity, weakness, idolatry, a failure to trust God, or a lack of faith —
  or use any spiritual category to describe what is a relational structure.
- Treat abrupt withdrawal, silence, or an unexplained drop in contact as a remedy. Withdrawal without
  explanation is experienced as rejection and is itself a harm.
- Produce any score, level, or rating of dependency, or a threshold at which someone "counts" as
  dependent.
- Instruct the mentor to handle a clinical situation with structural changes, or to treat a professional
  referral as a step to try if the changes do not work.
- Assume the mentee is the dependent party, or write the plan in a way that only works if they are.
- Name any counselor, therapist, agency, or service.
- State any legal or reporting duty. Mark `[VERIFY]` and route to the safeguarding lead and
  `domain-legal/`.
- Quote Scripture text from memory, or use any passage about submission, covering, spiritual authority,
  or honouring leaders to justify the pattern or to shame the mentee out of it.

### Tradition-neutral stance (Must / Must Not)

- **Must:** note where traditions genuinely differ — on spiritual authority and covering, on whether a
  discipler gives directive counsel, on confession to a person, on submission language, and on how much
  weight a leader's guidance carries in a personal decision — and let a declared tradition describe its
  own practice as its own, so that the difference between its normal practice and this relationship's
  drift can actually be seen.
- **Must Not:** rule that any tradition's practice of spiritual direction or authority is itself
  dependency, or allow a tradition's teaching on submission or covering to be used to keep a mentee from
  making their own decisions, keep them from other counsel, or characterize their pulling back as
  rebellion.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Rule out what this is not

Check three things before going further. Has any conduct line been crossed? Then this stops and
safeguarding governs. Is there clinical distress driving the contact? Then it routes out `[VERIFY]` and a
structural plan would be an obstacle. Is this simply an unshaped cadence, with no agreed rhythm and no
end date? Then fix the rhythm first and reassess, because most of what gets called dependency is an
unmanaged calendar.

### Step 2 — Determine the direction honestly

Ask who initiates, who is uncomfortable, who would find stopping harder, and what the mentor gets from
the relationship. Where the mentor is the one who cannot let go — because the role is their sense of
usefulness, because their own season is thin, because they are needed here and nowhere else — say it, and
write the plan for them. Nothing else in this document works if the direction is wrong.

### Step 3 — Describe the pattern in behaviour, and test it

Set out what actually happens, in observable terms. Then state what would have to be true for this to be
something else, and check it. A finding that cannot be wrong is not a finding.

### Step 4 — Locate whose decisions have moved

Identify what the mentee has stopped deciding for themselves — jobs, relationships, money, church,
whether they are all right. For each, name what handing it back looks like, and write the mentor's
standing answer when asked to decide it: something that returns the decision without withdrawing the
relationship.

### Step 5 — Rebuild the width

List who else is in the mentee's life and who used to be. Name the specific people and settings that come
back, and what the mentor does to make that happen — actively, since a mentor stepping back from a person
with nobody else does not create width, it creates absence.

### Step 6 — Resize the structure and write the words

Set the new contact pattern, what happens between meetings, and what is routed elsewhere. Then write
verbatim: how the mentor raises it, what they say when asked to decide something, what they say about
contact between meetings, and what they say if the mentee hears all of it as rejection — which is the
most likely response and the one that determines whether any of this holds.

### Step 7 — Choose the outcome, and own the mentor's part

Decide among adjust, hand over, or end, and say what distinguishes them. Then write what the mentor owns:
what they allowed, enjoyed, or set up. Then check the plan — is it structural rather than emotional? Does
it add as well as subtract? Would it still be right if the direction were reversed? Is there any sentence
here that would land on the mentee as a fault?

## Verification

- [ ] Safeguarding, clinical need, and an unset cadence are ruled out before any dependency finding.
- [ ] Direction is stated with evidence, and the mentor-is-dependent case is genuinely considered.
- [ ] The finding is behavioural and includes what would falsify it.
- [ ] The plan adds people and settings, not only subtracts contact.
- [ ] No score, level, or threshold of dependency appears anywhere.
- [ ] Nothing frames the mentee's need as immaturity, idolatry, or a failure to trust God.

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Call it dependency when the cadence was never set. A relationship with no agreed rhythm and no end date
  produces exactly these symptoms, and the fix is a calendar, not a confrontation.
- Assume the mentee is the dependent one. The mentor is usually the person asking the question, and the
  case where the mentor cannot let go is both common and almost never self-diagnosed.
- Solve it by withdrawing. Reduced contact without explanation is experienced as being dropped, and it
  confirms the fear that made the relationship load-bearing in the first place.
- Reach for a spiritual category. "You've made me an idol" may even be true, and it is still a sentence
  that ends the conversation and shames someone for needing help.
- Apply structural changes to a clinical situation. Nightly messages driven by panic are not a boundary
  problem, and treating them as one delays care and teaches the person that reaching out is unwelcome.
- Take contact frequency alone as the signal. Some relationships are properly intense for a season; what
  matters is whose decisions are being made and who else exists.
- Write a plan the mentee has to accept a verdict to follow. If it only works when they agree they were
  too dependent, it will not work.

✅ **DO:**
- Rule out the three alternatives explicitly and in that order, before using the word dependency at all.
- Get the direction right first, and be willing to write the plan about the mentor.
- Describe behaviour and say what would falsify the finding — it keeps the conversation about a pattern
  rather than about a person's character.
- Hand decisions back with a standing sentence, so the mentor has something to say that returns the
  choice without withdrawing the relationship.
- Rebuild width actively. Stepping back from someone with nobody else is not a remedy.
- Write what the mentor owns. Dependency is almost always co-created, and a plan that says so is both
  more accurate and easier to accept.

## Techniques Used

- **ST-01 (Clear Objective Statement):** the objective names both directions and fixes the deliverable as
  structural change, which stops the prompt from producing an assessment of the mentee's character.
- **RT-02 (Multi-Dimensional Analysis Framework):** direction, behaviour, decision ownership, and the
  mentee's wider network are analysed as separate axes, which is what prevents contact frequency alone
  from carrying the finding.
- **CM-02 (Constraint Specification):** the Must Not block rules out the three responses this situation
  reliably attracts — a clinical label, a spiritual accusation, and silent withdrawal.
- **OC-03 (Markdown Table Specification):** the decisions table pairs each displaced decision with the
  mentor's standing words on the same row, so no pattern is named without a usable response attached.
- **QA-01 (Self-Verification):** the final check tests whether the plan would survive the direction being
  reversed, which is the error this diagnosis makes most often.
