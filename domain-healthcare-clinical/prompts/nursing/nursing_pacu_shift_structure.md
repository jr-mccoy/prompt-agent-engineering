# PACU Phase 1 Shift Structure Card

**Objective:** Give a PACU orientee a default "how I run a shift" template that she carries until the flow becomes automatic. The card imposes organizational structure externally so it doesn't have to be held in working memory — freeing cognitive bandwidth for clinical thinking. It is not a protocol or a policy; it is a personal scaffold for how to move through a shift without losing track of where you are.

**Important Disclaimer:** This card reflects a general Phase 1 PACU workflow. Facility-specific policies, staffing models, and EMR workflows supersede anything here. Adapt phases and actions to your unit.

---

## Your Role

You are generating a personalized shift-structure pocket card for a PACU orientee. The card walks through the phases of a Phase 1 PACU shift in chronological order, with 3–5 key actions per phase and one "don't skip" anchor — the single most important action in that phase that orientees under load tend to drop.

## Framework

### Phase 1: Pre-Shift Setup (Arrival → First Patient)

**Goal:** Walk onto the floor ready to receive, not scrambling to set up.

Actions:
1. Check assignment board — how many bays, who is your backup, who is charge
2. Stock and prep your bay(s) — suction, O₂, monitors, warmer, emesis basin, IV supplies
3. Review the OR board — what cases are running, estimated end times, which ones are yours
4. Quick glance at patient charts for incoming cases — allergies, airway history, anesthesia plan, baseline vitals
5. Locate: your preceptor, the anesthesia provider, the charge nurse

**Don't skip:** Check the OR board. Without it, every patient arrival is a surprise. With it, you have a 10–30 minute head start on planning.

---

### Phase 2: Receiving a Patient From OR (Handoff → First 5 Minutes)

**Goal:** Get the critical information and establish baseline before anything else.

Actions:
1. Listen to anesthesia handoff — don't interrupt, don't chart, just listen
2. Confirm: airway (how managed, current status), lines/drains/tubes, anesthesia type, last doses of analgesics/antiemetics/reversal agents, surgical events of note, allergies
3. Initial assessment: airway patency, breathing pattern, SpO₂, BP, HR, level of consciousness, pain, temperature
4. Hook up monitors — verify waveforms, not just numbers
5. Chart the handoff and initial assessment within 5 minutes

**Don't skip:** Listen to anesthesia handoff fully before doing anything else. Charting during handoff means missed information. Information missed here stays missed.

---

### Phase 3: First 15 Minutes (Stabilization)

**Goal:** Establish trajectory — is this patient waking up normally or showing a deviation?

Actions:
1. Vitals q5 min (or per your unit's standard)
2. Assess pain and treat if indicated — don't wait for the patient to ask
3. Assess nausea — especially high-risk patients (females, non-smokers, hx of PONV, long procedures)
4. Reassess airway and breathing pattern — recovering from paralysis and sedation happens here
5. Orient the patient as they emerge — name, place, "surgery is done, you're in recovery"

**Don't skip:** Reassess airway and breathing. This is the highest-risk window. Pain and nausea can wait 60 seconds; a compromised airway cannot.

---

### Phase 4: Ongoing Management (15 Min → Discharge Criteria Met)

**Goal:** Manage the patient toward discharge readiness while staying organized.

Actions:
1. Vitals per unit standard (q5 → q15 as stable)
2. Cycle: assess → intervene → reassess → document
3. Manage pain and nausea — titrate, reassess, document response
4. Check surgical site — dressings, drains, output
5. Monitor I&O — especially if IV fluids running or regional block in place
6. Communicate with family if your unit allows recovery-area visitors
7. Begin discharge planning mentally — what criteria remain unmet?

**Don't skip:** The assess → intervene → reassess → document cycle. Orientees under load often intervene and forget to reassess or document. The cycle is the structure that prevents drift.

---

### Phase 5: Preparing for Discharge or Transfer

**Goal:** Hand off a complete, documented, safe patient — not a pile of undone tasks.

Actions:
1. Confirm discharge criteria met (Aldrete score or your unit's equivalent)
2. Complete all documentation — meds given, assessments, I&O, any events
3. Write or update the handoff note for the receiving unit/floor nurse
4. Verify orders are in — discharge instructions, prescriptions, follow-up
5. Call report to the floor nurse (use SBAR structure)
6. Brief the patient and family on what to expect next

**Don't skip:** Complete all documentation before calling report. If you call report with incomplete charting, the floor nurse gets an incomplete picture, and you now have to document from memory while already receiving your next patient.

---

### Phase 6: Turnover Between Patients

**Goal:** Reset the bay and reset your head.

Actions:
1. Strip and clean the bay (or call EVS per unit standard)
2. Restock — suction, O₂ setup, IV supplies, monitors wiped
3. Check the OR board again — what's coming next, how long do you have?
4. Quick mental reset: what went well with the last patient, anything to flag to preceptor

**Don't skip:** Check the OR board. Same reason as pre-shift — your next patient should not surprise you.

---

### Phase 7: End of Shift

**Goal:** Leave the unit with nothing undone and nothing unspoken.

Actions:
1. Complete all charting — no "I'll finish at home" (you won't; it will be inaccurate)
2. Give report to oncoming nurse (if applicable) — SBAR, pending items, anything unusual
3. Restock your bay(s) for the next shift
4. Brief your preceptor — anything unresolved, anything you want to debrief
5. Debrief (if your preceptor runs the daily debrief protocol, this is when)

**Don't skip:** Complete all charting before you leave. Late documentation is inaccurate documentation, and it follows the patient.

## Must / Must Not

**Must:**
- Present phases in chronological shift order
- Include 3–5 specific actions per phase (not vague categories)
- Include exactly one "don't skip" anchor per phase — the single action most likely to be dropped under load
- Use checkbox format for the pocket card — she should be able to physically check things off early in orientation
- Adapt to the specific unit's EMR, staffing model, and policies via input variables

**Must Not:**
- Include drug doses, specific protocols, or clinical algorithms — those belong in the unit's reference materials
- Replace the preceptor's direct teaching of EMR navigation or patient-type archetypes
- Imply a fixed time per phase — PACU is event-driven, not clock-driven
- Include so many items per phase that the card becomes a protocol document instead of a scaffold
- Skip the "don't skip" anchors — they are the mechanism that prevents the most common organizational drops

---

## Special Considerations

**Personalizing the card:** After 2–3 shifts using the generic version, the orientee should annotate her own copy with unit-specific additions (e.g., "Bay 4 has the broken suction — always check," "Dr. X wants a call before extubation on his cases"). Personalization converts a generic scaffold into a personal operating system.

**When to stop using the card:** The card has done its job when the orientee reaches for it and realizes she already did everything on it. That usually happens around week 3–4 of scaffolded practice. She should keep it in her pocket for another week as backup, then retire it.

**Multiple patients simultaneously:** The card assumes one patient at a time for simplicity. Once she's managing two bays, the prioritization rule card becomes the primary tool; this one shifts to background.

**Call shifts and off-hours:** Add a "call-shift addendum" line if applicable: who is the backup anesthesia provider, where are emergency supplies, what's the rapid-response process after hours.

---

**Critical Reminder:** The shift structure card is organizational scaffolding, not clinical protocol. Its purpose is to free cognitive bandwidth by offloading "what do I do next?" to a piece of paper so the orientee can spend her working memory on "what does this patient need?" A nurse who doesn't have to think about shift flow can think about patients. That's the trade.
