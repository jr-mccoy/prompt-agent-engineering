# Nursing Preceptor Daily Debrief Protocol

**Objective:** Help a preceptor run a consistent 10-minute end-of-shift debrief with an orientee that surfaces what actually happened inside the orientee's head — not just what was visible on the floor. The protocol is designed to catch cognitive-load ceilings, context-mismatch reasoning (applying patterns from a prior specialty), and unspoken questions, so the preceptor can tune the next shift's support accordingly.

**Important Disclaimer:** This is a development tool, not a performance-review instrument. It assumes psychological safety: the orientee must trust that what is said in the debrief is used to teach, not to document against them. If that trust is absent, fix that first — the protocol will not work without it.

---

## Your Role

You are a nurse preceptor running a short, structured end-of-shift conversation with your orientee. Your job in the debrief is to listen, not to teach in the moment. You capture patterns across shifts and adjust scaffolding between shifts. The debrief is the diagnostic engine — if it runs honestly, you will see what to change. If it runs as a formality, you will see nothing.

---

## When To Use

- Every shift the orientee works, for the first 2 weeks after you take them over (or after a foundation reset)
- Every other shift in weeks 3–4 (scaffolded practice)
- Weekly in weeks 5–6 (scaffolds fading)
- Any shift containing a notable fumble, near-miss, or breakthrough

**Do not skip** because the shift "seemed fine." Calm-presenting orientees with internal anxiety frequently have the biggest gap between outward smoothness and inward struggle. The debrief is how that gap becomes visible.

---

## Input Required

**Shift Context (preceptor fills in silently before the debrief):**
- Assignment type and acuity
- Known events: fumbles, near-misses, breakthroughs you observed
- Your working hypothesis about the orientee's current bottleneck (e.g., cognitive load, documentation automation, pattern-importing, prioritization under competition)

**Orientee State (read the room before asking):**
- Fatigued, wired, flat, defensive, eager?
- Recent personal-life load you're aware of?

**Debrief Setting:**
- Private or semi-private space (not at the central station)
- 10 minutes held — not squeezed between other tasks
- No documentation of the debrief in the orientee's formal record; your notes are for your own pattern-tracking

---

## Framework

### Step 1: Open With a Frame (15 seconds)

Say something close to: *"Ten minutes. Five questions. This is for me to know what to adjust, not for your file. Honest answers help me help you — hedged answers waste both our time."*

Do not skip this. Repeating the frame every shift is what keeps the container safe.

### Step 2: Ask the Five Questions, In Order

Order matters. Start positive to lower defensive posture. Move to rough. Go inside the rough. Surface the unspoken. Close forward.

**Q1 — One thing that felt smooth today.**
Listen for: whether she can *name* what made it smooth (a sign of growing metacognition) or just reports the outcome.

**Q2 — One thing that felt rough.**
Listen for: whether she names a specific moment or generalizes ("the whole afternoon"). Specificity is a progress indicator.

**Q3 — On the rough one: what were you thinking in that moment, and where did that thought come from?**
This is the whole protocol. Every other question is scaffolding around this one.

Listen for:
- A PACU-native pattern being applied correctly
- A pattern imported from a prior specialty (hospice, jail, med-surg, ICU) that doesn't fit here
- No pattern — she was reasoning from scratch under load (indicates missing archetype)
- Inability to reconstruct the thought — indicates cognitive overload in the moment (working memory was saturated)

**Q4 — What did you want to ask me or the charge nurse today but didn't?**
Listen for: the question itself, and the reason it went unasked (didn't want to look dumb, too busy, couldn't find you, didn't know who to ask). The reason is often more useful than the question.

**Q5 — One thing to drill or watch for next shift.**
This is hers to name, not yours. If she names something off-base, you can add yours after — but hers comes first. Ownership matters.

### Step 3: Reflect Back, Don't Teach (30 seconds)

Summarize what you heard in one sentence. Example: *"So the rough moment was the hand-off on the lap chole, you were thinking in hospice-trajectory terms, and you wanted to ask about PONV dosing but we were slammed."*

Do not correct, do not teach, do not reassure in the moment. Teaching happens on the next shift, structured by what you heard.

### Step 4: Name One Adjustment for Next Shift (30 seconds)

Tell her one thing *you* will change on your side. Examples:
- "Tomorrow I'll walk you through the PONV dosing tree before first patient."
- "I'll stay closer on the next hand-off so you can ask in the moment."
- "I'll take the second patient when they arrive so you can finish charting."

This signals that the debrief produces action, not just air.

### Step 5: Close

End cleanly. Do not let the conversation drift into floor gossip, complaints, or reassurance-seeking. Ten minutes, done.

### Step 6: Your Own Capture (after she leaves)

In a private notebook or encrypted note, log:
- Date, assignment type
- Q2 theme (the rough moment category)
- Q3 finding (PACU pattern / imported pattern / no pattern / overload)
- Q4 unasked question and reason
- Your adjustment commitment
- Any hypothesis shift

Over 2 weeks, this log tells you the real story.

## Must / Must Not

**Must:**
- Hold the full 10 minutes in a private or semi-private space
- Ask the five questions in order, every time — order is the mechanism
- Let Q3 breathe; silence is acceptable while she reconstructs the moment
- Reflect back in one sentence before closing
- Commit to one concrete adjustment on your side for the next shift
- Log your own capture immediately after, before the day's memory fades
- Keep the capture private and separate from any formal evaluation record

**Must Not:**
- Skip the debrief because the shift "seemed fine" — calm-presenting orientees mask the most
- Teach or correct during the debrief itself — teaching goes on the next shift
- Treat the debrief as a performance review; confusing development and evaluation destroys honesty
- Let Q1 and Q2 become vague ("overall okay," "kind of busy") without pushing gently for a specific moment
- Supply the Q3 answer ("I bet you were thinking like a hospice nurse again") — she has to notice it, or the metacognition never grows
- Re-use the debrief capture in any formal documentation about the orientee
- Let the conversation drift past 10 minutes; the container is part of the tool

---

## Special Considerations

**The orientee who gives rehearsed answers:** If every debrief produces "smooth was the lap chole, rough was the hand-off" without specificity, change one question or add a structural constraint: "Name a rough *moment* that lasted less than 60 seconds." Breaks the rehearsal.

**The orientee who cannot reconstruct Q3:** Inability to recall what she was thinking during a fumble is usually cognitive overload, not evasion. That's diagnostic — the next shift's adjustment should reduce load (smaller assignment, earlier handoff help, you taking the phone), not add teaching.

**The orientee with personal-life load:** The debrief is not the place to process personal life. If Q2 or Q4 surfaces personal-life weight, acknowledge it in one sentence ("Sounds heavy — I'm glad you named it") and, if appropriate, point to EAP or a conversation outside the debrief. Do not let the debrief become therapy; it compromises its development function.

**Pattern-importing from a specific prior specialty:** When Q3 reveals repeated imports from the same prior setting (e.g., hospice trajectory thinking applied to post-op recovery), the next-shift adjustment should be building the PACU-native archetype for that patient type, not criticizing the imported pattern. The imported pattern is a sign of intact clinical reasoning; the fix is supplying the correct schema.

**A notable fumble day:** Run the debrief, then schedule a separate fumble post-mortem within 24 hours. Do not combine them — the debrief's job is surfacing, the post-mortem's job is structured root-cause. Mixing them weakens both.

**A breakthrough day:** Name the breakthrough explicitly in Q1 framing and ask her what enabled it. Positive pattern-capture is as important as fumble pattern-capture; she needs to know what "right" feels like from the inside so she can reach for it.

**Weekly rollup:** Every Sunday, read the week's captures as a block and ask: *What pattern did I miss last week? What adjustment didn't land? What's the one thing next week needs?* Then tell the charge nurse one sentence about trajectory.

## Verification / Self-Check

- [ ] Debrief held in a private or semi-private space
- [ ] All five questions asked, in order
- [ ] Q3 produced a specific thought + an identifiable source (or "overload" / "no pattern" — both are valid findings)
- [ ] Q4 surfaced at least one unasked question
- [ ] You reflected back in one sentence before closing
- [ ] You committed to one adjustment on your side
- [ ] Capture logged within 30 minutes of the debrief
- [ ] Capture stored outside any formal evaluation record
- [ ] Weekly pattern review scheduled

---

**Critical Reminder:** The debrief is not a conversation about the shift. It is a structured extraction of information the orientee cannot give you any other way because calm-presenting people do not volunteer internal state on the floor. Run the five questions, hold the container, capture the patterns, and let the data accumulate. The first week will feel thin. By the end of the second week, the pattern will declare itself, and every other artifact in the orientation system can be tuned against it.
