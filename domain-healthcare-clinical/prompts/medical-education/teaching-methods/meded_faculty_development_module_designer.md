# Faculty Development Module Designer

**Objective:** Design a faculty development module for clinical educators that targets Kirkpatrick Level 3 behavior change — through skill practice, reflective exercises, and a concrete application commitment — not passive information transmission.

## When to Use
- ✅ Program directors, clerkship directors, or simulation faculty designing a faculty development workshop on clinical teaching, feedback, or assessment
- ✅ GME offices building faculty development curricula for new attending physicians or community preceptors
- ✅ Medical education leaders who need a structured, evidence-based module (not a lecture-and-slide-deck approach) for a faculty development day
- ✅ Simulation educators training other educators to facilitate debriefing
- ❌ Not for grand rounds or department educational updates — those are knowledge dissemination, not skill development
- ❌ Not for faculty onboarding administrative content (credentialing, EHR training) — this prompt designs pedagogical skill development, not operational orientation

## Inputs Required
- **Module topic:** e.g., Giving feedback using R2C2 / One-Minute Preceptor in the clinical setting / Designing OSCE checklists / Debriefing after simulation / Milestone narrative writing
- **Target faculty audience:** New attendings / Community preceptors / Clerkship directors / Simulation faculty / Program directors
- **Session duration:** e.g., 60 / 90 / 120 minutes / half-day workshop
- **Group size:** Small workshop (< 20) / Medium group (20–40) / Large session (> 40)
- **Faculty's prior skill level:** Novice (never used this technique) / Beginner (aware but inconsistent) / Intermediate (uses sometimes, wants to improve)
- **Desired behavior change:** What should faculty DO differently in their next teaching encounter after this module?

## Constraints
**Must:**
- Target Kirkpatrick Level 3 (behavior change in practice) — the session must require faculty to practice the teaching skill, not just hear about it
- Include a skill practice activity where faculty enact the target behavior (role-play, artifact critique, case analysis, peer observation)
- Include an application planning step: a concrete, specific commitment to use the skill in a real teaching context within 2 weeks
- Reference a validated framework or evidence base for the teaching technique being taught

**Must Not:**
- Design the module as a lecture about good teaching — faculty learn teaching by practicing teaching, not by hearing about it
- Omit the application planning step — without a specific commitment, transfer to practice is near zero (Guskey, 2002)
- Promise behavior change from a single module — realistic framing of what a single session can and cannot achieve
- Use the same design for all faculty skill levels — a novice module differs from an intermediate-level module on the same topic

## Instructions

1. **Define the educator competency target**
   - Name the specific educator competency being developed (e.g., "provide specific, behavior-based feedback after a clinical encounter")
   - Reference a recognized educator competency framework if applicable:
     - AMEE (Association for Medical Education in Europe) Educator Competencies
     - Academy of Medical Educators (AoME) Framework
     - FAIMER Medical Education Competencies
     - ACGME Faculty Development Core Competencies
   - State the target Kirkpatrick level and what evidence would demonstrate Level 3 achievement:
     - Level 1 (Reaction): Faculty rated the session positively
     - Level 2 (Learning): Faculty can describe the technique and explain its rationale
     - Level 3 (Behavior): Faculty apply the technique in their next clinical teaching encounter
     - Level 4 (Results): Learner feedback quality improves as measured by end-of-rotation evaluations

2. **Write module learning objectives**
   - Write 3–4 objectives tied to educator behavior (not knowledge):
     - "Faculty will [do something differently] in [specific teaching context] after this module"
     - Example: "Identify one specific learner behavior to reinforce and one to redirect in a post-encounter feedback conversation within 2 weeks"
     - Example: "Apply the One-Minute Preceptor 5-step script in a 5-minute teaching moment with a medical student on their next clinic day"
   - Ensure objectives are measurable: "will apply," "will write," "will deliver" — not "will understand" or "will appreciate"

3. **Design the warm-up activity (surfacing current practice)**
   Time: 5–10 minutes
   - Surface what faculty currently do — their existing mental models and habits — before introducing new content
   - Options:
     - **Pair discussion:** "Think of the last time you gave a learner feedback. Describe what you said in the first 30 seconds. Share with a partner."
     - **Anonymous poll:** "On a scale of 1–5, how confident are you that your feedback last month led to a behavior change? Vote anonymously."
     - **Written reflection:** "Write: what's the hardest part of giving feedback to a struggling learner?"
     - **Video case:** Show a 2-minute clip of a feedback or teaching interaction; ask faculty to write what they would do differently
   - The warm-up reveals the gap between current and target practice without triggering defensiveness

4. **Write the didactic mini (evidence base presentation)**
   Time: 10–15 minutes maximum — this is not a lecture, it's a brief framing
   - Present the validated framework or technique being taught:
     - Name the framework and its source
     - Explain why it works (evidence base, 2–3 key studies or principles)
     - Show a brief demonstration (live model, video, or annotated transcript)
   - Use a structured slide set no longer than 8–10 slides
   - End with a clear, memorable 1-sentence summary: "The core move in [technique] is [X]. Everything else supports it."
   - Do NOT attempt to teach every nuance — faculty can read; the module teaches what reading can't

5. **Design the skill practice activity (core of the module)**
   Time: 25–35 minutes — the practice activity is the module's most important element
   - Select a practice format matched to the teaching skill:

   | Teaching Skill | Best Practice Format |
   |---|---|
   | Feedback delivery | Role-play: one faculty plays preceptor, one plays learner, one observes with checklist |
   | Teaching script (OMP/SNAPPS) | Scripted scenario role-play with video playback if available |
   | Debriefing facilitation | Live simulation debrief with a standardized case (faculty practice advocating-inquiring) |
   | MCQ item writing | Artifact critique: critique 3 MCQ items against NBME guidelines; write 1 item |
   | Milestone narratives | Structured writing task: given behavioral evidence, write a milestone narrative using the template |
   | Lecture redesign | Active redesign task: take a real lecture slide (faculty's own or provided), redesign one chunk |

   - For role-play formats, write:
     a. **Scenario brief:** 1 paragraph clinical context + learner level + what the educator observed
     b. **Learner character card:** who the "learner" is, their level, what they did well, what they struggled with
     c. **Observer checklist:** 5–8 items the observer watches for and scores
     d. **Debrief questions** for the trio after the role-play: "What did the preceptor do that worked? What one move would you change?"

   - Design the practice activity for the stated group size:
     - Small group (< 20): Full role-play trios with written observation checklists
     - Medium group (20–40): Role-play trios in breakout rooms; 3 rounds with role rotation
     - Large group (> 40): Fishbowl demonstration (one trio in front, rest observe) + paired practice of 1 element

6. **Write the reflective practice prompt**
   Time: 5–8 minutes
   - Ask faculty to connect the practiced skill to their own teaching context:
     - "Think of one learner you'll teach this week. What specific scenario might be an opportunity to use [technique]?"
     - "What's the hardest part of this technique for you? What support do you need to use it?"
     - "What did you learn about your own teaching from the role-play that you didn't know before?"
   - Written individual reflection is more powerful than discussion here — it requires specificity and prevents social desirability bias
   - Collect reflections anonymously as data for program evaluation (Kirkpatrick Level 1 and 2)

7. **Design the application planning step**
   Time: 5–8 minutes
   - Every faculty member leaves with a written commitment card or digital form specifying:
     - **WHO:** Which learner or learner group will they apply this with?
     - **WHEN:** What is the next teaching encounter where they'll use it? (Specific date/clinic/rotation)
     - **WHAT:** Which specific element of the technique will they focus on first?
     - **HOW:** What will they say or do differently? (One sentence, behavioral specificity required)
   - Example commitment: "Next Thursday clinic, when my M3 student presents her H&P, I will use OMP Step 1 — ask for her diagnosis commitment before I say anything."
   - Collect commitments: program can send a follow-up email 2 weeks later asking: "Did you do it? What happened?"

8. **Design the follow-up accountability structure**
   - 2-week follow-up email or LMS prompt: "You committed to [their specific commitment]. What happened?"
   - Optional: peer observation paired follow-up — faculty observe each other using the skill and debrief
   - Optional: longitudinal check-in at next faculty meeting: "Who tried [technique]? One sentence on what happened."
   - Kirkpatrick Level 3 evidence collection: aggregate data from follow-up to demonstrate behavior change across the faculty group

9. **Write session evaluation (Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2)**
   - Level 1 (Reaction): 3-item satisfaction rating (was the session engaging? relevant to your work? would you recommend?)
   - Level 2 (Learning): 3-item knowledge/skill self-assessment (before → after comparison on a 5-point scale for the target behavior)
   - Administer immediately post-session via paper or phone form (response rate drops to < 30% if administered later)

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Common Mistake | ✅ Correct Approach |
|---|---|
| Designing the faculty development session as a slide lecture about good teaching techniques | Faculty learn teaching by practicing teaching; a module that explains feedback without practicing feedback changes Level 2 (learning) at best — Level 3 behavior change requires active skill rehearsal with feedback |
| "Reflect on a time when..." prompts without structured output | Unstructured reflection produces platitudes. Structured reflection with a specific format ("Write one sentence about what you'll say differently in your next encounter and who you'll say it to") produces actionable commitments |
| Assuming Level 3 behavior change from a single module | Research on faculty development consistently shows single sessions produce attitude change (Level 2) without sustained follow-up; explicitly design the follow-up accountability component and set realistic expectations |
| Same difficulty level for all faculty regardless of prior skill | A novice faculty module on feedback needs to teach the framework from scratch; an intermediate module should start at skill practice and go deeper. Presenting basic framework content to experienced faculty signals disrespect for their experience |
| Skipping application planning because "we ran out of time" | Application planning is the single highest-leverage component for behavior transfer. If time is tight, cut the didactic mini — not the application planning and reflection steps |
| Ignoring the hidden curriculum | Faculty development must address the gap between espoused teaching values ("we value feedback") and enacted practice ("I haven't given structured feedback in 3 months") — the warm-up surfaces this gap; the reflection names it; the application plan bridges it |

## Output Format

**SECTION 1 — MODULE OVERVIEW**
- Topic, target audience, session duration, group size, faculty skill level, desired behavior change
- Kirkpatrick target level and evidence of Level 3 achievement

**SECTION 2 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES**
- 3–4 behavior-level objectives (what faculty will DO differently)
- Educator competency framework alignment

**SECTION 3 — SESSION PLAN**
Table: Segment | Activity | Duration | Facilitator Role | Materials Needed

**SECTION 4 — WARM-UP ACTIVITY**
- Format, prompt text, debrief question, purpose statement

**SECTION 5 — DIDACTIC MINI**
- Framework name, evidence base summary (3–5 bullet points), demonstration format, 1-sentence core takeaway
- Slide count: ≤ 10

**SECTION 6 — SKILL PRACTICE ACTIVITY**
- Format (role-play / artifact critique / structured writing)
- Scenario brief (for role-play)
- Character card (for role-play learner)
- Observer checklist (5–8 items)
- Debrief questions for trio

**SECTION 7 — REFLECTIVE PRACTICE PROMPT**
- Written prompt text (faculty-facing)
- Facilitator instructions for collecting and using responses

**SECTION 8 — APPLICATION PLANNING CARD**
- Faculty-facing commitment card template (Who / When / What / How)
- Follow-up accountability mechanism

**SECTION 9 — EVALUATION INSTRUMENTS**
- Kirkpatrick Level 1 (reaction): 3-item satisfaction rating
- Kirkpatrick Level 2 (learning): 3-item before/after self-assessment

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Module targets Kirkpatrick Level 3 behavior change — not just Level 1 or 2
- [ ] Skill practice activity requires faculty to enact the target behavior (not just discuss it)
- [ ] Application planning step produces a specific, written commitment (Who / When / What / How)
- [ ] Follow-up accountability mechanism specified for 2 weeks post-session
- [ ] Didactic mini is ≤ 15 minutes and explicitly does NOT attempt to teach every nuance
- [ ] Warm-up activity surfaces current faculty practice before introducing new content
- [ ] Level 3 evidence collection mechanism designed (follow-up email, peer observation, or cohort check-in)
- [ ] Faculty skill level specified and module difficulty calibrated accordingly (novice ≠ intermediate design)
