## Objective

Reason through a hypothalamic–pituitary–target-organ axis: identify the level of the lesion (primary target organ, secondary pituitary, tertiary hypothalamic, or peripheral resistance) by paired hormone measurement and feedback logic, predict the dynamic-test response, and explain the treatment rationale (replacement, suppression, or removal of the autonomous source).

## Inputs

- Clinical scenario (e.g., "39-year-old woman with amenorrhea, low libido, and fatigue; FT4 low, TSH low," "central obesity, easy bruising, proximal weakness — Cushing's workup," "hypertension and hypokalemia — aldosterone vs renin," "61-year-old man with low AM testosterone — primary or secondary?", "new diabetes insipidus after pituitary surgery")
- Available paired hormone results, dynamic test results (if performed)
- Imaging if obtained (pituitary MRI, adrenal CT, thyroid ultrasound)
- Question to answer: localize the lesion and explain the biochemical pattern

## Role

Senior endocrinologist explaining axis logic to a colleague. Names the hormones, the feedback loop, and the discriminating test.

## Reasoning Steps

1. **Identify the axis at issue.** Each axis has the same structural logic:

   - **Hypothalamus** produces a releasing hormone (TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH/somatostatin, dopamine) via the median eminence → portal circulation.
   - **Anterior pituitary** produces a stimulating / trophic hormone (TSH, ACTH, LH/FSH, GH, prolactin) → systemic circulation.
   - **Target organ** produces the active hormone (T4/T3, cortisol, testosterone/estradiol, IGF-1 via liver, milk in lactation).
   - **Negative feedback** from the active hormone back to pituitary and hypothalamus.

2. **Define the three potential levels of dysfunction:**
   - **Primary (target organ failure or autonomy):** target organ does not respond OR is autonomous.
     - Failure: target hormone low → trophic hormone high (loss of negative feedback). Examples: primary hypothyroidism (high TSH, low FT4); primary adrenal insufficiency / Addison's (high ACTH, low cortisol); primary hypogonadism (high LH/FSH, low testosterone or estradiol); primary ovarian insufficiency (high FSH, low estradiol).
     - Autonomy: target hormone high → trophic hormone suppressed (negative feedback intact and being driven by autonomous source). Examples: toxic nodular goiter or Graves' (low TSH, high FT4 — Graves' overrides with TRAb; autonomous nodule with low TSH); adrenal adenoma / ACC (low ACTH, high cortisol); ovarian estrogen-producing tumor; testosterone-secreting Leydig cell tumor.
   - **Secondary (pituitary failure or autonomy):** pituitary does not stimulate OR over-stimulates.
     - Failure: target hormone low + trophic hormone inappropriately low or "normal" (when it should be high). Examples: secondary hypothyroidism (low FT4, low/inappropriate TSH); secondary adrenal insufficiency (low cortisol, low ACTH; usually from chronic exogenous steroid or pituitary disease); hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (low T or E2, low or inappropriately normal LH/FSH).
     - Autonomy: pituitary adenoma producing trophic hormone autonomously. Examples: TSH-secreting adenoma (inappropriately normal/high TSH with high FT4 — rare); Cushing's disease (ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma — high cortisol, inappropriately normal/high ACTH); acromegaly (GH-secreting adenoma — high IGF-1, high GH not suppressed on OGTT); prolactinoma (high prolactin, low gonadotropins from prolactin-induced GnRH suppression).
   - **Tertiary (hypothalamic) failure:** loss of releasing hormone (rare clinically, often grouped with secondary). Distinguishable in dynamic testing (e.g., CRH stimulation test response patterns) and by imaging.
   - **Peripheral resistance:** target organ cannot respond despite adequate hormone. Examples: thyroid hormone resistance (THRB mutations — high FT4, inappropriately normal/high TSH, no symptoms of thyrotoxicosis); androgen insensitivity syndrome (AR mutations); pseudohypoparathyroidism (PTH resistance — high PTH, low Ca, high phos).

3. **Apply paired-hormone logic to localize.**
   - Pattern of trophic-vs-target hormone determines level:
     - **Both target hormone and trophic hormone moving in *opposite* directions** → primary problem in the target organ (intact feedback).
     - **Both moving in the *same* direction** → secondary problem (pituitary either failing to stimulate or autonomously producing trophic hormone).
   - Specific examples:
     - Hypothyroidism: FT4 low + TSH high → primary. FT4 low + TSH low/normal → secondary (pituitary).
     - Cushing's syndrome (any hypercortisolism): cortisol high + ACTH high/normal → ACTH-dependent (pituitary or ectopic — distinguish further). Cortisol high + ACTH suppressed → ACTH-independent (adrenal source).
     - Hypogonadism: low T/E2 + high LH/FSH → primary (gonadal). Low T/E2 + low/normal LH/FSH → secondary (pituitary / hypothalamic — often with low LH pulse pattern).

4. **Use dynamic testing when paired baseline is ambiguous.**
   - **Cushing's syndrome workup:**
     - Screening: 24h urine free cortisol (×2), late-night salivary cortisol (×2), 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression (cortisol >1.8 µg/dL not suppressed).
     - Then ACTH to separate dependent vs independent.
     - If ACTH-dependent (pituitary vs ectopic): high-dose dexamethasone suppression — pituitary microadenomas typically retain some feedback and suppress >50%; ectopic tumors do not. CRH stimulation — pituitary increases ACTH/cortisol, ectopic typically does not. Pituitary MRI; inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) when pituitary MRI normal or ambiguous (central-to-peripheral ACTH gradient ≥2 baseline / ≥3 after CRH = pituitary source).
   - **Adrenal insufficiency workup:**
     - 8 AM cortisol + ACTH: cortisol <3 = AI; >18 = unlikely.
     - ACTH (cosyntropin) stimulation test 250 µg IV/IM, measure cortisol at 30 and 60 min — peak <18 µg/dL = AI. Adrenal in primary AI cannot respond regardless; in chronic secondary AI, adrenal has atrophied and also fails to respond (but ACTH baseline is low not high → distinguishes from primary).
     - Aldosterone, renin, and electrolytes also tested in primary AI (low aldo, high renin, hyperK, hypoNa — mineralocorticoid deficiency); secondary AI typically preserves aldosterone (RAAS intact).
   - **Acromegaly workup:** IGF-1 elevated for age/sex; GH suppression test (75 g oral glucose; GH should suppress to <1 ng/mL; not suppressed = acromegaly). Pituitary MRI.
   - **Hyperaldosteronism workup:**
     - Aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) screen. Plasma aldosterone (PAC) high + plasma renin activity (PRA) suppressed → primary aldosteronism likely.
     - Confirmatory: saline infusion test or oral salt loading — failure to suppress aldosterone confirms.
     - Subtyping: adrenal CT, adrenal venous sampling (AVS) to lateralize unilateral adenoma vs bilateral hyperplasia. Aldo-producing adenoma → adrenalectomy; bilateral idiopathic hyperaldosteronism → MR antagonist (spironolactone or eplerenone).
   - **Diabetes insipidus workup:**
     - Water deprivation test or copeptin response to hypertonic saline. Central DI = low ADH (low copeptin), responds to desmopressin; nephrogenic DI = high ADH (high copeptin), does not respond to desmopressin.

5. **Predict the biochemical pattern and recognize "inappropriate" results.**
   - "Inappropriately normal" is often more diagnostic than overtly abnormal. A normal TSH with low FT4 = secondary hypothyroidism. Normal LH with profoundly low testosterone = secondary hypogonadism. Normal ACTH with markedly elevated cortisol = Cushing's disease (the absence of suppression is the abnormality).

6. **Tie to treatment rationale.**
   - **Primary failure → hormone replacement:**
     - Levothyroxine 1.6 µg/kg/day for primary hypothyroidism (titrate to TSH 0.5–2.5, lower in pregnancy and CAD).
     - Hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day divided (15 AM + 5 afternoon + 5 evening, or 10/5/5) plus fludrocortisone 0.05–0.2 mg daily for primary AI; stress dosing (×2–3 for moderate illness; 50–100 mg IV q6–8h for major stress or adrenal crisis with 1 L NS bolus).
     - Testosterone in primary hypogonadism (gel, IM, pellet) — titrate to mid-normal AM testosterone; monitor hematocrit, PSA.
     - Estradiol ± progesterone in premature ovarian insufficiency (until age 51 to protect bone, mood).
   - **Secondary failure → replace target hormone (not pituitary hormone, except for fertility):**
     - Levothyroxine titrated to FT4 (not TSH — TSH unreliable when pituitary is the problem).
     - Hydrocortisone for secondary AI; usually no fludrocortisone needed (mineralocorticoid axis intact).
     - For fertility in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: pulsatile GnRH or gonadotropins (HMG + hCG).
   - **Autonomy → remove or suppress the autonomous source:**
     - Surgery (transsphenoidal adenomectomy for Cushing's disease and most pituitary adenomas except prolactinoma; adrenalectomy for adrenal adenoma; thyroidectomy or RAI for toxic nodule).
     - Medical suppression: dopamine agonists for prolactinoma (cabergoline first-line); somatostatin analogs for acromegaly (octreotide LAR, lanreotide); pegvisomant (GH receptor antagonist) for acromegaly when surgery / SSA insufficient; ketoconazole, metyrapone, mitotane, osilodrostat for Cushing's medical management; spironolactone / eplerenone for primary aldosteronism not surgically curable.
     - Targeted therapy: cabergoline for prolactinoma (D2 agonism on lactotrophs suppresses prolactin and shrinks tumor — rare for surgery to be first-line).
   - **Peripheral resistance:** management is supportive / bypass; e.g., higher doses of hormone to overcome partial resistance (PTH-resistance: high-dose oral Ca and calcitriol).

## Worked Example

**Input:** 48-year-old woman with central obesity, supraclavicular fat pads, easy bruising, proximal weakness, new-onset hypertension and prediabetes. 24h UFC 380 µg (normal <50). ACTH 92 pg/mL (normal 7–63). Pituitary MRI shows a 6 mm hypoenhancing right pituitary lesion. Localize and explain the management plan.

**Output:**

```
AXIS UNDER QUESTION: HPA (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) axis with hypercortisolism.

PAIRED HORMONE INTERPRETATION:
- Target hormone (cortisol via 24h UFC): markedly elevated (380 µg/24h vs <50 normal — ~7× ULN).
- Trophic hormone (ACTH): elevated (92 pg/mL).
- Pattern: both elevated → ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome. Cortisol is high, but ACTH is *not* suppressed as it should be if the source were an autonomous adrenal lesion; therefore the cortisol elevation is being driven by elevated ACTH.

LOCALIZATION:
- ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome.
- Two main sources: pituitary corticotroph adenoma (Cushing's *disease* — ~80% of ACTH-dependent cases) vs ectopic ACTH-secreting tumor (~20%; small cell lung, bronchial carcinoid, thymic carcinoid, pheochromocytoma, MTC).
- MRI shows a 6 mm pituitary lesion — supportive of pituitary adenoma. However, 10% of normals have incidental pituitary microadenomas, so imaging alone is not definitive when ACTH is dependent.

DYNAMIC TEST RATIONALE:
- **High-dose dexamethasone suppression test (8 mg overnight, or 2 mg q6h × 48h):** pituitary corticotroph adenomas typically retain some glucocorticoid feedback at high doses → cortisol suppresses by >50% from baseline. Ectopic ACTH tumors typically do not suppress.
- **CRH stimulation test:** pituitary adenomas respond with ≥35% increase in ACTH and ≥20% increase in cortisol; ectopic sources usually do not respond.
- **Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS):** gold-standard discriminator when MRI is normal or shows a small lesion, or when biochemistry is ambiguous. Bilateral catheterization with simultaneous central + peripheral ACTH at baseline and after CRH. Central-to-peripheral ACTH gradient ≥2:1 baseline or ≥3:1 after CRH = pituitary source. The 6 mm MRI lesion may or may not be the active adenoma; IPSS is often recommended even with positive imaging when adenoma is <6 mm or pre-test probability is uncertain. Right-vs-left gradient on IPSS also helps lateralize for surgery.

ETIOLOGIES AT THIS LEVEL (most likely first):
1. **Cushing's disease (pituitary corticotroph microadenoma)** — clinically consistent, MRI supportive, ACTH-dependent biochemistry.
2. **Ectopic ACTH from occult bronchial / thymic carcinoid** — possible if dynamic tests do not behave like pituitary; consider chest/abdomen CT and 68Ga-DOTATATE PET if IPSS does not confirm pituitary.
3. **Macroadenoma with cavernous-sinus extension** (less likely given small lesion).
4. **Cyclic Cushing's** — possible if biochemistry has been intermittent; current data confirm active hypercortisolism.

IMAGING / FURTHER WORKUP:
- Pituitary MRI with thin-section dynamic post-gadolinium imaging if not already done.
- Chest CT and abdominal/pelvic imaging to exclude ectopic source (small cell, bronchial carcinoid).
- 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT if ectopic source suspected — sensitive for neuroendocrine tumors.
- IPSS recommended given lesion is small (6 mm); definitive central source confirmation before surgery.
- DEXA for bone density (Cushing's drives rapid bone loss).
- Baseline glucose / HbA1c, BP, lipids, weight/BMI, mood / cognitive screen, infection / wound-healing review.

WHY THE BIOCHEMICAL PATTERN IS WHAT IT IS:
- Corticotroph adenoma in pituitary autonomously secretes ACTH at supraphysiologic levels despite high cortisol (partial loss of negative feedback at the tumor — tumor cells have reduced glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, but not zero).
- High ACTH → bilateral adrenal cortex hyperplasia → high cortisol production → suppression of CRH from hypothalamus (feedback is intact at hypothalamus, just bypassed at the autonomous pituitary source).
- All the clinical features (central obesity, moon facies, plethora, striae, easy bruising, proximal weakness, glucose intolerance, hypertension via mineralocorticoid receptor cross-occupation and vascular effects, depression / cognitive change, osteoporosis, hypokalemia in severe cases) are downstream consequences of chronic supraphysiologic cortisol.

TREATMENT RATIONALE:
- **First-line: transsphenoidal pituitary surgery** by an experienced pituitary neurosurgeon, after IPSS confirms central source and ideally lateralizes. Selective adenomectomy: cure rates 70–85% for microadenomas in expert centers.
- **Adjunctive if surgery incomplete or contraindicated / awaiting surgery / persistent disease:**
  - Steroidogenesis inhibitors: ketoconazole 200–400 mg PO BID (titrate; monitor LFTs — hepatotoxicity, QT interaction); metyrapone (blocks 11β-hydroxylase — accumulates 11-deoxycortisol, increases androgens); osilodrostat (newer 11β-hydroxylase inhibitor, oral, monitor electrolytes and QTc); mitotane (adrenolytic, slower onset, used in ACC).
  - Pituitary-directed: pasireotide (somatostatin receptor 5 agonist — reduces ACTH from corticotroph; significant hyperglycemia adverse effect, often precipitates or worsens diabetes); cabergoline (D2 agonist, modest efficacy in subset).
  - Glucocorticoid receptor antagonist: mifepristone (blocks cortisol at receptor; cortisol and ACTH levels still high — monitor by clinical markers, glucose, BP; cannot use cortisol as marker because receptor blocked).
- **Definitive salvage if pituitary surgery / medical therapy fail:** repeat surgery, radiotherapy (stereotactic radiosurgery — slow onset months to years, risk of hypopituitarism), bilateral adrenalectomy (definitive but creates lifelong adrenal insufficiency requiring replacement + risk of Nelson's syndrome — uncontrolled corticotroph adenoma growth post-bilateral adrenalectomy).
- **Why alternate-level-appropriate therapy:** in this ACTH-dependent picture, the source must be addressed; pure cortisol-replacement strategies after bilateral adrenalectomy are last-line because of Nelson's risk and lifelong replacement.

EXPECTED TREATMENT EFFECT ON LABS:
- Within 24–48 h of successful transsphenoidal adenomectomy: AM cortisol drops to <2 µg/dL — this profound transient hypocortisolism is the *expected* sign of cure (the autonomous source is gone and the normal pituitary remains suppressed).
- Patients require hydrocortisone replacement for months (typically 6–12 months) until HPA axis recovers; some require lifelong replacement.
- ACTH gradually normalizes; cortisol axis testing every 3–6 months until recovery confirmed (morning cortisol >10–12 µg/dL or normal ACTH-stimulation test).
- If post-op AM cortisol stays >5 µg/dL → residual / recurrent disease likely.

MONITORING:
- Post-op: AM cortisol daily for first week, hydrocortisone replacement, sodium and glucose monitoring (DI risk for ~3–7 days post-op — central DI from posterior pituitary trauma).
- Long-term: clinical and biochemical surveillance at 3, 6, 12 months and yearly thereafter for recurrence. Late recurrence in 10–25% over 10 years — late-night salivary cortisol useful screen.
- Comorbidities: glycemic control, BP, bone density (DEXA at baseline + at 1–2 years), psychiatric / cognitive follow-up — Cushing's comorbidities often persist for years after biochemical cure.
```
