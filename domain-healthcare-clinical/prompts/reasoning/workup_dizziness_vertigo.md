## Objective

Work up the dizzy patient: classify by timing/triggers (TiTrATE), apply HINTS+ in acute vestibular syndrome to distinguish posterior stroke from vestibular neuritis, and select appropriate maneuvers and treatment.

## Inputs

- Symptom characterization: spinning vertigo vs presyncope vs disequilibrium vs vague lightheadedness
- Timing (Ti): acute spontaneous, episodic positional, episodic spontaneous, chronic
- Triggers (Tr): head movement, position change, standing, exertion, stress
- Associated targeted exam (T) and evaluation (E): hearing loss, tinnitus, focal neuro deficit, headache, ear fullness/pain, neck pain (vertebral dissection)
- Duration of episodes, recurrence pattern
- Vascular risk: age, HTN, DM, AFib, smoking, prior stroke, anticoagulant use, neck trauma/manipulation
- Medications: ototoxics, antihypertensives, sedatives, anticonvulsants
- Vitals + orthostatics; cardiac/neuro exam; ear exam

## Reasoning Steps

1. **Use TiTrATE framework over "what type of dizzy is it?"**
   Old approach (vertigo vs presyncope vs disequilibrium) has poor reliability. Better:
   - **Acute Vestibular Syndrome (AVS):** acute, persistent (>24 h), spontaneous dizziness with N/V, gait instability, nystagmus. Causes: vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis, posterior stroke (PICA, AICA), MS demyelination, Wernicke.
   - **Triggered Episodic Vestibular Syndrome (t-EVS):** brief, triggered by position or head movement. Causes: BPPV, orthostatic hypotension, central positional vertigo (rare).
   - **Spontaneous Episodic Vestibular Syndrome (s-EVS):** episodic, spontaneous, lasting minutes to hours. Causes: Ménière, vestibular migraine, TIA, panic, cardiac arrhythmia.
   - **Chronic Vestibular Syndrome:** persistent for weeks-months. Causes: bilateral vestibulopathy, persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD), cerebellar degeneration, polyneuropathy with imbalance.

2. **Acute Vestibular Syndrome — apply HINTS+.**
   Three-step bedside exam in continuously dizzy/nystagmic patient (do not perform if asymptomatic at presentation):
   - **H**ead Impulse Test (HIT): rapid 20° head turn to each side while patient fixates examiner's nose. Abnormal (with corrective saccade) → peripheral lesion (vestibular neuritis). Normal HIT in continuously vertiginous patient → central (stroke).
   - **N**ystagmus: unidirectional horizontal that does not change direction with gaze → peripheral. Direction-changing horizontal nystagmus or vertical/torsional nystagmus → central.
   - **T**est of **S**kew: vertical misalignment on alternate cover test → central (skew deviation).
   - **+** new unilateral hearing loss → peripheral (AICA stroke can mimic, but at the bedside hearing loss is strongly peripheral) — though new hearing loss alone is also concerning for AICA.
   - HINTS+ "INFARCT" pattern (any one of: normal HIT, direction-changing nystagmus, skew, new hearing loss) → posterior stroke. **HINTS+ is more sensitive for stroke than early MRI** (early MRI misses 10–20% of posterior strokes within 24–48 h).
   - HINTS+ should only be performed by examiners trained in it; otherwise it is unreliable. If unsure, MRI with DWI ± repeat in 48–72 h.

3. **BPPV — diagnose and treat.**
   - Brief (<1 min), positional (rolling over, looking up, lying down), recurrent.
   - **Posterior canal BPPV (most common):** Dix-Hallpike maneuver. Positive: brief upbeat-torsional nystagmus toward affected ear with latency, fatigue, reversal on sit-up. Treat with **Epley maneuver** (canalith repositioning).
   - **Horizontal canal BPPV:** supine roll test (Pagnini-McClure). Treat with Lempert (BBQ roll) or Gufoni maneuver.
   - Mastoid vibration, vestibular suppressants generally avoided as they impede compensation.
   - Recurrent BPPV: check for vitamin D deficiency, osteoporosis, prior trauma.

4. **Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis.**
   - AVS with peripheral HINTS, often after viral illness. Labyrinthitis = neuritis + hearing loss.
   - Treatment: methylprednisolone 100 mg PO daily × 3 days then taper over 3 weeks (controversial benefit per modern Cochrane). Antiemetics (ondansetron, prochlorperazine, meclizine) and benzo (diazepam 5 mg) for first 24–48 h only — long use impedes compensation. Vestibular rehabilitation therapy improves outcomes.

5. **Posterior circulation stroke / TIA.**
   - PICA (lateral medullary, Wallenberg): vertigo, ipsilateral facial sensory loss, contralateral body sensory loss, dysphagia, hoarseness, Horner syndrome, ataxia.
   - AICA: vertigo, ipsilateral hearing loss (mimics labyrinthitis), facial palsy, ataxia.
   - Cerebellar (SCA / cerebellar): ataxia out of proportion to vertigo, dysmetria, dysarthria, may have severe headache with cerebellar hemorrhage.
   - Top of basilar: visual disturbance, AMS, oculomotor signs.
   - MRI with DWI is best test (CT misses posterior fossa). MRA / CTA to evaluate vertebrobasilar system; consider cervical artery dissection if neck pain or recent trauma/manipulation.
   - Acute stroke pathway: tPA / thrombectomy per stroke prompt.

6. **Ménière disease.**
   - Episodic vertigo lasting 20 min to 12 h, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss (low frequency early), tinnitus, aural fullness. Diagnosis: ≥2 spontaneous attacks ≥20 min, audiometric documentation of low-mid frequency SNHL, fluctuating aural symptoms.
   - Acute attack: meclizine 25–50 mg or dimenhydrinate; antiemetic; benzo for severe.
   - Maintenance: low-salt diet (<1500–2000 mg Na/day), HCTZ 25 mg or HCTZ-triamterene daily, betahistine (off-label US, standard EU). Refractory: intratympanic steroid or gentamicin, endolymphatic sac surgery, labyrinthectomy.

7. **Vestibular migraine.**
   - Episodic vertigo with migraine features (photophobia, phonophobia, visual aura, headache often but not required during attack). Often history of migraine.
   - Acute: triptan, NSAID, antiemetic.
   - Preventive (≥4 days/month or disabling): topiramate, propranolol, amitriptyline, candesartan, CGRP mAbs (off-label data emerging).
   - Lifestyle: sleep, hydration, regular meals, trigger identification.

8. **Orthostatic / cardiac causes.**
   - Lightheadedness on standing → orthostatic vitals; review meds; CBC for anemia, BMP for dehydration; ECG.
   - Episodic with palpitations or exertion → telemetry, echo, monitor; treat as syncope workup.

9. **Other.**
   - **Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma):** chronic unilateral SNHL + tinnitus + imbalance; MRI IAC.
   - **Multiple sclerosis:** young adult with recurrent vertigo, INO, optic neuritis history; MRI brain ± spine.
   - **Cerebellar degeneration / paraneoplastic / hereditary ataxia:** chronic, progressive imbalance.
   - **Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD):** chronic dizziness ≥3 months exacerbated by upright posture, motion, complex visual stimuli; treat with vestibular rehab, SSRI/SNRI, CBT.
   - **Mal de débarquement** after travel: persistent rocking sensation; vestibular rehab.

10. **Imaging and labs.**
    - MRI brain with DWI + MRA head/neck for any concerning AVS, focal deficit, vascular risk, age >60 with new symptoms, neck pain, sustained headache.
    - Audiometry for any unilateral hearing loss or chronic dizziness.
    - Routine labs: CBC, BMP, glucose, TSH, B12 if elderly or chronic.
    - ECG ± Holter / patch monitor for s-EVS suspected cardiac.

11. **Disposition.**
    - Acute peripheral vestibular syndrome with reassuring HINTS, stable, ambulating safely → home with oral steroid taper if neuritis suspected, antiemetics PRN, vestibular rehab referral, close follow-up.
    - Any concern for posterior stroke → admit to stroke service, urgent imaging, neurology.
    - BPPV resolved with Epley → home with home Brandt-Daroff exercises, return precautions.
    - Recurrent or refractory vertigo without diagnosis → ENT/neuro-otology referral.

## Output Format

```
SYMPTOM CLASSIFICATION (TiTrATE):
- Category (AVS / t-EVS / s-EVS / Chronic)
- Timing, triggers, associated features

EXAM:
- Vitals + orthostatics
- Neuro: cranial nerves, cerebellar, gait, Romberg
- Ear exam, hearing
- HINTS+ findings (or Dix-Hallpike if positional)

DIFFERENTIAL (ranked):
1. [most likely with reasoning]
2. [second]
3. [third — flag central causes]

WORKUP:
- MRI/MRA if indicated (specify why)
- Audiometry, ECG, labs

WORKING DIAGNOSIS / TREATMENT:
- Specific maneuver (Epley, Lempert) if BPPV
- Pharmacologic (steroid, antiemetic, vestibular suppressant)
- Vestibular rehab referral
- Migraine acute / preventive

DISPOSITION:
- Admit (stroke pathway) / discharge with follow-up
- Driving / fall precautions
```

## Worked Example

**Input:** 64 y/o M with HTN, DM, smoker, presents with sudden onset of constant spinning vertigo for 6 hours, nausea, vomiting, unsteady gait — cannot walk without help. No headache, no hearing loss, no tinnitus, no facial weakness or dysarthria. Exam: spontaneous left-beating horizontal nystagmus, increases on left gaze, decreases on right gaze. Head impulse test: normal bilaterally. Skew deviation: small downward correction of right eye on alternate cover test. Hearing intact bilaterally. Romberg falls to right. Finger-nose mildly dysmetric on right. Vital signs stable. Glucose 142. Cr 1.0.

**Output:**

```
SYMPTOM CLASSIFICATION (TiTrATE):
- Acute Vestibular Syndrome (continuous, spontaneous, persistent >hours).
- Vascular risk factors: HTN, DM, smoker, age 64.

EXAM:
- HINTS+:
  - HIT: NORMAL bilaterally — central pattern in continuous vertigo.
  - Nystagmus: left-beating, INCREASES with leftward gaze (Alexander law) but appears unidirectional here. Reassess for direction change.
  - Skew: PRESENT (vertical misalignment on alternate cover) — central pattern.
- Mild right-sided dysmetria, Romberg fall right — cerebellar.
- No hearing loss.
- HINTS+ INFARCT pattern: normal HIT + skew + cerebellar findings → posterior circulation stroke until proven otherwise.

DIFFERENTIAL (ranked):
1. Posterior circulation stroke (cerebellar, likely PICA territory or vermis) — most likely given HINTS+ central, vascular risk, dysmetria, gait failure.
2. Vestibular neuritis — unlikely given normal HIT and skew presence.
3. Cerebellar hemorrhage — must exclude given posterior fossa involvement and risk of obstructive hydrocephalus.
4. Vertebral artery dissection — review for neck pain or recent manipulation.

WORKUP:
- MRI brain with DWI + MRA head/neck NOW. CT head non-contrast also acceptable initially to exclude hemorrhage if MRI delayed; CT sensitivity for posterior stroke is poor — do not rely on it to rule out.
- Stroke labs: CBC, BMP, coags, troponin, lipid panel, HbA1c, glucose, troponin, ECG.
- Telemetry to evaluate for atrial fibrillation.
- Carotid/vertebral imaging: included in CTA/MRA.

WORKING DIAGNOSIS / TREATMENT:
- Working: posterior circulation ischemic stroke (likely cerebellar).
- Activate stroke pathway: NIHSS, last known well time, screen for tPA candidacy if within 4.5 h of symptom onset (window depends on local protocol; posterior strokes often eligible). Time of onset 6 h here — outside standard tPA window but within potential thrombectomy window for posterior circulation in selected patients.
- BP management: tPA candidate <185/110; non-tPA permissive HTN up to 220/120 unless end-organ injury.
- Aspirin 325 mg if not given tPA (within 24–48 h).
- HOB 30° if no concern for poor perfusion; flat if perfusion-dependent until imaged.
- NPO until swallow screen.
- Neurology / stroke consult.
- Neurosurgery on standby if cerebellar hemorrhage or cerebellar infarct with mass effect (posterior fossa edema can rapidly compress brainstem; consider suboccipital decompression).
- Address vascular risk factors during admission: high-intensity statin, antiplatelet therapy, BP optimization, glycemic control, smoking cessation.

DISPOSITION:
- Admit to stroke unit / neuro ICU if cerebellar mass effect concern.
- Driving cessation; education on stroke recurrence symptoms; rehab referral (PT, OT, vestibular rehab) once stable.
```
