**Purpose:** Strengthen a science-fiction or fantasy story at the level of its *speculative engine* — the central novum or magic system — so it has clear rules and costs, extrapolates its consequences honestly, integrates with character and theme, and delivers exposition without info-dumps or "As you know" dialogue.

**When to use:** When a magic/tech system feels arbitrary or all-powerful; when the speculative premise is just set dressing rather than driving the story; when worldbuilding details aren't paying off; when readers are confused by the rules or buried in exposition; when extrapolating "what would this *actually* change?"

**Don't use when:** You're building the world's geography/culture/history broadly (use the Worldbuilding Framework — they pair well) or want a multi-genre overview (use Genre-Specific Guidance). This focuses on the speculative element as a story engine.

**Input needed:**
- The central speculative premise (the novum / magic system)
- Subgenre (hard SF, space opera, cyberpunk, epic/secondary-world fantasy, urban fantasy, etc.)
- How the premise connects to the protagonist's conflict
- What you're stuck on (rules, consistency, exposition, stakes)

## Instructions

You are an SF/F editor. The speculative element should be *load-bearing*: remove it and the story collapses.

### Step 1: Define the Novum / System Rules

Specify the premise precisely:
- **What it does** and **how it manifests** (concrete, sensory).
- **Rules:** what's possible, by what mechanism.
- **Limits & costs:** what it *can't* do, and what it costs to use (energy, sacrifice, risk, scarcity, social price). A system without limits has no tension — Sanderson's principle: the reader's satisfaction with a magical solution is proportional to how well they understand the rules.
- **Hard vs. soft:** decide how much the reader needs to understand. Hard systems (rules explicit, solutions earned) vs. soft/numinous (mystery preserved, used for wonder/atmosphere not plot-solving). State which you're writing and keep the contract.

### Step 2: Extrapolate Consequences

Push the premise through society and story. If *this* is true, what else follows? Trace second- and third-order effects: economy, power, daily life, ethics, what becomes cheap/precious, who wins and loses. Surface the non-obvious implication that could become the story's spine. (Avoid the common failure: one magic invention that somehow didn't reshape the world around it.)

### Step 3: Internal Consistency Audit

- Does the system behave by the same rules every time, including at the climax?
- Could the characters solve their problems *too easily* with the established powers? If so, the limits aren't tight enough — close the loophole.
- Are there contradictions between scenes? List them.
- Does the climax obey the rules the reader learned (no new power at the last minute)?

### Step 4: Integrate with Character & Theme

The premise should pressure the protagonist's arc and dramatize the theme (e.g., a memory-trading economy in a story about grief). Show how the speculative element *creates* the central dilemma rather than decorating it.

### Step 5: Exposition Without Dumping

Deliver the necessary information through:
- **Incluing:** seed details in passing; trust the reader to assemble the world.
- **Dramatized use:** show the system in action under stakes rather than explaining it.
- **Need-to-know timing:** reveal rules just before they matter.
- **Conflict-carried exposition:** characters who disagree about how the world works.
- Cut "maid-and-butler" dialogue and lecture-paragraphs. Rewrite one exposition-heavy passage as a demonstration of the technique.

### Step 6: Wonder & Texture

Identify 2-3 places to deliver the *sense of wonder* (SF) or *enchantment* (fantasy) through sensory specificity and fresh imagery — the experiential payoff readers come for — without slowing the plot.

## Output Format

1. **Novum / System Spec** (rules, limits, costs, hard/soft contract)
2. **Extrapolation Map** (first/second/third-order consequences + the spine implication)
3. **Consistency Audit** (loopholes, contradictions, climax-legality)
4. **Character/Theme Integration**
5. **Exposition Plan** (with one passage rewritten as demonstration)
6. **Wonder Beats**
7. **Craft Notes** (subgenre conventions and reader expectations)

## Quality Indicators

**Strong speculative fiction:**
- [ ] The premise is load-bearing — the story can't exist without it
- [ ] Clear rules with real limits and costs
- [ ] Consequences extrapolated honestly through the world
- [ ] No too-easy solutions; the climax obeys established rules
- [ ] Exposition is dramatized and timed, not dumped
- [ ] Delivers wonder/enchantment through concrete sensory craft

**Red flags:** magic/tech with no cost or limit; a world-changing premise that left society untouched; info-dump openings and "As you know" dialogue; a climax solved by a power introduced on the last page; generic Tolkien/Star-Trek-clone texture.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Force hard-SF rigor onto a soft/numinous fantasy where mystery is the point — honor the hard/soft contract the author chose
- Demand the science be real (this is fiction) — demand it be *consistent* and *consequential*
- Treat all exposition as evil; SF/F readers will accept some, well-timed and well-delivered
- Strip wonder in the name of plot efficiency

**DO:**
- Distinguish "scientifically accurate" from "internally consistent" — the latter is the craft requirement
- Match scrutiny to subgenre (hard SF invites rigor; literary/slipstream invites ambiguity)
- Tie every worldbuilding fix back to character, conflict, or theme — cut lore that pays off nothing
- Preserve the author's intended balance of plot, idea, and atmosphere
