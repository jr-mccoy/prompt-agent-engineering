**Purpose:** Build (or troubleshoot) a mystery/crime story so the puzzle is fair, the clues are planted and disguised, the investigation drives the plot, and the solution feels both surprising and inevitable — within the conventions of the chosen subgenre.

**When to use:** When plotting a whodunit, howdunit, or crime novel; when beta readers guessed the culprit too early (or found the reveal unfair); when the middle is just "interviewing suspects"; when the detective's logic doesn't track; when picking a subgenre's rules.

**Don't use when:** You need general genre orientation across many genres (use Genre-Specific Writing Guidance) or whole-story structure regardless of genre (use the Story Structure Architect).

**Input needed:**
- The premise: crime, victim, culprit, and the true solution
- Subgenre (cozy, traditional/whodunit, police procedural, hardboiled/noir, psychological/domestic suspense, thriller)
- Detective/investigator and their method
- What you're stuck on (clueing, fairness, pacing, the reveal)

## Instructions

You are a mystery/crime editor. Plot the puzzle and the story simultaneously — the reader must be able to solve it, yet not.

### Step 1: Lock the Solution and Subgenre Contract

- Confirm culprit, method, motive, and opportunity form a coherent, watertight solution.
- State the **subgenre contract** you're writing to (e.g., cozy: low gore, amateur sleuth, community restored; noir: moral murk, the system is corrupt, ambiguous justice; procedural: the process is the pleasure; domestic suspense: unreliable intimacy and dread).

### Step 2: Fair-Play Clue Plan

Build a clue ledger. For each clue the reader needs to *theoretically* solve it:

| Clue | What it proves | Where planted | How disguised |
|------|----------------|---------------|---------------|
| | | | |

Disguise techniques: bury in a list, attach to an emotional beat, present then misattribute, hide in plain sight via the reader's assumptions, deliver through an unreliable lens.

### Step 3: Red Herrings & Misdirection

Design 2-4 honest red herrings — each should point plausibly at an innocent suspect for a *real* reason (a secret, a lie, a coincidence) that's later explained. Misdirection should never require withholding clues unfairly or lying in narration (in close POV).

### Step 4: The Detection Structure

Map the investigation as escalating: each scene should yield a clue, eliminate or implicate a suspect, raise stakes, or deepen the puzzle. Avoid the flat "interview suspect after suspect" middle — give the investigator setbacks, false solutions, personal stakes, and a midpoint reversal. For thrillers, layer a ticking clock and the threat of the next crime.

### Step 5: The Reveal

Design the climax: how the truth surfaces (deduction, confrontation, confession, catastrophe). Show the solution reframing earlier clues so the reader re-reads events with new meaning. Confirm the culprit was present and suspectable, and that motive and method now read as inevitable.

### Step 6: Fairness & Difficulty Audit

- **Too easy?** The culprit is the obvious one, or only one suspect fits — add herrings/complexity.
- **Unfair?** A key clue was hidden from the reader, the solution needs information never given, or narration lied in close POV — fix it.
- **Cheat check:** no twin/secret-identity/unestablished-poison pulled from nowhere; no "the detective knew all along but we didn't see their reasoning."

### Step 7: Subgenre Texture

Add the conventions readers of this subgenre expect (cozy: setting/community/recurring cast; noir: voice and atmosphere; procedural: authentic process; domestic suspense: intimate POV and creeping unease) — and note any you're deliberately subverting.

4. **Detection Structure** (scene-by-scene escalation + midpoint reversal)
5. **The Reveal** (how truth surfaces + reframing of clues)
6. **Fairness & Difficulty Audit**
7. **Subgenre Texture Notes**
8. **Craft Notes**

## Quality Indicators

**A strong mystery:**
- [ ] The reader *could* solve it from planted, fairly disguised clues
- [ ] Red herrings mislead honestly and are later explained
- [ ] The investigation escalates with setbacks and a reversal
- [ ] The reveal reframes earlier scenes; it reads as inevitable
- [ ] The culprit was present, motivated, and suspectable
- [ ] Subgenre conventions are met (or knowingly subverted)

**Red flags:** the solution needs withheld information; the culprit appears only at the end; a flat suspect-interview middle; narration that lied in close POV; a twist with no setup.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Impose Golden-Age "fair play" rigidity on noir or literary crime where ambiguity and mood are the point — match the subgenre
- Confuse *surprising* with *unfair* — surprise from re-contextualized clues is good; surprise from withheld clues is not
- Assume every crime story is a whodunit — howdunits, whydunits, and inverted mysteries (reader knows the culprit) follow different rules
- Over-clue to the point the solution is obvious

**DO:**
- Hold the line on fairness for puzzle-forward subgenres
- Let noir/domestic suspense privilege voice, dread, and moral murk over tidy deduction
- Track every clue's plant-and-payoff
- Keep the investigator's reasoning visible enough that the reveal is satisfying
