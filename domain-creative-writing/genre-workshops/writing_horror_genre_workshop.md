# Horror Genre Workshop

**Objective:** Make a horror story frightening for a reason — a threat that
embodies a specific human fear, obeys rules the reader slowly learns, is shown
sparingly, and escalates through violations of safety until the ending lands
with the weight the subgenre promises.

**When to Use:**
- Plotting supernatural, cosmic, folk, body, psychological, haunted-house,
  gothic, or slasher horror (novel, novella, or short story).
- Readers said "it wasn't scary," "the monster got silly once I saw it," or
  "why didn't they just leave?"
- The scares work individually but the story does not build.

**Not this prompt if:**
- You need conventions across many genres →
  `domain-creative-writing/genre-workshops/writing_genre_specific_guidance.md`.
- The magic/technology system is the point and fear is incidental →
  `domain-creative-writing/genre-workshops/writing_speculative_fiction_craft.md`.
- The danger is a human antagonist the hero can outrun, outwit, or stop by a
  deadline → `writing_thriller_suspense_workshop.md`.
- The reader is a child or teen → `domain-childrens-writing/`, which gates
  intensity by age.

## Inputs

1. **Premise and threat** (entity, place, person, condition, or unknown).
2. **Subgenre** and intended intensity (quiet/literary through extreme gore).
3. **Protagonist(s)**: what they love, what they fear, what they are hiding.
4. **Setting** and why escape is hard.
5. **Intended ending** type, if decided.
6. **Draft or outline**, and the sticking point.

## Method

1. **Set the contract (CM-01).** Subgenre, intensity ceiling, and the dominant
   affect: *dread* (anticipation), *terror* (the moment of threat), *horror*
   (the revelation), or *revulsion* (the body). Most stories lead with one.
2. **Name the fear beneath (DT-04, layer 1).** Analyse on three layers:
   - **Surface threat** — what attacks.
   - **Underlying fear** — the human fear it embodies (loss of control of
     the body, inherited guilt, being unbelieved, the indifferent universe).
   - **Personal fear** — how that fear maps onto the protagonist's own secret.
   A threat with no underlying fear is a hazard, not horror.
3. **Write the threat's rules (layer 2).** What it wants, what triggers it,
   what it cannot do, and what it costs to resist. The reader learns these
   gradually; the author must know them from page one so violations feel
   deliberate, not arbitrary.
4. **Set the reveal budget.** Plan exactly what the reader sees, when: sign
   (evidence), glimpse (partial), encounter (full presence), explanation
   (optional — cosmic and folk horror often withhold it). Each step up the
   budget should be spent on a turning point, never on filler.
5. **Build the dread ladder (ST-02).** Five to seven rungs, each a violation of
   something the protagonist believed safe — the house, the family, the body,
   the mind, the rules themselves. Each rung is worse in *kind*, not only in
   quantity. Pair every rung with a release beat so the next one lands.
6. **Close the exits honestly.** For each obvious escape (leave, call for help,
   destroy it), give a reason rooted in character, rules, or setting. "They
   stayed because the plot needed them" fails; "she stays because leaving means
   abandoning her mother, who will not go" holds.
7. **Choose the ending (layer 3).** Survival with cost, pyrrhic victory,
   downbeat (the threat wins), cyclical (it begins again), or ambiguous. The
   ending should answer the underlying fear — confirm it, defy it, or leave it
   open — not merely stop the monster.
8. **Render the atmosphere.** Mark three recurring sensory anchors (a sound, a
   smell, a texture) that shift meaning as the story darkens.
9. **Self-check (QA-01)** against Verification before reporting.

## Output Format (ST-03)

```
# [Title] — Horror plan
## Contract
Subgenre · Intensity ceiling · Dominant affect
## The fear beneath
Surface threat · Underlying fear · Personal fear
## Threat rules
Wants · Triggers · Cannot · Cost of resisting
## Reveal budget
| Stage | Scene | What is shown | What stays hidden |
## Dread ladder
| Rung | Violation | Scene | Release beat after |
## Closed exits
| Escape | Why it fails |
## Ending
Type · How it answers the underlying fear
## Sensory anchors
## Diagnosis & top 3 revisions
```

## Verification

- [ ] The underlying fear is a human fear, stated in one sentence.
- [ ] The threat's rules include at least one limit.
- [ ] Each ladder rung is a different kind of violation.
- [ ] Every obvious escape has a character- or rule-based reason to fail.
- [ ] The reveal budget reaches full presence no earlier than the second half, unless the subgenre (e.g., slasher) opens on it.
- [ ] The ending responds to the underlying fear.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Do not equate more gore with more horror.** Intensity is a contract choice;
   quiet horror is not "under-delivered" horror.
2. **Do not insist on explaining the threat.** Cosmic and folk horror often
   depend on withheld explanation; flag missing *rules*, not missing lore.
3. **Do not demand a happy or triumphant ending.** Downbeat endings are a
   legitimate horror tradition.
4. **Do not call slow openings a pacing flaw by default.** Dread needs
   normality to violate; check whether the opening seeds unease.
5. **Do not mistake "the characters make bad choices" for a flaw** when the
   bad choice is motivated; flag only unmotivated choices.
6. **Do not import real tragedies or identifiable victims as set dressing.**
   Flag any use of real trauma that lacks the author's evident intent and care.
7. **Do not reduce every threat to a metaphor.** The monster should work as a
   monster first; the fear beneath gives it weight, not a thesis.

## Example

**Input (summary):** Folk horror novella, quiet-to-moderate intensity. Nell
returns to her late grandmother's hill farm to sell it. Each dusk, the sheep
face the house. Sticking point: "Nothing feels scary until the last chapter."

```
# The Watching Flock — Horror plan
## Contract
Folk horror · moderate (no on-page gore) · dread

## The fear beneath
Surface: the flock, and what stands among them at dusk.
Underlying: inheritance you cannot refuse.
Personal: Nell moved her grandmother into a care home against her wishes and
rarely visited; she fears she is the kind of person who abandons.

## Threat rules
Wants: a keeper at the farm. Triggers: an attempt to sell or leave after dusk.
Cannot: cross running water or enter a lit hearth room.
Cost of resisting: the flock takes something that was the grandmother's.

## Reveal budget
| Stage | Scene | Shown | Hidden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sign | Ch1 sheep face the house | Behaviour | Cause |
| Glimpse | Ch4 a tall shape in the flock | Height, stillness | Face |
| Encounter | Ch9 it stands at the kitchen window | Its hands | What it is |
| Explanation | withheld | — | Origin stays folk rumour |

## Dread ladder
| Rung | Violation | Scene | Release |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The land (sheep behaviour) | Ch1 | Estate agent's cheerful visit |
| 2 | Objects (grandmother's shawl in the field) | Ch3 | Pub scene |
| 3 | Time (clocks stop at dusk) | Ch5 | Phone call with sister |
| 4 | Others (the agent will not return) | Ch7 | Nell finds the ledger |
| 5 | The body (Nell wakes with mud to the knee) | Ch8 | — |
| 6 | Self (she has signed the ledger herself) | Ch10 | — |

## Closed exits
| Escape | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Leave | The only road crosses no stream; the flock blocks it after dusk |
| Call for help | Neighbours treat it as the farm's business, not theirs |
| Burn the farm | The hearth room is the one safe place |

## Ending
Cyclical: Nell stays as keeper. Confirms the fear — and reframes it: she
chooses not to abandon, at the cost of her freedom.

## Sensory anchors
Bleating (comfort → summons); lanolin smell (grandmother → the thing); wet wool.

## Diagnosis & top 3 revisions
The draft spends the whole budget in the last chapter. 1. Move the glimpse to Ch4.
2. Add rungs 3–5 so each chapter violates a new kind of safety. 3. Plant the
ledger early so the final signature is recognition, not surprise.
```
