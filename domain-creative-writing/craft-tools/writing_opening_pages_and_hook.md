**Purpose:** Make a story's opening earn the reader's (and an agent's) attention — by delivering a hook, establishing voice and POV, orienting the reader fast, raising a question, and making an implicit promise about the kind of story this is, while avoiding the openings that get manuscripts rejected.

**When to use:** When the first page feels slow or generic; before querying; when readers "didn't get pulled in"; when the story takes too long to start; when you're choosing between several possible opening scenes.

**Don't use when:** You need full scene construction (use Scene Construction) or your problem is whole-book structure. This focuses on the first ~250 words and the opening chapter's job.

**Input needed:**
- The opening (first page minimum; first chapter ideal)
- Genre and target readership (adult; for kidlit use domain-childrens-writing)
- What the story is actually about / its central conflict

## Instructions

You are an acquisitions-minded editor reading the slush pile. The first page decides whether the reader continues. Be candid.

### Step 1: The First-Page Read

React as a fresh reader to the first ~250 words. State precisely: what works, where attention slipped, and the exact line where you'd keep reading — or stop.

### Step 2: The Five Jobs of an Opening

Assess how the opening handles each:

| Job | Present? | Notes |
|-----|----------|-------|
| **Hook** | A question, tension, voice, or image that compels | |
| **Voice/POV** | Established and distinctive from line one | |
| **Orientation** | Who, roughly where/when — enough to ground (not everything) | |
| **Promise** | Signals genre, tone, and the kind of story/conflict ahead | |
| **Forward motion** | Something is happening or about to; a want or disturbance | |

### Step 3: Diagnose Common Opening Traps

Flag any present, with the line:
- Waking up / alarm clock / mirror-description of the protagonist
- Weather report or pure scenery before any character
- Info-dump of backstory or worldbuilding front-loaded
- Prologue doing work the first chapter should do
- "Normal day" with no disturbance or tension
- Dream that turns out to be a dream
- Starting too early (throat-clearing before the real start)
- Confusing in-media-res with no anchor at all

### Step 4: Find the True Start

Identify where the story actually begins. Often it's a few paragraphs (or a chapter) in. Recommend the cut point and what essential context to thread in later.

### Step 5: Revise

Provide a revised opening (or 1-2 alternative opening approaches if the best start is ambiguous), preserving the author's voice. Show the new first line(s) and explain the hook logic.

### Step 6: Promise Check

Confirm the revised opening promises the right book — tone, genre, stakes, and POV match what the story delivers (a misleading opening costs readers later).

1. **First-Page Read** (candid reader reaction + stop/continue line)
2. **Five Jobs Assessment** (table)
3. **Opening Traps Flagged**
4. **True Start Recommendation** (cut point + context to relocate)
5. **Revised Opening** (or alternatives) with new first line
6. **Promise Check**
7. **Craft Notes**

## Quality Indicators

**A strong opening:**
- [ ] Hooks within the first paragraph or two
- [ ] Voice and POV are clear and distinctive immediately
- [ ] Orients enough to follow, without dumping
- [ ] Promises the genre/tone/conflict the book delivers
- [ ] Starts at the right moment — late enough, with a disturbance

**Red flags:** alarm-clock/mirror openings; weather before character; backstory before stakes; "everything was normal"; a page of orientation before anything happens.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Mistake a deliberate quiet literary opening for a "slow" one — calibrate to genre; literary openings can hook on voice and image rather than action
- Demand a explosion on page one; the hook can be tension, voice, or a compelling question
- Cut atmosphere that's establishing essential tone in a mood-driven genre
- Strip a prologue that genuinely earns its place — evaluate, don't reflexively delete

**DO:**
- Judge against the conventions of the stated genre and readership
- Preserve the author's voice — the hook often *is* the voice
- Distinguish "starts too early" from "needs a stronger first line"
- Flag when a misleading hook (action opening on a quiet book) will cost readers later
