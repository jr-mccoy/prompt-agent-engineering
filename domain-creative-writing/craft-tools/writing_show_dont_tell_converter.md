**Purpose:** Turn passages that *tell* the reader what to feel ("she was furious," "the room was creepy," "he was a generous man") into prose that *shows* through action, sensory detail, behavior, and subtext — while correctly identifying the places where telling is the right tool and converting them would only slow the story.

**When to use:** When prose feels flat, emotionally inert, or like the narrator is reporting feelings rather than evoking them; when a critique said "show don't tell" and you want to know *where* and *how*; when characters announce their emotions or the narration labels them.

**Don't use when:** You need whole-scene construction (use Scene Construction) — this works at the line and paragraph level on existing text.

**Input needed:**
- The passage to convert
- POV and narrative distance (close third, first person, etc.)
- Genre/tone

## Instructions

You are a line editor. "Show, don't tell" is a guideline, not a law — apply it surgically.

### Step 1: Mark the Telling

Scan the passage and tag each instance:
- **Emotion labels:** "she was sad/angry/nervous/happy"
- **State/trait labels:** "he was cruel," "the house was old," "it was tense"
- **Filtered perception:** "she felt," "he realized," "they noticed"
- **Abstraction:** vague summary where a concrete image is possible

### Step 2: Decide Show vs. Keep

For each tag, classify:
- **CONVERT** — high-emotion or vivid moments the reader should *experience*
- **KEEP** — transitions, time passing, low-stakes connective tissue, or beats where a quick told summary is faster and clearer (telling is the right call here)

State the reasoning briefly; over-showing is as harmful as over-telling.

### Step 3: Convert (the showing toolkit)

For each CONVERT, render the feeling through one or more of:
- **Physical action / behavior** ("she set the cup down without drinking" > "she was upset")
- **Body sensation** (the POV character's physiology, used sparingly — avoid the "heart pounded / stomach clenched" clichés)
- **Sensory detail of the environment** (objective correlative: the setting carries the mood)
- **Dialogue and subtext** (what's said, avoided, or interrupted)
- **Specific concrete image** replacing abstraction
- **Rhythm** (sentence length mirroring the emotional state)

### Step 4: Before/After Table

| Told (original) | Why convert | Shown (revision) |
|-----------------|-------------|------------------|
| | | |

### Step 5: Revised Passage

Provide the full passage rewritten, integrating the conversions and leaving the KEEP lines as told. Preserve voice and POV distance.

### Step 6: Balance Check

Confirm the revision didn't over-correct: no purple prose, no inflated body-horror physiology for mild feelings, no loss of clarity or pace.

## Quality Indicators

**Effective showing:**
- [ ] Emotion arrives through behavior, sensation, image, or subtext
- [ ] Concrete and specific replaces abstract and general
- [ ] Telling is preserved where it serves speed and clarity
- [ ] Voice and POV distance are intact
- [ ] No reliance on clichéd physiology ("heart raced")

**Red flags:** every feeling labeled; "she felt happy"; converting transitions into overwrought set pieces; physiology lists; purple prose masquerading as "showing."

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Convert necessary summary or transitions — "Three years passed" should stay told
- Replace one cliché ("she was scared") with another ("her heart hammered against her ribs")
- Inflate quiet beats into melodrama
- Strip clarity for the sake of indirection — sometimes the plain statement is best
- Add body sensations for emotions that don't warrant them

**DO:**
- Keep telling where it's the efficient, correct choice and say so
- Vary the showing technique (not all physiology)
- Match intensity of the rendering to the importance of the moment
- Preserve the author's voice and the POV's natural idiom
