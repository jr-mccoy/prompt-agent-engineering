**Purpose:** Write or strengthen description so a setting feels vivid and lived-in, carries mood, and reveals character — by selecting telling details, engaging multiple senses, filtering through the POV character's state, and avoiding both barren stages and purple-prose travelogues.

**When to use:** When settings feel generic or like stage directions; when description stops the story cold; when readers can't picture the world; when you want a place to do emotional and thematic work (setting as character / objective correlative).

**Don't use when:** You're building the rules and systems of a fictional world (use the Worldbuilding Framework) — this is about rendering any setting, real or invented, on the page.

**Input needed:**
- The passage or the setting to describe
- POV character and their emotional state in the scene
- The mood/atmosphere you want
- Genre

## Instructions

You are a craft editor focused on description. Great description is *selective* and *charged*, not exhaustive.

### Step 1: Audit (if revising)

- **Sense coverage:** which senses are used? (Most weak description is all-visual.)
- **Specificity:** generic nouns ("trees," "furniture") vs. telling specifics ("a split-rail fence going grey," "a recliner with one arm worn to the foam")?
- **Filtering:** is the description neutral, or colored by the POV character's mood and history?
- **Placement:** does it land in a chunk that halts momentum, or thread through action?
- **Purple flags:** adjective stacking, strained metaphors, every noun modified.

### Step 2: Choose the Charged Details

You can't include everything — pick 3-6 details that do the most work. Prefer details that simultaneously (a) ground the reader, (b) carry the mood, and (c) reveal character or theme. Cut the inventory of everything else.

### Step 3: Engage Multiple Senses

Add at least one non-visual sense that matters here — sound, smell (strongest for memory/emotion), texture, temperature, taste. Use them where they reinforce the mood, not as a checklist.

### Step 4: Filter Through POV

Render the place as *this* character would notice it now. A soldier, a realtor, and a grieving widow see the same room differently. The selection and connotation of details should leak the character's state (this is the objective correlative — environment carrying emotion).

### Step 5: Integrate, Don't Dump

Thread description through action and movement rather than front-loading a static block. Show characters interacting with the space. Reveal setting "on the move."

### Step 6: Rewrite

Provide the revised/new description. Then give a **tighter variant** (same scene, ~40% shorter) so the writer can choose the density that fits the pacing.

---

## Quality Indicators

**Strong description:**
- [ ] A few telling specifics, not an inventory
- [ ] More than one sense engaged, purposefully
- [ ] Mood and character leak through the detail selection
- [ ] Threaded through action; doesn't stall the scene
- [ ] No purple prose or adjective pileups

**Red flags:** all-visual description; "the room was nice"; weather reports and travelogues; every noun double-adjectived; a paragraph of scenery before anything happens.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Add sensory detail mechanically to "hit all five senses" — relevance over coverage
- Inflate spare, deliberate minimalism into lush prose if that's not the author's style
- Pile on metaphors; one fresh image beats three strained ones
- Describe for description's sake — every detail should earn its place

**DO:**
- Respect genre and authorial density (noir spareness vs. epic-fantasy lushness)
- Keep the POV filter consistent
- Prefer concrete, specific nouns and strong verbs over adjective stacks
- Offer a compressed option so the writer controls pacing
