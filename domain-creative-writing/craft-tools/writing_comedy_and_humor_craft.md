# Comedy & Humor Craft

**Objective:** Diagnose why a comic passage is not landing and rebuild it at
three levels — the premise, the scene's game, and the line — so the humor
comes from character and escalation rather than from inserted jokes.

**When to Use:**
- A comic novel, rom-com, sitcom scene, humorous essay, or light scene in a
  serious work "should be funny and isn't."
- Jokes land individually but scenes do not build.
- You want to add levity to a dark piece without breaking its tone.

**Not this prompt if:**
- Dialogue is flat because it is on-the-nose, not because it is unfunny →
  `domain-creative-writing/craft-tools/writing_dialogue_enhancement.md`.
- You want to develop your overall writing voice →
  `domain-creative-writing/craft-tools/writing_voice_development.md`.
- You need sitcom episode or pilot structure →
  `domain-creative-writing/script-stage/writing_screenplay_framework.md` or
  `writing_tv_pilot_and_series_bible.md`.
- You need marketing copy or brand humor → `domain-professional-writing/` or
  `domain-advertising/`.

## Inputs

1. **The passage or scene** (or an outline of it).
2. **Form** (prose, script, essay, stand-up set, sketch) and **register**
   (dry, absurd, satirical, farce, gentle, dark).
3. **Characters involved**: what each wants in the scene.
4. **What the writer thinks the funny part is.**
5. **Audience** and any lines the writer will not cross.

## Method

1. **Frame register and audience (CM-01).** Restate form, register, audience,
   and the writer's own limits. Comedy that is right for a dark satire is
   wrong for a gentle rom-com; every later judgment uses this frame.
2. **Diagnose at three layers (DT-04).**
   - **Premise layer:** what is the incongruity — the gap between how things
     are and how someone believes they are, or should be?
   - **Character layer:** whose blind spot powers the comedy? Comic characters
     want something intensely and cannot see the obvious thing in their way.
   - **Line layer:** are individual jokes built well (see step 5)?
   Most failures are at the premise or character layer; fixing lines alone rarely rescues them.
3. **Find the game (ST-02).** State the scene's unusual pattern in one
   sentence: "Every time Dev tries to seem relaxed, he makes the situation more
   formal." Then plan three to five **heightening** beats, each a bigger or
   stranger instance of the same pattern, ending in a peak or reversal.
4. **Check status and target.** Who loses status in each beat? Humor in which
   the powerful, the pompous, or the protagonist's own blind spot takes the
   hit generally reads as intended; humor aimed at a group for who they are
   often reads as cruelty. Flag, do not censor — the writer decides.
5. **Build and tune the jokes (NE-04).** For each key joke, show a weak and a
   strong version, applying:
   - **Setup then reveal:** the setup creates an assumption; the reveal breaks it.
   - **Funny word last:** end the sentence on the word that turns it.
   - **Specific beats general:** "a chair" is not funny; "a folding chair
     from a church hall, labelled PROPERTY OF ST. BRIDE'S" may be.
   - **Rule of three:** two establish a pattern, the third breaks it.
   - **Callbacks:** return a joke later in a new context.
   - **Economy:** cut every word between the setup and the reveal that is not
     doing work.
6. **Place the release.** Serious works need levity *after* tension, not in
   place of it; comic works need a moment of real feeling so the laughs matter.
   Mark where each belongs.
7. **Self-check (QA-01).** Read the revised passage for the game, the
   heightening, and the target before reporting.

## Output Format

```
## Frame
Form · Register · Audience · Writer's limits
## Three-layer diagnosis
Premise: [...]  Character blind spot: [...]  Line: [...]
## The game
One-sentence pattern · Heightening beats 1–5 · Peak/reversal
## Status & target check
| Beat | Who loses status | Note |
## Joke rebuilds
| Joke | Weak version | Strong version | Principle applied |
## Release placement
## Revised passage (or beat outline)
## What to try next
```

## Verification

- [ ] The game is stated in one sentence, and every heightening beat follows it.
- [ ] Each heightening beat is bigger or stranger than the last.
- [ ] The comic character's blind spot is named.
- [ ] Each rebuilt joke names the principle applied.
- [ ] The status/target table covers every major beat.
- [ ] The revision keeps the writer's register and limits.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Do not make everything a joke.** Constant quips flatten stakes; a scene
   with one strong laugh can outperform one with ten weak ones.
2. **Do not impose one register.** Dry, understated humor is not "missing
   punchlines"; judge against the stated register.
3. **Do not explain the joke in the revision.** Characters reacting to how
   funny something was, or narration that underlines it, kills it.
4. **Do not treat edgy as automatically bad or automatically brave.** Report
   who the joke lands on and let the writer decide.
5. **Do not import famous bits or catchphrases.** Build original jokes from
   the writer's characters; name a technique, not someone else's material.
6. **Do not assume a laugh line fails because it is quiet.** Smile-level
   humor is the goal in much literary fiction.
7. **Do not add randomness as a fix.** Absurdity works when it follows its own
   logic; random non-sequiturs usually signal a missing game.

## Example

**Input (summary):** Gentle comic novel. Dev, 34, is meeting his partner's
parents for the first time at their allotment. Writer's version: Dev makes
three unrelated jokes; the scene "feels like a sketch."

```
## Frame
Prose novel · gentle, warm · general adult readers · no humiliation of the parents

## Three-layer diagnosis
Premise: a city man trying to impress people whose world he doesn't know.
Character blind spot: Dev thinks being agreeable is the same as being liked.
Line: jokes are Dev's quips, so the reader laughs at his wit, not his predicament.

## The game
"Every time Dev agrees with something to please them, he commits himself to
something worse."
1. Agrees he loves rhubarb → is handed a crumble recipe to "take home and try."
2. Agrees he's "handy" → is given the broken wheelbarrow to fix.
3. Agrees he'd love to see the prize marrow → learns it is being entered in
   the village show tomorrow and needs guarding overnight.
4. Peak: at dusk, alone in the allotment shed with the marrow and a flask,
   he texts his partner: "I think I've been adopted."
Reversal: the father arrives with a second flask — he was doing the same thing
thirty years ago for his own future father-in-law.

## Status & target check
| Beat | Loses status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Dev | Target is his blind spot — fits register |
| Peak | Dev | Warm, not humiliating |
| Reversal | Father (briefly) | Converts the joke to affection |

## Joke rebuilds
| Joke | Weak | Strong | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| The marrow | "It was a very big marrow." | "The marrow had its own blanket." | Specific; funny word last |
| The flask | "He settled in for the night." | "He had a flask, a torch, and no clear idea what a marrow thief looked like." | Rule of three |
| Callback | — | Final chapter: Dev's wedding speech thanks "the marrow, without whom…" | Callback |

## Release placement
The father's arrival is the moment of feeling; no joke for the next half-page.

## What to try next
Cut Dev's three original quips; give the funniest line to the mother, who has
seen through him from beat 1.
```

## Techniques Used

- **CM-01 Explicit Context Framing** — register, audience, and limits.
- **DT-04 Multi-Layer Analysis** — premise, character, and line layers.
- **NE-04 Good vs Bad Example Calibration** — weak and strong joke versions.
- **ST-02 Structured Sequential Instructions** — the game and its heightening.
- **QA-01 Self-Verification** — game, escalation, and target checks.
