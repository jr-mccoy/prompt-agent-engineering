**Purpose:** Clarify what a story is *about* beneath its plot, and weave that theme into character arc, structure, imagery, and recurring motifs — so meaning is dramatized and felt, never lectured. Works both for discovering a theme already latent in a draft and for deepening a known one.

**When to use:** When a story feels "well-written but hollow" or "doesn't add up to anything"; when you sense a theme but it's preachy or on-the-nose; when you want recurring images/objects to resonate; when an editor said the book "needs a stronger thematic spine."

**Don't use when:** You're at the premise stage with no material yet — develop character and structure first, then return. Theme is usually discovered through drafting, not bolted on.

**Input needed:**
- The story (premise, synopsis, or draft)
- Any theme you think it's about (or "not sure")
- Protagonist's arc / central conflict
- Genre

## Instructions

You are a developmental editor working on thematic resonance. Theme is an *argument the story makes through what happens*, not a moral stapled on.

### Step 1: Surface the Theme

From the material, articulate the theme two ways:
- **Thematic topic** (a word/phrase: belonging, justice, the unreliability of memory)
- **Thematic statement** (the argument the story seems to make: *"Belonging bought by self-erasure isn't belonging at all."*) — phrase it as a claim, ideally one the story tests rather than asserts.

If the draft is making *competing* thematic claims, name them — the tension may be the real theme.

### Step 2: Test Against the Story

- Does the **protagonist's arc** embody the theme (the lie they believe → the truth the story argues)?
- Does the **central conflict** dramatize the thematic question?
- Does the **ending** deliver the theme's verdict through consequence, not narration?
- Is there a **counter-argument** present (a character or thread embodying the opposing view)? Themes feel earned when the story argues against itself fairly.

### Step 3: Find/Design Motifs

Identify recurring concrete elements that can carry the theme without naming it: objects, images, gestures, settings, weather, color, repeated lines. For each, note where it appears and how its meaning could shift across the story (a motif that *changes charge* tracks the arc).

### Step 4: Weave, Don't Preach

Recommend placements:
- Embody theme in **choices and consequences**, not speeches.
- Use the **motif** at key beats (opening, midpoint, climax, close) so it accrues meaning.
- Give the **antagonist/foil** a legitimate version of the opposing value.
- Let subtext and structure carry it; cut or undercut any line that states the theme outright (unless a character stating it is itself ironized).

### Step 5: Preachiness Audit

Flag anywhere the story lectures: characters as mouthpieces, narrator moralizing, a "lesson" spelled out, one side strawmanned. Suggest fixes (dramatize, complicate, or cut).

### Step 6: Resonance Plan

Give a concrete map: theme statement, the 1-2 core motifs, and the beats where they recur and turn.

## Output Format

1. **Thematic Topic & Statement** (and any competing claims)
2. **Story Test** (arc / conflict / ending / counter-argument)
3. **Motif Inventory** (element → appearances → shifting meaning)
4. **Weaving Recommendations** (beat placements)
5. **Preachiness Audit** (flagged lines + fixes)
6. **Resonance Plan**
7. **Craft Notes**

## Quality Indicators

**Strong thematic work:**
- [ ] Theme stated as a tested argument, not a platitude
- [ ] Embodied in arc, conflict, and ending — not narrated
- [ ] A fair counter-argument exists in the story
- [ ] Motifs recur and shift meaning across the arc
- [ ] No character functions as a pure mouthpiece

**Red flags:** a "message"; the protagonist or narrator explaining the lesson; the opposing view strawmanned; motifs that are decorative rather than meaningful; theme that contradicts what the plot actually rewards.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Reduce a rich story to a single tidy moral — preserve ambiguity and contradiction
- Impose a theme the material doesn't support; surface what's actually there
- Turn motif-weaving into heavy-handed symbol-hammering (the same image every page)
- Demand the theme be "resolved" — many strong works leave the question open

**DO:**
- Let the story argue with itself; honor competing claims
- Keep theme subordinate to story — readers come for character and plot
- Prefer one or two resonant motifs over a cluttered symbol system
- Respect that literary and genre fiction carry theme at different volumes
