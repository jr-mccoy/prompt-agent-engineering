**Purpose:** Guide writers through the craft of screenwriting — from concept and structure to scene construction, dialogue, and proper formatting for film, television, and stage.

**When to use:** When developing a screenplay, teleplay, or stage play from concept to draft; when adapting prose to screen; when a script feels flat, talky, or structurally weak.

**Input needed:**
- Medium (feature film, TV pilot, short film, stage play)
- Genre and tone
- Concept or logline
- Target audience

## Instructions

### Step 1: Concept Validation

Before structuring, verify the concept is screen-ready:

| Element | Film/TV Requirement | Your Concept |
|---------|---------------------|--------------|
| **Visual potential** | Can this be SHOWN, not just told? | [Assessment] |
| **Active protagonist** | Does the lead DO things (not just think/feel)? | [Assessment] |
| **External conflict** | Is there visible opposition? | [Assessment] |
| **Ticking clock** | Is there urgency or escalating pressure? | [Assessment] |
| **Castable roles** | Would actors want to play these parts? | [Assessment] |

**Key difference from prose:** Screenwriting is a visual medium. If it can't be seen or heard, it doesn't exist on screen. Internal monologue must become external action.

### Step 2: Structure by Medium

**Feature Film (90-120 pages, ~1 page per minute):**

| Beat | Page Range | Purpose | Your Story |
|------|-----------|---------|------------|
| **Opening Image** | 1 | Establish tone, world, "before" | [Scene] |
| **Setup** | 1-10 | Normal world, introduce protagonist | [Scenes] |
| **Catalyst** | 12 | The event that changes everything | [Event] |
| **Debate** | 12-25 | Protagonist resists the call | [Conflict] |
| **Break into Two** | 25 | Protagonist commits, enters new world | [Decision] |
| **B Story** | 30 | Subplot begins (often the love story) | [Subplot] |
| **Fun and Games** | 30-55 | The promise of the premise | [Set pieces] |
| **Midpoint** | 55 | False victory or false defeat; stakes raise | [Shift] |
| **Bad Guys Close In** | 55-75 | Complications mount, allies fracture | [Obstacles] |
| **All Is Lost** | 75 | Lowest point, whiff of death | [Crisis] |
| **Dark Night of the Soul** | 75-85 | Processing loss, finding new resolve | [Realization] |
| **Break into Three** | 85 | New plan based on lessons learned | [Decision] |
| **Finale** | 85-110 | Climactic sequence, all threads resolve | [Climax] |
| **Final Image** | 110 | Mirror of opening — show change | [Scene] |

**TV Pilot — 1-Hour Drama (55-65 pages):**

| Element | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| **Cold Open / Teaser** | Hook immediately — the show's thesis in miniature |
| **Act 1** | Introduce world, protagonist, central conflict |
| **Act 2** | Complicate — nothing is as simple as it seemed |
| **Act 3** | Midpoint reversal — new information changes everything |
| **Act 4** | Escalate toward crisis |
| **Act 5 / Climax** | Resolve THIS episode's story while opening series questions |
| **Tag / Cliffhanger** | Reason to watch next episode |

**Key pilot requirement:** Prove the show is repeatable. The pilot must answer: "What is episode 2? Episode 10? Season 2?"

**TV Pilot — 30-Minute Comedy (28-35 pages):**

| Element | Purpose |
|---------|---------|
| **Cold Open** | Establish tone and comedic voice immediately |
| **Act 1** | Setup — introduce the situation and complication |
| **Act 2** | Escalation — things get worse (and funnier) |
| **Resolution** | Resolve (or hilariously fail to resolve) |
| **Tag** | Final joke, button on the episode |

**Short Film (5-20 pages):**
- One central idea, executed cleanly
- Single location often strongest
- Aim for one powerful emotional beat
- Ending is everything — must land

**Stage Play:**
- Dialogue-driven (less visual action)
- Fewer locations (physical staging constraints)
- Longer speeches acceptable (theater convention)
- Audience is present — direct address and asides are options

### Step 3: Scene Construction

Every scene in a screenplay must:

**Scene Header (Slug Line):**
```
INT. LOCATION - TIME OF DAY

Action description. Present tense. Only what we can see and hear.

            CHARACTER NAME
      Dialogue goes here.
```

**Scene Essentials:**

| Element | Question | If Missing |
|---------|----------|------------|
| **Goal** | What does the POV character want RIGHT NOW? | Scene has no drive |
| **Conflict** | What opposes them IN THIS SCENE? | Scene is flat |
| **Outcome** | Does the scene end Yes, No, Yes-but, or No-and? | Scene has no consequences |
| **Visual storytelling** | What does the CAMERA see that reveals story? | You're writing a novel, not a script |

**Enter late, leave early.** Start after pleasantries. End before the scene resolves completely. Let the audience fill gaps.

### Step 4: Visual Storytelling

**Show, Don't Tell — The Screenwriter's Prime Directive:**

| Instead of... | Show... |
|---------------|---------|
| "She's nervous" | She picks at her cuticles, checks her phone three times |
| "They're in love" | He adjusts her collar without thinking; she lets him |
| "He's dangerous" | Other characters move out of his path instinctively |
| "The town is dying" | Boarded storefronts, a FOR SALE sign sun-bleached to white |

**Visual motifs:** Identify 2-3 recurring visual elements that carry thematic weight:
- Objects (a watch, a photograph, a locked door)
- Colors (shifting palette as story darkens)
- Spatial patterns (distance between characters shrinking/growing)

### Step 5: Screenplay Dialogue

**Screenplay dialogue differs from prose dialogue:**

| Principle | Why |
|-----------|-----|
| **Shorter is better** | Actors add performance; don't over-write |
| **People talk past each other** | Realistic conversations have competing agendas |
| **Subtext over text** | What's NOT said matters more than what is |
| **Each character sounds different** | Cover the names — can you tell who's speaking? |
| **Avoid exposition dumps** | "As you know, Bob..." is the screenwriter's cardinal sin |

**Exposition techniques:**
- Conflict-driven exposition (argue about the information)
- Visual exposition (show the audience, don't tell)
- Discovery scenes (character learns information naturally)
- In media res (start in the middle, fill in gaps later)

### Step 6: Character Through Action

**In screenwriting, character = what they DO, not what they say or think.**

| Character Element | How It's Shown on Screen |
|-------------------|-------------------------|
| **Values** | What they sacrifice for, what they protect |
| **Intelligence** | How they solve problems under pressure |
| **Emotion** | Physical behavior, what they avoid, micro-expressions |
| **Change** | Different choices in similar situations (early vs. late) |
| **Relationships** | Blocking, proximity, who speaks first, who defers |

### Step 7: Pacing and Rhythm

| Pacing Need | Technique |
|-------------|-----------|
| **Speed up** | Short scenes, cross-cutting, montage, dialogue overlap |
| **Slow down** | Long takes, silence, single-location scenes, reaction shots |
| **Build tension** | Delay the reveal, let the audience know more than characters |
| **Release tension** | Comic beat, change of location, a character exhales |

**Page count discipline:**
- Action scenes: 1-3 pages
- Dialogue scenes: 2-5 pages
- Sequences (montage, chase): 3-8 pages
- If a scene runs longer than 5 pages, justify it or split it

## Output Format

Structure the response as:
1. **Concept Assessment** (visual potential, screen-readiness)
2. **Structural Outline** (beat sheet for chosen medium)
3. **Key Scenes** (5-8 pivotal scenes with visual storytelling notes)
4. **Character Through Action** (how each main character is revealed through behavior)
5. **Dialogue Strategy** (voice differentiation, subtext approach)
6. **Visual Motifs** (recurring images that carry theme)
7. **Pacing Map** (where the script accelerates, breathes, and builds)
8. **Format Reminders** (medium-specific formatting notes)

## Quality Indicators

**Strong screenwriting includes:**
- [ ] Every scene can be filmed (no unfilmable internal states)
- [ ] Protagonist drives the action through choices
- [ ] Dialogue has subtext — characters rarely say exactly what they mean
- [ ] Visual storytelling carries as much weight as dialogue
- [ ] Each scene enters late and exits early
- [ ] Structure builds escalating tension toward a satisfying climax

**Common Screenwriting Pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| **"Talking heads"** | Characters sit and discuss | Add physical activity, change location, create interruption |
| **On-the-nose dialogue** | Characters state their feelings directly | Layer in subtext; show through action |
| **Passive protagonist** | Things happen TO the lead | Give them decisions that drive plot |
| **Directing on the page** | Camera angles, actor direction | Imply the shot through scene construction |
| **Novel in script format** | Flowery action lines, internal monologue | Strip to what camera sees and mic hears |
| **Second act sag** | Midpoint is weak or missing | Add a major reversal at the midpoint |

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Apply film structure rigidly to TV or stage (each medium has its own rhythm)
- Suggest adding voiceover as a fix for unclear story (it's usually a crutch)
- Treat page counts as inflexible rules (they're guidelines)
- Impose mainstream Hollywood structure on indie or art-house projects
- Ignore the production reality (budget, castability, location feasibility)

**DO:**
- Ask about the intended medium and audience before advising
- Respect genre conventions while encouraging fresh execution
- Acknowledge that great scripts break rules purposefully
- Consider practical production constraints
- Recommend reading produced scripts in the same genre
