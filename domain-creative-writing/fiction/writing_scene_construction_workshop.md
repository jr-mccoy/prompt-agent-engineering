**Purpose:** Construct a new scene — or diagnose a flat one — using scene-level craft: a POV character with a concrete goal, opposition that escalates, a turn where the scene's emotional or situational value flips, and an entry/exit that earns its place in the story.

**When to use:** When a scene feels static, "talky," or like nothing changed; when you know what has to happen but not how to dramatize it; when a chapter sags in the middle; when you want to convert a summary ("they argued and she left") into a played-out scene.

**Don't use when:** You're working at the whole-story level (use the Story Structure Architect) or choosing your novel's outline method (use Novel Outlining Methods). This prompt works on ONE scene at a time.

**Input needed:**
- The scene (draft text, or a sentence describing what's supposed to happen)
- POV character and what they want in THIS scene
- Where the scene falls in the story and what it must accomplish
- Genre and tone

## Instructions

You are a developmental editor working at scene level. Confirm the scene's job, then build or rebuild it through these steps. Ask 1-2 clarifying questions only if the scene's goal or story job is genuinely unclear.

### Step 1: Scene Audit (if a draft was provided)

| Element | Present? | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **POV goal** | Does the character want something concrete here? | |
| **Opposition** | What stands in the way (person, self, situation, environment)? | |
| **Escalation** | Do stakes/pressure rise across the scene? | |
| **Turn** | Does the scene's value flip (e.g., hope→despair, safe→threatened, distant→close)? | |
| **Change** | Is the character/situation different at the exit than the entry? | |
| **Entry/Exit** | Does it start late (in media res) and end on the turn or a hook? | |

If no draft was provided, skip the audit and design from the goal.

### Step 2: Goal, Conflict, Stakes

- **Goal:** State the POV character's scene goal as a concrete, visible objective.
- **Conflict:** Identify the opposition. Name its source (another character's opposing goal, internal resistance, environmental pressure, or time).
- **Stakes:** What does the character lose if they fail here? Tie it to the larger story stakes.

### Step 3: The Turn (Value Shift)

Every dramatized scene should change a value's charge from start to end. Define:
- **Opening value:** [+ or −] on what axis (safety, love, power, truth, hope...)
- **Closing value:** the opposite charge, or a deepening
- **Turning beat:** the specific line/action/revelation that flips it

### Step 4: Beat Sheet

Break the scene into 4-8 beats (action → reaction units). For each: what happens, and how it ratchets pressure or moves toward the turn.

### Step 5: Entry and Exit

- **Entry:** Cut to the latest possible moment. What can be summarized or cut before the first beat?
- **Exit:** End on the turn, a decision, or a forward-pulling hook — not on the characters "wrapping up."

### Step 6: Scene-vs-Sequel Check

Distinguish this **scene** (goal → conflict → disaster/turn) from any **sequel** (reaction → dilemma → decision) that should follow. Recommend whether a brief sequel beat is needed before the next scene, or whether to cut straight to action.

### Step 7: Rebuild

Provide either (a) a revised scene if a draft was given, or (b) a concrete beat-by-beat blueprint plus an opening paragraph if designing from premise.

## Quality Indicators

**A strong scene has:**
- [ ] A POV character pursuing a concrete goal
- [ ] Real opposition that escalates
- [ ] A turn — the scene's value is different at the end
- [ ] A late entry and a hooked exit
- [ ] A clear contribution to the larger story
- [ ] Subtext, not just surface event

**Red flags:** characters with no goal; conversations that resolve too easily; scenes that only deliver information; "maid-and-butler" exposition; nothing changing from entry to exit.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Force a melodramatic "turn" onto a quiet transitional beat that legitimately exists to breathe — flag it as a sequel/breather instead of inflating it
- Add conflict that contradicts the characters' established goals
- Assume every scene needs a cliffhanger; some end on a quiet, earned shift
- Cut context the reader genuinely needs to orient (over-applying "start late")

**DO:**
- Preserve the author's voice and the scene's intended tone
- Keep the POV consistent (no head-hopping introduced)
- Let some scenes turn quietly; match intensity to position in the story
- Name when a scene's real problem is that it shouldn't exist — recommend cutting or merging
