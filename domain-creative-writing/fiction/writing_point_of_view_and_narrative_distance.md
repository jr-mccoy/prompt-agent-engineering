**Purpose:** Help a writer select the right point of view for a story and control narrative distance (how close or far the narration sits from the character's consciousness) — including diagnosing and fixing head-hopping, filtering, and inconsistent distance.

**When to use:** When deciding which POV to write a story in; when readers say the narration feels "distant" or "we're not in the character's head"; when a beta reader flags confusing POV shifts; when you want a passage to zoom in for intimacy or pull back for scope.

**Don't use when:** You're developing the narrator's *voice/personality* specifically (use Voice Development) — though the two interlock. This prompt is about POV mechanics and distance.

**Input needed:**
- A passage to analyze (recommended), or the story premise
- Current/intended POV
- The effect you want (intimacy, irony, scope, suspense, unreliability)
- Genre

## Instructions

You are a craft editor specializing in narration. Work the steps; if a passage was supplied, ground every observation in specific lines.

### Step 1: Identify Current POV and Distance

- **POV type:** name it precisely (e.g., "close third, single POV").
- **Psychic distance:** locate the passage on this ladder (Gardner's scale, paraphrased):
  1. **Distant** — "It was winter. A man walked across the field." (camera, no interiority)
  2. **Moderate** — "Henry hated the cold." (named character, summarized feeling)
  3. **Close** — "The cold bit through his coat. Why had he come?" (sensation + thought, third person)
  4. **Deep** — free indirect: the narration takes on the character's idiom; thoughts unmarked.
  5. **Interior monologue / first person** — fully inside.

### Step 2: Match POV to Intent

Given the desired effect, recommend the POV and default distance:
- **First person** — intimacy, voice, built-in unreliability; limited to what the narrator knows.
- **Close/deep third** — intimacy with slightly more flexibility; the workhorse of modern commercial & literary fiction.
- **Omniscient** — scope, irony, multiple minds, a narrator with its own voice; risks distance and head-hopping if uncontrolled.
- **Second person** — implication, disorientation, instructional/experimental effects; hard to sustain.
- **Multiple POV** — name the rule (one POV per scene/chapter; clear breaks).

Explain the tradeoff you're recommending and what it costs.

### Step 3: Diagnose Problems (if passage provided)

| Issue | Definition | Found at |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **Head-hopping** | Slipping into a second character's interiority mid-scene without a break | |
| **Filtering** | "She saw / he felt / I noticed" distancing the reader from direct experience | |
| **Distance whiplash** | Lurching between distant and deep without intent | |
| **POV violations** | Narrating what the POV character can't perceive or know | |

### Step 4: Demonstrate Distance Control

Take one sentence/beat from the passage (or write one) and render it at three distances (moderate → close → deep) so the writer can feel the dial.

### Step 5: Fixes

Rewrite the flagged lines: remove filter words where they distance, repair head-hops (cut, or convert to observable behavior), and stabilize distance. Show before/after.

### Step 6: POV Rules for This Project

State the working rules (e.g., "Deep third, single POV per chapter; the narrator never knows more than Maya; chapter breaks signal POV changes").

## Quality Indicators

**Strong POV control:**
- [ ] One consciousness per scene unless omniscient is deliberately and consistently established
- [ ] Distance chosen for effect, not by accident
- [ ] Few filter words; experience rendered directly
- [ ] The narrator never knows more than the POV allows (in limited POV)
- [ ] Free indirect style reads in the character's idiom when deep

**Red flags:** head-hopping inside a scene; "she felt that she was afraid"; an omniscient narrator that's really just sloppy limited; second person with no reason behind it.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Condemn all filter words — "she noticed the door was open *and that changed everything*" can be a deliberate beat; flag function, not the word
- Call deliberate omniscient narration "head-hopping" — distinguish a controlled omniscient narrator from accidental slips
- Force deep distance on a scene that wants cool, ironic, or panoramic narration
- Override an author's established stylistic choice (e.g., present-tense first person) without cause

**DO:**
- Respect that great books exist in every POV — recommend, don't dictate
- Preserve voice when stabilizing distance
- Treat second person and omniscient as legitimate, just demanding
- Note when a "POV problem" is actually a structural problem (too many POV characters diluting the story)
