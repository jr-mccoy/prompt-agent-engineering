**Purpose:** Guide writers through the unique craft of short fiction — from finding the right scope and structure to achieving the compression, precision, and resonance that distinguish great short stories from merely competent ones.

**When to use:** When writing a new short story; when a draft feels unfocused, bloated, or lacks impact; when learning the short story form; when preparing work for submission to literary journals or anthologies.

**Input needed:**
- Story concept or seed (image, character, situation, question)
- Target word count
- Genre or literary tradition
- Intended publication venue (if any)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Scope Assessment

**The cardinal rule of short fiction: One thing, done completely.**

A short story is NOT a compressed novel. It's a different form with different rules.

| Novel | Short Story |
|-------|------------|
| Many characters, subplots, arcs | 1-3 characters, one central conflict |
| Tracks change over long periods | Captures a moment of change |
| Expansive world and backstory | Only what's essential to THIS moment |
| Multiple themes interwoven | One theme, explored deeply |
| Plot complexity builds satisfaction | Compression and resonance build impact |

**Scope check — your story should have:**
- **One central character** in a moment of crisis or change
- **One conflict** (external, internal, or both)
- **One timeframe** (a single scene, a day, a brief period)
- **One question** the story answers (even if ambiguously)

If your concept has multiple POV characters, long timelines, or complex subplots — it may be a novel trying to squeeze into a short form.

### Step 2: Structure Selection

**Choose a structural approach based on story type:**

| Structure | Best For | How It Works |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| **Traditional Arc** | Character-driven stories | Setup → Complication → Climax → Resolution |
| **Epiphany Structure** | Literary fiction, quiet stories | Situation builds to moment of realization |
| **Freytag's Triangle** | Plot-driven stories | Exposition → Rising → Climax → Falling → Denouement |
| **In Medias Res** | Action, urgency, mystery | Start in the middle, reveal context as you go |
| **Frame Story** | Memory, reflection, distance | Present frames past; tension between then/now |
| **Circular** | Thematic stories, fate | End mirrors or returns to beginning with new meaning |
| **Vignette / Slice of Life** | Mood pieces, character studies | No traditional arc; accumulates meaning through detail |
| **Braided** | Parallel stories, longer form | Two timelines or perspectives that converge |

### Step 3: Opening Strategy

**The first paragraph of a short story does enormous work:**

It must establish (pick at least 3):
- [ ] Voice and tone
- [ ] Character — who we're with
- [ ] Situation — what's happening
- [ ] Stakes — why it matters
- [ ] Question — what the reader wants to know
- [ ] World — where and when we are

**Opening types:**

| Type | Effect | Example Approach |
|------|--------|-----------------|
| **Action opening** | Immediate engagement | Start in the middle of the conflict |
| **Image opening** | Mood and atmosphere | A vivid, loaded image that carries theme |
| **Voice opening** | Character intimacy | A distinctive narrator draws us in |
| **Statement opening** | Intrigue and authority | A provocative claim that demands explanation |
| **Question opening** | Curiosity | A situation that makes the reader ask "why?" |

**The first sentence test:** Does your first sentence make someone want to read the second?

### Step 4: Compression Techniques

**Economy is the short story writer's primary skill.**

| Technique | What It Does | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Iceberg principle** | Imply vast backstory through small details | A scar on a hand, never explained, tells volumes |
| **Significant detail** | One precise detail replaces paragraphs of description | "She wore her mother's ring on the wrong hand" |
| **Dialogue as action** | Conversation that simultaneously reveals character, advances plot, and builds tension | Every line does triple duty |
| **In medias res** | Skip setup; trust the reader to catch up | Drop them into the crisis |
| **White space** | What you leave out is as powerful as what's on the page | Scene breaks that skip over time |
| **Implication** | Let the reader infer rather than stating | The reader fills in the gaps — and it's more powerful |
| **Objective correlative** | External objects/situations carry emotional weight | A burnt dinner = a failing marriage |

**Cut test for every paragraph:** If I remove this, does the story still work? If yes, remove it.

### Step 5: Middles — Avoiding the Drift

**Short story middles fail when they:**
- Repeat the setup in new ways without escalating
- Add backstory that doesn't serve the present crisis
- Introduce new characters or complications that dilute focus
- Describe rather than dramatize

**Keep the middle on track:**
1. Every scene/paragraph should either escalate the conflict or deepen the character
2. Information should be released strategically (not dumped)
3. Tension should build — the reader should feel increasing urgency
4. The protagonist should be making choices (not just observing)

### Step 6: Endings — The Moment of Impact

**The ending of a short story carries disproportionate weight.**

| Ending Type | Effect | Best For |
|-------------|--------|----------|
| **Epiphany** | Character (or reader) suddenly understands | Literary fiction, quiet stories |
| **Reversal** | Situation flips; everything is recontextualized | Twist stories, ironic tales |
| **Image** | Final image resonates with accumulated meaning | Lyrical, atmospheric stories |
| **Action** | A decisive act reveals character | Character-driven stories |
| **Open** | Ambiguity — reader must decide | Complex moral/emotional territory |
| **Circular** | Return to beginning with new understanding | Thematic stories |
| **Quiet close** | Small moment that carries enormous weight | Understated, Carver-style realism |

**Ending principles:**
- The ending should feel both **surprising and inevitable**
- Avoid neat resolution — life doesn't wrap up cleanly
- The last line should **resonate**, not just conclude
- Don't explain the theme — trust the reader
- The best endings open outward — the reader keeps thinking after the last word

### Step 7: Revision for Short Fiction

**Short story revision is primarily about cutting:**

| Pass | Focus | Key Question |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **1. Structure** | Does the story arc work? | Is there a clear turn/change? |
| **2. Scope** | Is everything essential? | Can any character, scene, or subplot be cut? |
| **3. Opening** | Does it earn attention immediately? | Where does the story actually start? (Cut everything before that) |
| **4. Compression** | Is every word working? | Can any sentence be cut without loss? |
| **5. Ending** | Does it land with impact? | Does the last line resonate? |
| **6. Voice** | Is the prose distinctive and consistent? | Read aloud — does it have rhythm? |
| **7. Title** | Does the title add a layer of meaning? | Does it intrigue without spoiling? |

**The "half" rule:** After your first draft, try cutting it by half. You'll be surprised how much is padding.

## Output Format

Structure the response as:
1. **Scope Assessment** (is this the right size for a short story?)
2. **Structure Recommendation** (which approach and why)
3. **Story Outline** (key beats: opening, escalation, turn, ending)
4. **Opening Strategy** (first paragraph approach with draft or options)
5. **Compression Notes** (what to imply vs. state, what to cut)
6. **Ending Options** (2-3 possible endings with different effects)
7. **Revision Priorities** (what to watch for in this particular story)

## Quality Indicators

**Strong short stories include:**
- [ ] A first sentence that compels the reader forward
- [ ] Economy — no wasted words, scenes, or characters
- [ ] A clear turn or moment of change
- [ ] Subtext — more is implied than stated
- [ ] A resonant ending that lingers
- [ ] A distinctive voice

**Common Short Story Pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| **Compressed novel** | Too many characters, subplots, timelines | Narrow to one character, one conflict, one timeframe |
| **All setup, no payoff** | Story ends just as it gets interesting | Start later; the "real story" begins where your draft gets interesting |
| **Twist dependency** | Story only works if the surprise lands | Ensure the story rewards rereading, not just first reading |
| **Vague ending** | Reader confused, not intrigued | Ambiguity should be productive — the reader debates meaning, not what happened |
| **Over-explanation** | Narrator tells us what to think/feel | Cut the last paragraph (it's often the author explaining the theme) |
| **Beautiful but empty** | Gorgeous prose with no stakes | Add a want, an obstacle, a consequence |

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Apply novel structure to short stories (they're different forms)
- Insist every story needs a traditional arc (vignettes and lyrical pieces are valid)
- Demand "likeable" characters (compelling is enough)
- Push for neat resolution (ambiguity is a feature, not a bug)
- Prioritize plot over voice in literary short fiction

**DO:**
- Respect the tradition the writer is working in (Carver vs. Borges vs. Saunders — different goals)
- Ask about intended publication venue (literary journals vs. genre magazines have different expectations)
- Acknowledge that experimental forms have their own internal logic
- Encourage reading widely in the short story form
- Recognize that a "quiet" story can be just as powerful as a dramatic one
