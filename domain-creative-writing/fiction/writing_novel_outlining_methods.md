**Purpose:** Help novelists choose and apply an outlining method that fits their process — from highly structured beat sheets to flexible discovery-based approaches — to plan a complete novel before or during drafting.

**When to use:** When starting a new novel and need a roadmap; when stuck mid-draft without a clear direction; when deciding between plotting methods; when converting a concept into a full-length structure.

**Input needed:**
- Genre and target word count
- Premise or concept
- How you prefer to work (plotter, pantser, or plantser)
- What you already have (characters, scenes, ending, theme)

## Instructions

### Step 1: Method Selection

Choose the method that best matches your working style and genre:

| Method | Best For | Structure Level | Process |
|--------|----------|----------------|---------|
| **Snowflake Method** | Systematic plotters, complex novels | Very High | Build from 1 sentence → full outline |
| **Save the Cat! Beats** | Commercial fiction, screenwriter-minded | High | 15 specific beats with page targets |
| **Three-Act Structure** | All genres, classic approach | Medium-High | Setup → Confrontation → Resolution |
| **Hero's Journey** | Fantasy, adventure, quest narratives | Medium-High | 12 mythic stages |
| **Seven-Point Story Structure** | Plot-driven genre fiction | Medium | 7 key turning points |
| **Story Grid** | Thriller, mystery, genre fiction | High | Scene-by-scene value tracking |
| **Romancing the Beat** | Romance specifically | High | Romance-specific beat sheet |
| **Scene-Sequel Method** | Character-driven fiction | Medium | Alternating action and reaction |
| **Dan Harmon's Story Circle** | Any genre, character-arc focused | Medium | 8-step circular structure |
| **The Plantser Method** | Discovery writers who need guardrails | Low-Medium | Key milestones + open space |

### Step 2: Apply Chosen Method

---

#### Method A: Snowflake Method (Randy Ingermanson)

**Build from small to large in 10 steps:**

**Step 1 — One-Sentence Summary:**
Write your novel in one sentence (25 words max). No character names — use descriptors.
> Example: "A hobbit must carry an evil ring across a war-torn continent to destroy it in the volcano where it was forged."

**Step 2 — One-Paragraph Summary:**
Expand to 5 sentences:
1. Setup and backdrop
2. First quarter — first disaster
3. Second quarter — second disaster
4. Third quarter — third disaster
5. Ending

**Step 3 — Character Summaries:**
For each major character, write:
- Name and one-line description
- Motivation (what do they want abstractly?)
- Goal (what concrete thing do they pursue?)
- Conflict (what prevents them?)
- Epiphany (what do they learn?)
- One-paragraph summary of their storyline

**Step 4 — Expand to One Page:**
Expand each sentence from Step 2 into a full paragraph (5 paragraphs total).

**Step 5 — Character Synopses:**
Write a one-page synopsis from each major character's POV.

**Step 6 — Expand to Four Pages:**
Expand each paragraph from Step 4 into a full page.

**Step 7 — Character Charts:**
Full character profiles for every character.

**Step 8 — Scene List:**
Build a spreadsheet of every scene:
| # | POV | Scene Summary | Chapter |
|---|-----|---------------|---------|

**Step 9 — Scene Descriptions:**
Write a multi-paragraph description of each scene.

**Step 10 — First Draft:**
Write the novel.

#### Method B: Save the Cat! Beats (Blake Snyder / Jessica Brody)

**15 beats mapped to a novel:**

| # | Beat | % of Novel | Word Count (80K) | Purpose |
|---|------|-----------|------------------|---------|
| 1 | **Opening Image** | 0-1% | 0-800 | Snapshot of "before" |
| 2 | **Theme Stated** | ~5% | ~4,000 | Someone hints at the lesson |
| 3 | **Setup** | 1-10% | 800-8,000 | Normal world, introduce characters, plant setups |
| 4 | **Catalyst** | 10% | ~8,000 | The life-changing event |
| 5 | **Debate** | 10-20% | 8,000-16,000 | Should I go? Can I do this? |
| 6 | **Break Into Two** | 20% | ~16,000 | Protagonist enters the new world |
| 7 | **B Story** | 22% | ~17,600 | Subplot begins (often relationship) |
| 8 | **Fun and Games** | 20-50% | 16,000-40,000 | The promise of the premise |
| 9 | **Midpoint** | 50% | ~40,000 | False victory or false defeat; stakes raise |
| 10 | **Bad Guys Close In** | 50-75% | 40,000-60,000 | Things get worse, allies fracture |
| 11 | **All Is Lost** | 75% | ~60,000 | The lowest moment |
| 12 | **Dark Night of the Soul** | 75-80% | 60,000-64,000 | Wallowing, processing, realizing |
| 13 | **Break Into Three** | 80% | ~64,000 | New plan, merging A and B stories |
| 14 | **Finale** | 80-99% | 64,000-79,200 | Climax, applying lessons, resolution |
| 15 | **Final Image** | 99-100% | 79,200-80,000 | Proof of transformation |

#### Method C: Seven-Point Story Structure (Dan Wells)

**Work backward from the ending:**

| Point | Definition | Your Story |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| **1. Hook** | Where the character starts (opposite of Resolution) | [Starting state] |
| **2. Plot Turn 1** | Introduction of conflict; call to adventure | [What changes] |
| **3. Pinch Point 1** | Pressure applied; villain shows strength | [Force applied] |
| **4. Midpoint** | Character shifts from reaction to action | [The pivot] |
| **5. Pinch Point 2** | More pressure; all seems lost | [Jaws tighten] |
| **6. Plot Turn 2** | Final piece of the puzzle; hero gets what they need | [The key] |
| **7. Resolution** | Climax and new status quo | [Ending state] |

**Order of creation:** Start with Resolution → Hook → Midpoint → Plot Turn 1 → Plot Turn 2 → Pinch Points

#### Method D: The Plantser Method

**For discovery writers who need minimal guardrails:**

Define only these 5 anchors before writing:

1. **Opening:** The first scene or image (how does the world feel at the start?)
2. **Inciting Incident:** What disrupts the protagonist's life?
3. **Midpoint:** What changes everything halfway through?
4. **Crisis:** What is the darkest moment?
5. **Ending:** Where does the story land? (Even a vague sense is enough)

**Between anchors:** Write toward the next anchor. Discovery happens in the spaces between.

**Safety net questions (check every 10,000 words):**
- Is the protagonist still driving the story?
- Are stakes escalating?
- Am I still heading toward (or revising) my next anchor?

### Step 3: Chapter Planning

Once structure is set, plan chapters:

**Chapter Template:**

| Chapter | Scenes | POV | Purpose | Word Count Target |
|---------|--------|-----|---------|-------------------|
| 1 | [Scene list] | [POV character] | [What it accomplishes] | [Target] |
| 2 | [Scene list] | [POV character] | [What it accomplishes] | [Target] |

**Chapter pacing guidelines:**
- Average chapter: 2,000-5,000 words (genre-dependent)
- Thriller/suspense: shorter chapters (1,500-3,000)
- Literary/epic fantasy: longer chapters OK (3,000-6,000)
- End chapters on hooks, questions, or shifts

### Step 4: Subplot Integration

Map subplots to the main plot:

| Subplot | Characters | Theme Connection | Begins | Intersects Main Plot | Resolves |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|--------|---------------------|----------|
| [Name] | [Who] | [How it reflects theme] | [Chapter/beat] | [Where] | [When] |

**Rules for subplots:**
- Each subplot should reflect or complicate the main theme
- Subplots should intersect the main plot (not run in parallel forever)
- Resolve most subplots before or during the climax
- 2-4 subplots for a standard novel; more for epic/saga

### Step 5: Timeline and Continuity

Create a story timeline:

| Day/Date | Chapter | Events | Character Locations |
|----------|---------|--------|---------------------|
| [When] | [Ch #] | [What happens] | [Who is where] |

**Continuity checks:**
- Travel times between locations
- Character knowledge (who knows what when)
- Seasonal/weather consistency
- Wound/injury tracking
- Relationship status changes

## Output Format

Structure the response as:
1. **Method Recommendation** (which method and why it fits)
2. **Complete Beat Sheet / Outline** (using chosen method)
3. **Chapter Breakdown** (scene-by-scene or chapter-by-chapter)
4. **Subplot Map** (how subplots weave through main plot)
5. **Character Arc Tracking** (where key characters change)
6. **Timeline** (if needed for the story)
7. **Gap Analysis** (what's still undefined and needs discovery)

## Quality Indicators

**Strong novel outline includes:**
- [ ] Clear protagonist goal with escalating obstacles
- [ ] A midpoint that genuinely shifts the story's direction
- [ ] Stakes that increase through every act
- [ ] Subplots that connect to theme
- [ ] An ending that feels both surprising and inevitable
- [ ] Room for discovery (not so rigid it kills creativity)

**Common Outlining Mistakes:**

| Mistake | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| **Over-outlining** | Outline is so detailed the writing feels mechanical | Leave gaps for discovery in scenes |
| **Under-outlining** | Got lost at 30,000 words with no direction | Add midpoint and ending anchors at minimum |
| **No midpoint shift** | Second act sags, reader gets bored | Ensure midpoint changes the game |
| **Episodic structure** | Things happen but don't build | Verify each event causes the next |
| **Climax doesn't require growth** | Protagonist could have solved this in chapter 1 | Ensure the climax requires what was learned |
| **Missing emotional arc** | Plot moves but feelings don't | Track internal change alongside external events |

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Insist every writer needs a detailed outline (some write best by discovery)
- Apply one genre's pacing to another (romance ≠ thriller ≠ literary)
- Treat beat sheet percentages as rigid laws
- Suggest the writer plan every scene before writing any
- Dismiss pantsing as inferior to plotting

**DO:**
- Match the method to the writer's natural process
- Acknowledge that outlines evolve during drafting
- Encourage writers to hold their outline loosely
- Suggest the minimum structure needed for confidence
- Respect that different novels need different levels of planning
