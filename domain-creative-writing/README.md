## Domain-Specific Considerations

### What Makes Creative Writing Unique

Creative writing prompts operate in environments where:

1. **Subjectivity is Central** - "Good" writing is largely a matter of taste
2. **Voice Matters** - The author's unique style should be preserved/developed
3. **Rules Can Be Broken** - Conventions exist to be violated purposefully
4. **Creativity Can't Be Forced** - Prompts guide but don't dictate
5. **Show, Don't Tell** - Demonstrating through scene beats instruction
6. **Emotion is the Goal** - The reader's experience is the measure of success
7. **Revision is Essential** - First drafts are starting points, not endpoints

### The Creative Writing Difference

| Dimension | Business/Technical Writing | Creative Writing |
|-----------|---------------------------|------------------|
| **Goal** | Inform, persuade | Evoke, transport, illuminate |
| **Voice** | Clear, professional, consistent | Distinctive, varied, personal |
| **Rules** | Follow conventions | Use conventions strategically |
| **Structure** | Standardized formats | Organic to the story's needs |
| **Success Metric** | Task accomplished | Emotional/aesthetic impact |
| **Editing** | Clarity, accuracy | Sound, rhythm, resonance |

### Critical Success Factors

1. **Preserve Author Voice** - Don't impose a style; develop the writer's own
2. **Serve the Story** - All advice should serve the narrative's goals
3. **Specific Over Generic** - Concrete details, not abstract prescriptions
4. **Ask Before Prescribing** - Understand intention before suggesting changes
5. **Enable, Don't Replace** - Help writers develop their own abilities
6. **Respect Genre** - Different genres have different conventions

### Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Example | Prevention |
|---------|---------|------------|
| **Imposing style** | Making all writing sound the same | Ask about desired voice first |
| **Generic advice** | "Add more conflict" | Specify: what kind, where, how |
| **Ignoring genre** | Literary advice for genre fiction | Understand audience expectations |
| **Overwriting** | Adding unnecessary purple prose | Match tone to story needs |
| **Killing voice** | Editing out unique quirks | Distinguish errors from style |
| **Premature editing** | Perfecting prose before story works | Structure first, polish later |

## Quality Indicators for Creative Writing

### What "Good" Looks Like

**A high-quality creative writing prompt output:**

1. **Respects Author Intention**
   - Asks about genre, audience, and goals first
   - Suggestions serve the author's vision
   - Preserves unique voice elements

2. **Is Specific and Concrete**
   - Gives actionable examples, not abstract principles
   - Shows, doesn't just tell
   - Provides options rather than single prescriptions

3. **Understands Genre**
   - Knows the conventions of the form
   - Applies appropriate standards
   - Acknowledges when conventions can be broken

4. **Enables Development**
   - Helps the author grow, not just fixes the text
   - Teaches principles, not just solutions
   - Builds craft, not dependence

5. **Balances Criticism and Support**
   - Identifies what's working as well as what's not
   - Criticism is constructive and specific
   - Respects the creative process's difficulty

### Creative Quality Framework

```markdown
## Evaluating Creative Writing

**Story Level:**
- Does the story have a clear arc (beginning, middle, end)?
- Is there meaningful change (character, situation, or understanding)?
- Are stakes clear and compelling?
- Does the pacing serve the story?

**Scene Level:**
- Does each scene have a purpose?
- Is there tension or conflict in each scene?
- Do scenes advance plot, character, or theme?
- Are transitions smooth?

**Character Level:**
- Are characters motivated by something the reader can understand?
- Do characters have distinct voices?
- Do characters change or reveal hidden depths?
- Are character choices consistent with who they are?

**Prose Level:**
- Is the writing clear (unless obscurity is intentional)?
- Does the prose have rhythm and flow?
- Is the voice consistent and distinctive?
- Are there strong, specific details?

**Reader Experience:**
- Will the reader care about what happens?
- Are there moments of emotional impact?
- Is the reading experience appropriate to the genre?
- Does it deliver on its promises (genre, tone, setup)?
```

### False-Positive Prevention for Creative Writing

**DON'T:**

- Impose a single "correct" style
- Apply literary standards to genre fiction (or vice versa)
- Suggest changes that would erase unique voice
- Give vague advice ("make it more interesting")
- Prioritize polish over structure
- Assume the first interpretation is right
- Treat all creative work the same regardless of stage

**DO:**

- Ask about genre, audience, and goals first
- Give specific, actionable suggestions
- Acknowledge what's working, not just problems
- Respect intentional rule-breaking
- Understand the stage of the draft (early vs. polishing)
- Offer options rather than single prescriptions
- Teach principles behind suggestions

## Worked Example

### Scenario: Developing a Villain Character

**User says:** "Help me create a villain for my fantasy novel"

**Step 1: Classification**

- Task Type: CREATE
- Domain: Creative Writing
- Specific Type: Character development

**Step 2: Context Gathering**

**What we need to know:**
- Genre specifics (epic fantasy? urban fantasy? dark fantasy?)
- Protagonist (to create meaningful opposition)
- Theme (villain should relate to theme)
- Tone (serious, humorous, morally gray?)
- Story role (main antagonist, secondary villain?)

**Step 3: Approach**

Rather than just generating a villain, guide the writer to develop one that serves THEIR story.

**Final Prompt Approach:**

```markdown
# Villain Development: Questions First

Before I help create your villain, let me understand your story better. The best villains are designed to challenge your specific protagonist and illuminate your theme.

## Questions to Answer First

**About Your Story:**
1. What genre/subgenre of fantasy? (Epic, urban, dark, YA, etc.)
2. What's the tone? (Grim, adventurous, morally complex, hopeful?)
3. What's the central theme? (Power corrupts, love conquers, etc.)

**About Your Protagonist:**
1. What's your protagonist's core belief or value?
2. What's their greatest fear?
3. What's their biggest flaw?

**About Conflict:**
1. What kind of opposition do you need? (Physical threat, ideological opponent, corrupted mirror, etc.)
2. Should the villain be sympathetic? Understandable? Pure evil?
3. Do you want the villain to have a point (even if wrong)?

## Why This Matters

The most compelling villains are designed in relationship to:

**The Protagonist:**
- They should challenge the hero's specific weaknesses
- They might represent what the hero could become
- They should be capable of defeating this particular hero

**The Theme:**
- If your theme is "power corrupts," your villain embodies that corruption
- If your theme is "hope persists," your villain represents despair
- They should be a living argument against the hero's worldview

**The Genre:**
- Epic fantasy: Larger-than-life, world-threatening
- Dark fantasy: Morally complex, possibly sympathetic
- YA fantasy: Relatable motivation, clear but not simplistic

## Once I Know Your Answers

I'll help you develop:
1. **Motivation** - What do they want? Why? What's the wound?
2. **Methods** - How do they pursue goals? What lines do they cross?
3. **Relationship to Hero** - Why are they perfect opposition?
4. **Complexity** - What makes them more than "evil for evil's sake"?
5. **Arc** - How do they change (or fail to change)?

**What's your story about?**
```

## Anti-Patterns for Creative Writing

### Mistake 1: Imposing a Single Style

**Problem:** Making all writing sound the same

**Bad:**
```
"Your prose should be more literary. Add more metaphors and vary your sentence length."
(Applied regardless of genre or author's voice)
```

**Good:**
```
"What style are you aiming for? If you're writing fast-paced thriller, your punchy sentences work well. If you want a more literary feel, we could experiment with varying rhythm. What feels right for YOUR story?"
```

---

### Mistake 2: Generic Feedback

**Problem:** Advice too vague to be actionable

**Bad:**
```
"The middle section drags. You need to add more conflict and make it more interesting."
```

**Good:**
```
"The scene between pages 45-52 (the dinner conversation) slows momentum because nothing changes—they start and end in the same place. Consider: What if the protagonist learns something that changes their plan? What if the conversation reveals a betrayal? The reader needs a reason to feel this scene matters."
```

---

### Mistake 3: Ignoring Genre

**Problem:** Applying wrong standards for the form

**Bad:**
```
(To a romance writer): "The love interest is introduced too early and gets too much page time."
(To a thriller writer): "The pacing is too fast; slow down to develop character more."
```

**Good:**
```
"In romance, readers expect to meet the love interest early and see substantial relationship development. In thriller, pacing is often rapid by design. Let's make sure we're evaluating against what YOUR genre's readers expect."
```
