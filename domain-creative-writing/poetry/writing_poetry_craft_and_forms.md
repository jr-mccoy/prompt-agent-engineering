**Purpose:** Guide poets through the craft of writing poetry — from understanding traditional forms and meter to developing imagery, sound, and meaning in free verse and formal verse alike.

**When to use:** When writing a new poem and choosing a form; when studying poetic techniques; when revising a poem for stronger imagery, rhythm, or emotional impact; when exploring unfamiliar forms.

**Input needed:**
- What you want to write about (subject, emotion, experience)
- Your experience level with poetry
- Whether you want formal (sonnets, villanelles) or free verse
- Desired tone and emotional effect

## Instructions

### Step 1: Form Selection

If the poet is unsure, recommend a form based on their subject and intent:

| Form | Best For | Structure | Example Poets |
|------|----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Free Verse** | Personal expression, narrative, contemporary voice | No fixed meter or rhyme; line breaks create rhythm | Mary Oliver, Ocean Vuong |
| **Sonnet** | Argument, turn of thought, love, contradiction | 14 lines, iambic pentameter, volta | Shakespeare, Terrance Hayes |
| **Villanelle** | Obsession, grief, circular thinking | 19 lines, 2 refrains, ABA pattern | Dylan Thomas, Elizabeth Bishop |
| **Haiku** | Precise observation, nature, a single moment | 3 lines (5-7-5 syllables traditionally) | Bashō, Issa |
| **Ghazal** | Longing, loss, spiritual yearning | Couplets with refrain and rhyme | Agha Shahid Ali, Rumi |
| **Pantoum** | Memory, dreamlike recursion, layered meaning | Quatrains with repeating lines | Nellie Wong, Peter Meinke |
| **Ode** | Praise, celebration, deep attention to a subject | Variable; elevated tone | Pablo Neruda, Sharon Olds |
| **Ballad** | Storytelling, folk tradition, narrative verse | Quatrains, ABAB or ABCB rhyme | Anonymous folk ballads, Gwendolyn Brooks |
| **Prose Poem** | Blurring prose and poetry, dreamlike narrative | Paragraph form, no line breaks | Claudia Rankine, Russell Edson |
| **Elegy** | Mourning, loss, remembrance | Variable; moves through grief toward consolation | W.H. Auden, Jack Gilbert |

**Recommendation reasoning:** Match form to emotional logic. A villanelle's repetition suits obsessive grief. A sonnet's volta suits an argument or reversal. Free verse suits discovery.

### Step 2: Core Craft Elements

Evaluate and develop these elements regardless of form:

**Imagery — The Foundation**

| Type | What It Does | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Visual** | Creates pictures | "the sun-bleached jawbone of a horse" |
| **Auditory** | Creates sounds | "the hiss of tires on wet asphalt" |
| **Tactile** | Creates texture/touch | "sand gritty under her nails" |
| **Olfactory** | Creates smell | "woodsmoke and pine sap" |
| **Gustatory** | Creates taste | "the iron tang of blood" |
| **Kinesthetic** | Creates movement/body sensation | "her stomach dropping like an elevator" |

**Principle:** Engage at least 2-3 senses. Concrete beats abstract. "Grief" is abstract; "the empty chair at the kitchen table" is concrete.

**Figurative Language**

| Device | Function | When to Use |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| **Metaphor** | Identity — X IS Y | When you want to transform understanding |
| **Simile** | Comparison — X is LIKE Y | When you want to illuminate while keeping distance |
| **Personification** | Human qualities to non-human | When landscape/objects reflect emotion |
| **Synecdoche** | Part for whole | When a detail can carry the weight of the whole |
| **Metonymy** | Associated thing for the thing | When indirection is more powerful |

**Sound — The Music of Poetry**

| Device | What It Does | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Alliteration** | Repeating initial consonants | "black branch, bare bone" |
| **Assonance** | Repeating vowel sounds | "the low moaning of the old road" |
| **Consonance** | Repeating consonant sounds (any position) | "the stroke of luck" |
| **Enjambment** | Line breaks mid-phrase, creates momentum | "I wanted to tell you / everything" |
| **End-stop** | Line ends with punctuation, creates pause | "The door closed. That was all." |
| **Caesura** | Mid-line pause | "I looked — and there it was" |
| **Anaphora** | Repeating opening words | "I have been... / I have been... / I have been..." |

### Step 3: Line and Stanza Craft

**Line Breaks — The Poet's Primary Tool:**

- **Break on surprise:** End the line where the next line shifts meaning
- **Break on tension:** Leave the reader leaning into the next line
- **Break on breath:** Create natural pauses that serve pacing
- **Break on image:** Let an image land before moving to the next

**Test each line break by asking:**
1. Does breaking here create tension or surprise?
2. Does the line stand as a meaningful unit on its own?
3. Does the enjambment add energy or meaning?

**Stanza Structure:**
- **Couplets:** Tight, epigrammatic, good for argument
- **Tercets:** Forward momentum, good for narrative
- **Quatrains:** Balanced, traditional, good for song-like poems
- **Irregular stanzas:** Organic, shaped by content
- **Single stanza (block):** Relentless, claustrophobic, immersive

### Step 4: Meter and Rhythm (If Applicable)

**Common Meters:**

| Meter | Pattern | Feel |
|-------|---------|------|
| **Iambic** | da-DUM | Natural English speech |
| **Trochaic** | DUM-da | Forceful, incantatory |
| **Anapestic** | da-da-DUM | Rolling, galloping |
| **Dactylic** | DUM-da-da | Waltz-like, formal |
| **Spondaic** | DUM-DUM | Heavy, emphatic (used for effect, not whole poems) |

**Line Lengths:**
- **Pentameter** (5 feet): Conversational, spacious
- **Tetrameter** (4 feet): Songlike, brisk
- **Trimeter** (3 feet): Compressed, intense
- **Dimeter** (2 feet): Spare, breathless

**For free verse:** You're not ignoring rhythm — you're creating your own. Read the poem aloud. Where does it speed up? Slow down? Where does the rhythm reinforce meaning?

### Step 5: Emotional Architecture

**Build the poem's emotional arc:**

1. **Entry:** Where does the reader enter the emotional landscape?
2. **Deepening:** How does the poem move deeper or complicate the initial feeling?
3. **Turn (Volta):** Where does understanding shift? (Even free verse benefits from a turn)
4. **Landing:** Where does the poem arrive — and is it different from where it started?

**The turn is the most important moment in a poem.** It's where the poem earns its existence — the moment of surprise, reversal, or deepened understanding.

### Step 6: Revision Checklist for Poetry

| Check | Question |
|-------|----------|
| **First line** | Does it pull the reader in immediately? |
| **Last line** | Does it resonate, surprise, or land with weight? |
| **Every line** | Does each line earn its place? Could any be cut? |
| **Abstractions** | Have you replaced abstractions with concrete images? |
| **Sound** | Read aloud — does the music serve the meaning? |
| **Line breaks** | Does every break create energy, tension, or meaning? |
| **The turn** | Is there a moment where the poem shifts or deepens? |
| **Clichés** | Have you found and replaced every tired phrase? |
| **Ending** | Does it avoid tying a neat bow? Does it open outward? |

## Quality Indicators

**Strong poetry includes:**
- [ ] Concrete, sensory imagery (not abstractions)
- [ ] A turn or shift in understanding
- [ ] Intentional line breaks that create meaning
- [ ] Sound patterns that reinforce content
- [ ] An authentic voice (not forced or imitative)
- [ ] An ending that earns its weight

**Common Poetry Pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| **Abstract fog** | "Love is beautiful and eternal" | Replace with concrete image: "the dent in his pillow" |
| **Greeting card syndrome** | Neat rhyme, tidy sentiment | Break the pattern; let the poem surprise you |
| **Forced rhyme** | Unnatural word order to hit a rhyme | Use slant rhyme or drop the rhyme scheme |
| **Overexplaining** | Telling the reader what to feel | Trust the image to carry the emotion |
| **No stakes** | Pleasant description without tension | Find the conflict, contradiction, or question |
| **Purple prose** | Piling on adjectives and metaphors | Strip to essentials; one strong image beats three weak ones |

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Impose rhyme where it doesn't serve the poem
- Treat syllable counts as more important than rhythm and meaning
- Apply one era's standards to all poetry (Romantic rules ≠ contemporary norms)
- Suggest making the poem "clearer" when ambiguity is intentional
- Flatten experimental forms into conventional structures

**DO:**
- Ask what the poet is trying to achieve before suggesting changes
- Respect the tradition the poet is working in
- Distinguish between unclear writing and productive ambiguity
- Encourage reading widely in the form they're attempting
- Acknowledge that poetry is the most subjective literary form
