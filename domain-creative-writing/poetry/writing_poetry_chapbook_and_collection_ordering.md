# Chapbook and Collection Ordering

**Objective:** Help a poet build a book out of poems: decide which poems belong,
which need one more revision, and which go; find what the book is *about* as a whole;
put the poems in an order where each one changes how the next is read; and catch the
habits that only show when thirty poems sit side by side.

**When to Use:**
- You have 20–80 finished or nearly finished poems and want a chapbook or a first
  full-length collection.
- A manuscript keeps getting declined and you suspect the order or the selection,
  not the individual poems.
- You are preparing a submission and need the order, sections, title, and
  acknowledgments page settled.
- **Not this prompt if** you are revising a single poem — use
  `domain-creative-writing/poetry/writing_poetry_craft_and_forms.md` (craft and
  revision checklist), `poetry/writing_imagery_and_figurative_language.md` (images),
  or `poetry/writing_poetry_meter_scansion_and_fixed_forms.md` (meter and form). For a
  verse *novel* for young readers, use
  `domain-childrens-writing/fiction-workshops/childrens_verse_novel_workshop.md`. For
  prose story collections or a novel manuscript, use
  `domain-creative-writing/craft-tools/writing_revision_and_self_editing.md`.

> **No-fabrication guard:** this prompt does not name presses, contests, judges,
> reading periods, entry fees, or page limits as fact. Page ranges below are common
> conventions to confirm against each press's or contest's current guidelines
> `[VERIFY]`. Acknowledgments list only publications the poet supplies.

## Inputs / Context

1. **The poems** (full text), or a list with title, length in pages, and a one-line
   description of each.
2. **Target**: chapbook (commonly about 16–40 pages of poems) or full-length
   (commonly about 48–100 pages) `[VERIFY against target guidelines]`.
3. **What the poet thinks the book is about**, in a sentence, even if unsure.
4. **Poems the poet will not cut** and poems they already doubt.
5. **Prior publication** of individual poems (journal and issue, as supplied).

## Method

1. **Build a card for every poem (ST-02).** Title · pages · mode (lyric, narrative,
   list, prose poem, persona) · form · dominant images and words · emotional
   temperature (cool, warm, hot) · speaker and addressee · how it opens and how it
   ends.
2. **Score each poem on anchored scales (DP-03)** before discussing order:
   | Criterion | 1 | 3 | 5 |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Finish | draft-level; obvious slack | one or two weak lines | no line you'd change |
   | Necessity | another poem does this better | overlaps a neighbour | does something no other poem does |
   | Fit | unrelated to the book's concern | tangential | central to it |
   Keep 4–5 on all three. Revise-then-keep when finish is low but necessity and fit
   are high. Cut when necessity is 1 — a good poem that repeats a better one weakens
   both. Fit 1 poems go to a different manuscript, not the bin.
3. **Name the through-line.** From the kept poems, write one sentence for what the
   book does that no single poem does ("a year of selling a father's boat, read as
   an inventory of what can and cannot be sold"). If the sentence needs "and also",
   there may be two books.
4. **Choose an ordering logic.** Narrative or seasonal arc; sections by mode or
   subject; braided threads that alternate; or a musical order that varies length,
   form, and temperature. Chapbooks rarely need sections; full-length books often
   use 2–4.
5. **Place the anchors.** The opening poem teaches the reader how to read the book
   (its mode and stakes) — it is not necessarily the best poem. Put strong poems in
   the first three, because editors and judges read the opening closely. The last
   poem opens outward rather than summarising. Decide where the title poem sits.
6. **Sequence by adjacency (RT-06).** For each consecutive pair, name the link:
   *echo* (a shared image, word, or object), *contrast* (long after short, hot after
   cool, prose poem after sonnet), or *hinge* (the last line of one is answered by
   the first of the next). Flag pairs with no link and runs of three or more poems
   with the same form, length, or temperature.
7. **Audit manuscript-level tics.** Count, across all kept poems: repeated key words
   ("bone", "light", "salt"), poems ending on a question or a one-line stanza, poems
   opening with "I" or "When", and identical stanza shapes. Repetition that builds a
   motif stays; repetition that is a habit is revised in all but the strongest uses.
8. **Stress-test the order (QA-02).** Swap the first two poems and read the opening;
   move the last poem to the middle; read only the last lines of every poem in
   order. If a swap makes no difference, that part of the order is not doing work.
9. **Assemble the front and back matter.** Working title options (often a phrase
   from a poem), table of contents, section titles, and an acknowledgments page from
   the poet's supplied publication list only.

## Output Format

```

Target: [chapbook | full-length] · page limit [VERIFY] · current pages: [n]
Through-line: [one sentence]

| Poem | Pp | Finish | Necessity | Fit | Decision (keep / revise / cut / other ms) | Note |

## Ordering logic and anchors

## Sequence with adjacency links
| # | Poem | Link to next (echo / contrast / hinge) | Flag |

## Front and back matter (title options, contents, acknowledgments as supplied)
## Revision queue before submission
```

## Verification

- [ ] Every poem has a card and three anchored scores; decisions follow the scores.
- [ ] The through-line is one sentence, without "and also".
- [ ] Every consecutive pair has a named link or a flag.
- [ ] Tic counts are actual counts across the kept poems.
- [ ] Page total is within the stated target, marked `[VERIFY]` against guidelines.
- [ ] No press, contest, fee, judge, or publication credit appears unless supplied.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Best poem first is not a rule.** The opener's job is to set terms; a quieter
   poem that teaches the book's method can outperform a showpiece.
2. **Thematic sameness is not coherence.** Twenty-four poems on grief in the same
   register read as one poem repeated; coherence needs variation in mode and tempo.
3. **Cut is not a verdict on the poem.** A poem that does not fit may lead the next
   manuscript.
4. **A repeated word can be the book's spine.** Count, then decide; do not strip a
   motif because it recurs.
5. **Sections are not mandatory.** In a short chapbook they often break momentum.
6. **There is no single correct order.** Offer a recommended sequence and one
   alternative logic; the poet chooses.
7. **Do not assert market facts.** Which presses publish chapbooks, contest fees,
   and page limits change; mark them for the poet to verify.

## Example Output

```
# Manuscript plan — working title "What the Hull Held"
Target: chapbook · page limit 20–28 [VERIFY per contest] · candidates: 31 poems, 38 pp
Through-line: a year of selling a father's fishing boat, read as an inventory of what
can and cannot be sold.

## Selection (excerpt)
| Poem | Pp | Fin | Nec | Fit | Decision | Note |
| Inventory of the Hull | 1 | 5 | 5 | 5 | keep | teaches the list mode |
| Listing Photos | 1 | 4 | 5 | 5 | keep | |
| Salt Year | 2 | 3 | 2 | 5 | cut | "Low Tide Ledger" does its job better |
| Ghazal of the Asking Price | 1 | 3 | 5 | 5 | revise | radif drifts in couplet 4 |
| Divorce Papers, Kitchen Table | 2 | 4 | 4 | 1 | other ms | strong, but a different book |
Result: 22 kept (3 after revision), 6 cut, 3 to another manuscript → 26 pp.

## Ordering logic and anchors
Logic: seasonal arc, no sections (spring listing → winter sale).
Opening: "Inventory of the Hull" — establishes list-as-elegy. Closing: "Buyer's
Wake" — ends on the new owner's wake widening, not on the father.

## Sequence with adjacency links (first six)
| 1 | Inventory of the Hull | echo: "bilge pump" → opens #2 | |
| 2 | Listing Photos | contrast: prose poem → short lyric | |
| 3 | Fair Market | hinge: ends "what's it worth" → #4 opens with a price | |
| 4 | Ghazal of the Asking Price | — | no link to #5; three couplet poems in a row (#4–6) |
| 5 | Two Gulls | contrast needed | move "Bait Shop Receipt" (prose poem) here |

## Manuscript-level tics
| "salt" | 11 poems | keep in 4 (title-adjacent), revise in 7 |
| ends on a question | 7 poems | keep 2 (#3, closing); revise 5 |
| opens with "I" | 9 poems | revise 4 so the run #7–#10 does not open identically |

## Order stress tests
Swapping #1 and #2 weakens the opening: "Listing Photos" assumes the reader knows
the boat. Keep. Last lines read in sequence show a three-poem run (#14–16) ending on
"water"; vary one.

## Front and back matter
Title options: "What the Hull Held" (from #1); "Asking Price" (from #4).
Acknowledgments: four poems as supplied by the poet — journal names and issues exactly
as provided; nothing added.

## Revision queue before submission
Ghazal radif (couplet 4); 7 "salt" passes; 5 question endings; #5 placement swap.
```

## Techniques Used

- **DP-03 Anchored Scoring Scales** — finish, necessity, and fit scored on observable anchors before any ordering talk.
- **RT-06 Correlation and Cross-Analysis** — every adjacent pair analysed for echo, contrast, or hinge, and runs of sameness flagged.
- **QA-02 Adversarial Stress-Test** — swap, move, and last-lines tests try to break the proposed order.
- **ST-02 Structured Sequential Instructions** — cards → scores → through-line → logic → anchors → sequence → tics → tests.
