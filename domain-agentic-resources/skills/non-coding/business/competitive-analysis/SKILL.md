# Competitive Analysis

Systematically evaluate competitors, market structure, and strategic positioning to produce decision-ready intelligence for leadership, product, and go-to-market teams.

## When to Use This Skill

- Entering a new market or adjacent segment and need to understand the competitive landscape before committing resources.
- Preparing a board or leadership briefing on competitive threats and opportunities.
- Evaluating a competitor's recent move (acquisition, pricing change, new product) and modeling your response options.
- Informing product roadmap prioritization by mapping unmet needs against competitor coverage.
- Supporting M&A due diligence by assessing a target's competitive moat and vulnerability.

## Core Concepts

### Porter's Five Forces Framework

Assess industry-level profitability drivers before analyzing individual competitors. Each force is scored Low / Medium / High with supporting evidence.

| Force | Key Questions |
|-------|---------------|
| **Threat of New Entrants** | Capital requirements? Regulatory barriers? Network effects? Brand loyalty switching costs? |
| **Bargaining Power of Suppliers** | Supplier concentration? Switching costs? Forward integration risk? |
| **Bargaining Power of Buyers** | Buyer concentration? Price sensitivity? Availability of substitutes? Information asymmetry? |
| **Threat of Substitutes** | Functional equivalents? Price-performance trade-offs? Buyer propensity to switch? |
| **Competitive Rivalry** | Number and size of rivals? Industry growth rate? Exit barriers? Differentiation degree? |

### SWOT for Competitors (Not Just Yourself)

Apply SWOT to each major competitor to understand their strategic posture:

- **Strengths** -- Capabilities, assets, or positions that give them advantage (brand, distribution, IP, talent).
- **Weaknesses** -- Gaps, constraints, or liabilities that limit them (technical debt, narrow customer base, regulatory exposure).
- **Opportunities** -- External trends they could exploit (market shifts, regulatory changes, technology inflection points).
- **Threats** -- External forces that could erode their position (substitutes, new entrants, macroeconomic headwinds).

### Value Chain Analysis

Decompose each competitor's activities into primary (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing/sales, service) and support (infrastructure, HR, technology, procurement) to identify where they create or destroy value relative to the market.

### Strategic Group Mapping

Plot competitors on two dimensions that matter most for the market (e.g., price vs. breadth of offering, technology sophistication vs. market reach). Clusters reveal strategic groups, mobility barriers between groups, and white-space opportunities.

### Market Share Estimation

When hard data is unavailable, triangulate from multiple signals:

1. **Revenue proxies** -- Job postings, office footprint, LinkedIn headcount, funding rounds.
2. **Digital footprint** -- Web traffic (SimilarWeb), app downloads, social following, review volume.
3. **Channel checks** -- Reseller conversations, industry analyst estimates, conference presence.
4. **Bottoms-up modeling** -- Known customer count x estimated ACV = revenue estimate.

## Workflow

### Phase 1: Scope and Frame

Define the analysis boundaries before collecting data.

1. **State the decision** the analysis will inform (e.g., "Should we enter the SMB segment?").
2. **Identify the competitive set** -- direct competitors, indirect competitors, potential entrants, and substitutes. Use a 2x2 of "similar product / different product" vs. "similar customer / different customer" to avoid blind spots.
3. **Select dimensions** that matter for the decision (pricing, features, distribution, brand, technology, geographic coverage).
4. **Set the time horizon** -- current state, 12-month outlook, or 3-year strategic view.

### Phase 2: Intelligence Gathering

Collect structured data across the competitive set.

- **Public sources:** 10-K/10-Q filings, earnings transcripts, press releases, patent filings, job postings, blog/changelog history.
- **Digital signals:** Website traffic trends, SEO keyword overlap, G2/Capterra reviews, app store ratings and review velocity.
- **Human intelligence:** Sales win/loss interviews, customer advisory board feedback, industry analyst briefings, conference takeaways.
- **Product intelligence:** Free trial teardowns, feature matrices, API documentation audits, pricing page archaeology (Wayback Machine).

Organize into a **Competitor Profile Template** per competitor (see Templates section).

### Phase 3: Structural Analysis

Apply frameworks to the gathered data.

1. **Run Porter's Five Forces** at the industry level. Score each force and document evidence.
2. **Build SWOT matrices** for each competitor (and your own company for contrast).
3. **Map the value chain** for the top 3 competitors. Highlight where their cost structure or differentiation advantage lives.
4. **Create a strategic group map** with the two dimensions most relevant to the decision.

### Phase 4: Positioning and Gap Analysis

Synthesize findings into actionable positioning insights.

1. **Build a competitive feature matrix** -- rows are capabilities, columns are competitors, cells are coverage level (strong / partial / absent).
2. **Identify positioning white space** -- combinations of value propositions no competitor owns.
3. **Assess switching costs and lock-in** -- what keeps customers with each competitor, and what could break that bond.
4. **Map customer segment coverage** -- which segments are over-served, under-served, or unserved.

### Phase 5: Response Modeling

Model likely competitive dynamics forward.

1. **Competitor motivation analysis** -- what does each competitor's strategy, investor pressure, and leadership signaling suggest about their next moves?
2. **Scenario planning** -- build 2-3 scenarios (aggressive competitor expansion, market consolidation, technology disruption) and assess your position in each.
3. **Response option generation** -- for each scenario, identify 2-3 strategic responses with trade-offs.
4. **War-gaming** -- for the highest-impact scenario, simulate a move-countermove sequence through 2-3 rounds.

### Phase 6: Synthesis and Recommendations

Package findings for the decision-maker.

1. **Executive summary** -- 3-5 key findings that directly answer the framing question from Phase 1.
2. **Competitive landscape visual** -- strategic group map or positioning map with annotations.
3. **Threat/opportunity ranking** -- prioritized list with likelihood and impact scores.
4. **Recommended actions** -- specific, time-bound, owner-assigned next steps.
5. **Monitoring plan** -- leading indicators to track and trigger points for re-analysis.

## Best Practices

- **Start with the decision, not the data.** Frame the analysis around a specific strategic question. Unfocused competitive research produces shelves of useless reports.
- **Separate facts from inferences.** Label every data point as confirmed (public filing, direct observation) or inferred (triangulated, estimated). Decision-makers need to know confidence levels.
- **Update continuously, not annually.** Competitive intelligence decays fast. Set up lightweight monitoring (Google Alerts, job board tracking, review site RSS) rather than relying on periodic deep dives.
- **Include yourself in the analysis.** Apply the same critical lens to your own company. The most dangerous blind spot is an honest SWOT you never did on yourself.
- **Talk to customers, not just dashboards.** Win/loss interviews and churned customer feedback reveal competitive dynamics that no amount of desk research uncovers.
- **Beware the feature checklist trap.** A feature matrix shows what competitors have built, not what customers value. Weight features by customer importance, not count.
- **Model motivations, not just capabilities.** A well-funded competitor with no strategic reason to enter your segment is less threatening than a cash-strapped competitor whose survival depends on it.
- **Keep deliverables decision-ready.** Every page should answer "so what?" If a finding doesn't influence the decision, cut it.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| Confirmation bias in data collection | Analysts unconsciously seek data supporting existing beliefs | Assign a "red team" analyst to argue the opposite thesis; require disconfirming evidence for each claim |
| Over-indexing on direct competitors | Easiest to research; feels productive | Explicitly include substitutes and potential entrants in the competitive set definition |
| Stale intelligence driving decisions | One-time analysis treated as permanent truth | Attach expiration dates to every finding; automate signal monitoring |
| Feature parity obsession | Product teams default to matching competitors feature-for-feature | Anchor on customer needs and willingness to pay, not competitor feature lists |
| Ignoring non-market competition | Focus only on companies, missing regulatory, cultural, or behavioral barriers | Include "do nothing" and "build internally" as competitors in the analysis |
| Analysis paralysis | Collecting data feels safer than making recommendations | Set a time-box for research and require a recommendation even with incomplete data |

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] Decision question is explicitly stated and the analysis answers it
- [ ] Competitive set includes direct, indirect, substitute, and potential entrant categories
- [ ] Porter's Five Forces assessed with evidence for each force rating
- [ ] SWOT completed for each major competitor AND for own company
- [ ] Market share estimates include methodology and confidence level
- [ ] Strategic group map uses dimensions relevant to the decision
- [ ] Feature matrix is weighted by customer importance, not just presence/absence
- [ ] Response modeling includes at least 2 scenarios with counter-moves
- [ ] Recommendations are specific, time-bound, and assigned to owners
- [ ] Monitoring plan defines leading indicators and re-analysis triggers
- [ ] All inferences are labeled separately from confirmed facts
- [ ] Deliverable is sized appropriately for the audience (exec summary for C-suite, detail appendix for strategy team)

## Examples

### Example: SaaS Startup Evaluating Mid-Market Entry

**Decision question:** Should we launch a mid-market tier in Q3, given that Competitor X just raised $50M and announced enterprise focus?

**Phase 1 output:** Competitive set of 6 players mapped on price vs. feature breadth. Mid-market segment identified as under-served cluster on the strategic group map.

**Phase 2 output:** Competitor X's job postings show 80% enterprise sales hiring, confirming upmarket move. Their G2 reviews show declining mid-market NPS (from 4.2 to 3.6 over 12 months). Two smaller competitors serve mid-market but lack key integrations.

**Phase 3 output:** Porter's analysis shows moderate buyer power (low switching costs but high evaluation effort), low supplier power, high rivalry in enterprise but moderate in mid-market.

**Phase 5 output:** Three scenarios modeled:
1. Competitor X ignores mid-market (70% likely) -- we capture share with a focused mid-market tier.
2. Competitor X launches a stripped-down mid-market product (20% likely) -- we differentiate on onboarding speed and integrations.
3. A new entrant targets mid-market with AI-native approach (10% likely) -- we accelerate our AI roadmap.

**Recommendation:** Launch mid-market tier in Q3 with a 90-day pilot program targeting Competitor X's churning mid-market customers. Allocate $200K for competitive displacement campaigns. Monitor Competitor X job postings and product changelog weekly for early warning of counter-move.
