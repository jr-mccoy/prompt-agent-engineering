# Patient Communication

Craft patient-facing communication that is empathetic, clinically accurate, safe, and action-oriented across contexts from routine instructions to difficult conversations.

## When to Use This Skill

- Writing patient letters, portal messages, or follow-up instructions
- Preparing for difficult conversations (bad news, treatment changes)
- Creating templates for common clinical communication scenarios
- Training staff on patient communication frameworks
- Reviewing existing patient communications for clarity and empathy

## Core Concepts

### The AIDET Framework

A structured approach for every patient interaction:

| Letter | Meaning | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **A** | Acknowledge | "Hello Mrs. Johnson, thank you for coming in today." |
| **I** | Introduce | "I'm Dr. Smith, and I'll be managing your care today." |
| **D** | Duration | "This visit should take about 30 minutes." |
| **E** | Explanation | "I'm going to review your test results and discuss next steps." |
| **T** | Thank | "Thank you for being so patient during the wait." |

### SBAR for Structured Communication

Used for clinical handoffs and structured patient updates:

- **S**ituation: What's happening right now?
- **B**ackground: What's the clinical context?
- **A**ssessment: What do I think is going on?
- **R**ecommendation: What do I suggest we do?

### SPIKES Protocol for Breaking Bad News

1. **S**etting: Private space, sit down, ensure no interruptions
2. **P**erception: "What have you been told so far?" / "What's your understanding?"
3. **I**nvitation: "Would you like me to explain the results in detail?"
4. **K**nowledge: Share information in small chunks, avoid jargon
5. **E**mpathy: Name and validate the emotion — "I can see this is really upsetting."
6. **S**ummary: Recap key points, confirm understanding, define next steps

### Empathy Statements

Empathy is a clinical skill, not just bedside manner:

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| Naming | "It sounds like you're feeling frustrated." |
| Validating | "That's a completely understandable reaction." |
| Supporting | "We're going to work through this together." |
| Respecting | "I appreciate you being honest with me about this." |
| Exploring | "Can you tell me more about what worries you most?" |

### Safety Netting

Every patient communication should include "red flag" return-to-care instructions:

```
When to seek help:
- [Specific symptom 1] → Call our office
- [Specific symptom 2] → Go to urgent care
- [Emergency symptom] → Call 911 or go to the ER immediately
```

## Workflow

### Phase 1: Assess the Communication Context

1. Identify the communication type (routine, sensitive, urgent)
2. Consider the patient's health literacy level
3. Note any cultural or language considerations
4. Determine the communication channel (in-person, portal, phone, letter)
5. Identify emotional context (anxious, angry, grieving, relieved)

### Phase 2: Structure the Message

For **routine communications** (results, instructions, follow-up):
1. Lead with the key message
2. Provide specific action steps
3. Include safety netting
4. Offer a clear path for questions

For **sensitive communications** (bad news, diagnosis, treatment changes):
1. Use SPIKES protocol
2. Allow space for emotional response
3. Chunk information — don't overload
4. Confirm understanding before moving to next steps

### Phase 3: Apply Plain Language

1. Use common words (see Health Literacy Rewriter skill)
2. Short sentences, active voice
3. Define any necessary medical terms
4. Use "you" and "your" — direct address
5. Numbered steps for any instructions

### Phase 4: Add Empathy and Personalization

1. Use the patient's name
2. Acknowledge their experience or concern
3. Validate emotions without dismissing them
4. Avoid clinical detachment ("The patient presents with..." → "You have...")
5. Express partnership: "We'll work on this together"

### Phase 5: Include Safety and Follow-up

1. Add clear safety netting with specific symptoms and actions
2. State the follow-up plan (next appointment, when to expect results)
3. Provide contact information for questions
4. Include after-hours or emergency contact if relevant

### Phase 6: Review and Validate

1. Check clinical accuracy with the care team
2. Verify readability (target grade 5-8 for written materials)
3. Ensure empathy and tone are appropriate for the context
4. Confirm no critical safety information is missing
5. Have a colleague review sensitive communications before delivery

## Templates

### Portal Message Template (Normal Results)

```markdown
Dear [Patient Name],

Your [test name] results are back, and everything looks normal.

**Your Results:**
- [Test]: [Result] (normal range: [range])

**What This Means:**
[1-2 sentences in plain language]

**What to Do Next:**
- [Action if any, or "No action needed"]
- Your next [test/visit] is scheduled for [date]

If you have questions, message us through the portal or call [number].

Take care,
[Provider Name]
```

### Difficult Conversation Prep Template

```markdown
## Communication Plan: [Patient Name]

**Context:** [Diagnosis/situation]
**Patient's likely emotional state:** [Anxious/Unaware/Angry/etc.]

**SPIKES Plan:**
- Setting: [Where, who present]
- Perception check: "What's your understanding of..."
- Invitation: "Would you like me to explain..."
- Key message (plain language): [The core information]
- Empathy response prepared: [Naming + validation statement]
- Summary and next steps: [Concrete plan]
```

## Best Practices

- **Name the emotion before giving more information**: "I can see this is a lot to take in" before explaining treatment options.
- **Chunk, check, chunk**: Deliver information in small pieces, check understanding, then continue.
- **Avoid false reassurance**: "I'm sure it'll be fine" undermines trust. Instead: "I understand this is uncertain. Here's what we know and what we'll do."
- **Use Teach-Back**: "I want to make sure I explained clearly. Can you tell me in your own words what you'll do at home?"
- **Always include safety netting**: Every communication should tell patients when to seek help.
- **Match the channel to the message**: Don't deliver bad news via portal message. Sensitive information deserves a phone call or in-person conversation.
- **Document the communication**: Note what was communicated, patient's response, and confirmed understanding.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| Information overload | Trying to be thorough | Chunk and prioritize; 3 key points max per conversation |
| False reassurance | Discomfort with uncertainty | Acknowledge uncertainty honestly; explain the plan |
| Jargon in patient materials | Clinician's second nature | Have non-clinical staff review all written materials |
| Missing safety netting | Assumed patient knows when to return | Include explicit red flags in every communication |
| One-way communication | Provider talks, patient listens | Use open questions and Teach-Back to confirm understanding |
| Emotional dismissal | "Don't worry" / "It's routine" | Name and validate the emotion instead |

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] Key message is stated in the first 1-2 sentences
- [ ] Medical jargon replaced with plain language
- [ ] Empathy statement included (for sensitive communications)
- [ ] Action steps are specific and numbered
- [ ] Safety netting with specific symptoms and actions included
- [ ] Follow-up plan clearly stated
- [ ] Contact information for questions provided
- [ ] Readability at grade 5-8 level for written materials
- [ ] Clinical accuracy verified
- [ ] Teach-Back or understanding check planned (for verbal communication)

## Examples

### Example: Post-Procedure Follow-up Message

**Before (clinical):**
> "Post-endoscopic findings revealed grade B esophagitis per LA classification. Recommend PPI BID × 8 weeks. F/U EGD in 3 months. Avoid NSAIDs. RTC if hematemesis or melena."

**After (patient-facing):**
> Dear Michael,
>
> Thank you for coming in for your procedure today. Here's a summary of what we found and what to do next.
>
> **What We Found:**
> You have some irritation in your esophagus (the tube from your throat to your stomach). This is common and treatable.
>
> **What to Do:**
> 1. Take omeprazole (Prilosec) — **1 pill, 2 times a day** — for 8 weeks
> 2. Take it 30 minutes before breakfast and dinner
> 3. Avoid ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) and aspirin — use Tylenol for pain instead
>
> **When to Call Us:**
> Call our office at [number] if you have:
> - Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
> - Black or tarry stools
> - Severe stomach pain
>
> **Your next appointment** is in 3 months for a follow-up scope. We'll call to schedule.
>
> Questions? Message us anytime through the portal.
