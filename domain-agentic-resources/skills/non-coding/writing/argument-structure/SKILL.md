# Argument Structure

Build logically sound, persuasive arguments with explicit claims, structured evidence, and robust counterargument handling for essays, proposals, briefs, and presentations.

## When to Use This Skill

- Writing persuasive essays, op-eds, or position papers
- Constructing business cases or investment proposals
- Preparing debate arguments or policy briefs
- Structuring legal arguments or compliance justifications
- Reviewing arguments for logical soundness before publication

## Core Concepts

### The Toulmin Model

Stephen Toulmin's model breaks arguments into six components:

| Component | Role | Example |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **Claim** | The thesis or position | "Remote work improves productivity." |
| **Grounds** | Evidence supporting the claim | "Studies show 13% productivity increase." |
| **Warrant** | The logical bridge connecting grounds to claim | "Fewer office distractions enable deeper focus." |
| **Backing** | Support for the warrant itself | "Research on flow states shows..." |
| **Qualifier** | Limits on the claim's scope | "For knowledge workers in individual contributor roles..." |
| **Rebuttal** | Conditions where the claim doesn't hold | "...unless the role requires constant collaboration." |

Most weak arguments are missing the **warrant** (they state a claim and evidence but don't explain the logical connection) or the **qualifier** (they overstate the claim's scope).

### Evidence Hierarchy

| Strength | Evidence Type | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Strongest | Meta-analyses, systematic reviews | "A meta-analysis of 47 studies found..." |
| Strong | Randomized controlled trials | "In a controlled experiment with 500 participants..." |
| Moderate | Observational studies, large datasets | "Analysis of 10,000 companies showed..." |
| Weaker | Expert opinion, case studies | "According to Dr. Smith..." / "At Company X..." |
| Weakest | Anecdote, analogy, common sense | "In my experience..." / "It's like..." |

### Logical Fallacies to Avoid

| Fallacy | What It Is | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Straw man | Distorting the opposing view | "They want no regulations at all" (when they want fewer) |
| Ad hominem | Attacking the person, not the argument | "You're not qualified to comment" |
| False dichotomy | Presenting only two options | "Either we do this or the company fails" |
| Appeal to authority | Citing status instead of evidence | "The CEO thinks so" (without supporting data) |
| Slippery slope | Assuming extreme consequences without evidence | "If we allow X, then Y and Z will follow" |
| Cherry picking | Selecting only favorable evidence | Ignoring studies that contradict your position |
| Post hoc | Assuming causation from sequence | "After we launched X, metrics improved" (correlation) |

## Workflow

### Phase 1: Define the Claim

1. State your position as a specific, debatable claim
   - Weak: "AI is changing education" (observation, not argument)
   - Strong: "AI tutoring should supplement, not replace, human instruction in K-12"
2. Identify your audience and what they currently believe
3. Determine the argument's purpose (persuade, justify, rebut, propose)
4. Scope the claim with qualifiers — where does it apply and where doesn't it?

### Phase 2: Gather and Evaluate Evidence

1. Collect evidence from multiple source types (data, studies, expert analysis)
2. Evaluate evidence strength using the hierarchy above
3. Identify gaps — which parts of the claim lack strong support?
4. Look actively for disconfirming evidence (steelmanning, not cherry-picking)
5. Note the source quality and potential biases

### Phase 3: Build the Argument Structure

**Classical Rhetoric Structure:**
1. **Exordium** (Introduction) — Hook, establish credibility, state the issue
2. **Narratio** (Background) — Context the audience needs
3. **Confirmatio** (Proof) — Present your evidence-backed claims
4. **Refutatio** (Rebuttal) — Address and dismantle counterarguments
5. **Peroratio** (Conclusion) — Emotional appeal, call to action, restate thesis

**For shorter pieces, use PEEL paragraphs:**
- **P**oint — State the sub-claim
- **E**vidence — Provide data or support
- **E**xplain — Connect evidence to claim (the warrant)
- **L**ink — Connect back to the main thesis

### Phase 4: Address Counterarguments

The strongest arguments engage opposing views head-on:

1. **Identify** the strongest counterarguments (not the weakest — avoid straw man)
2. **Steelman** them — state the counterargument more strongly than your opponent would
3. **Concede** where appropriate — partial concessions build credibility
4. **Rebut** with evidence and logic — explain why your position prevails despite the counterargument
5. **Synthesize** — show how addressing the counterargument strengthens your position

### Phase 5: Refine Logic and Rhetoric

1. Check every claim-to-evidence connection for logical fallacies
2. Verify all qualifiers are in place (avoid overclaiming)
3. Ensure the overall flow follows a logical progression
4. Add rhetorical devices where appropriate (analogy, repetition, parallel structure)
5. Calibrate tone to audience (academic, business, public)

### Phase 6: Stress Test

1. **Red team** the argument: try to tear it apart from the opposing side
2. Ask: "If the strongest critic read this, what would they attack first?"
3. Check for unstated assumptions — make them explicit
4. Verify the conclusion follows from the premises (no logical leaps)
5. Ensure the argument works without the weakest piece of evidence

## Templates

### Argument Outline Template

```markdown
## Thesis
[One clear, debatable, qualified claim]

## Argument Map
### Point 1: [Sub-claim]
- Evidence: [Source, data, quote]
- Warrant: [Why this evidence supports the sub-claim]
- Qualifier: [Scope limitation]

### Point 2: [Sub-claim]
- Evidence: [Source, data, quote]
- Warrant: [Logical connection]

### Point 3: [Sub-claim]
- Evidence: [Source, data, quote]
- Warrant: [Logical connection]

## Counterarguments
### Counter 1: [Strongest opposing view]
- Concession: [What's valid about this view]
- Rebuttal: [Why your position prevails]

### Counter 2: [Second opposing view]
- Rebuttal: [Evidence-based response]

## Conclusion
[Restate thesis with added nuance from the counterargument engagement]
[Call to action or implication]
```

## Best Practices

- **Lead with your second-strongest point, end with your strongest**: The primacy-recency effect means readers remember the first and last points most.
- **Steelman, don't straw man**: Engaging the strongest version of the counterargument builds more credibility than defeating a weak one.
- **Qualify your claims**: "In most cases" and "for knowledge workers" are not weaknesses — they're signs of rigorous thinking.
- **Show your reasoning, not just your conclusion**: The warrant (why evidence supports the claim) is what persuades.
- **Use concrete evidence over abstract reasoning**: Data and examples persuade more than logical arguments alone.
- **Concede gracefully**: Acknowledging valid counterpoints builds trust and strengthens your remaining claims.
- **Vary evidence types**: Combine data, expert opinion, case studies, and logical reasoning.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| Missing warrant | Assumes the connection is obvious | Explicitly state why evidence supports each claim |
| Overclaiming | Enthusiasm outpaces evidence | Add qualifiers; state the claim's boundaries |
| Straw manning opposition | Easier to defeat a weak version | Steelman: state the counter more strongly |
| Evidence-free assertions | Writer treats opinion as self-evident | Back every claim with at least one source |
| Linear structure only | Every point builds sequentially | Use also concession-rebuttal rhythm for variety |
| Ignoring audience beliefs | Arguing from your values, not theirs | Start from common ground; meet the audience where they are |

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] Thesis is specific, debatable, and qualified
- [ ] Every sub-claim has evidence from the upper half of the evidence hierarchy
- [ ] Warrants explicitly connect evidence to claims
- [ ] At least one counterargument is steelmanned and rebutted
- [ ] No logical fallacies present
- [ ] Claims are appropriately scoped with qualifiers
- [ ] Argument survives removal of its weakest piece of evidence
- [ ] Tone and register match the target audience
- [ ] Conclusion restates thesis with added nuance
- [ ] Argument has been red-teamed or stress-tested
