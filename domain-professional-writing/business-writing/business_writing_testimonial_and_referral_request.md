# Testimonial and Referral Request

**Objective:** Draft two related but **separate** asks to a satisfied client — a
testimonial, and an onward introduction — timed to the moment the value is most
visible, structured so each takes the client under five minutes, and sequenced so
that declining one does not decline both.

**When to Use:** Use at the point of demonstrated value, which is usually **not**
project close-out. For most engagements the right moment is when a result becomes
visible to the client — often weeks after delivery — or immediately after they say
something unprompted and positive.

Neither `testimonial` nor a services referral ask appears anywhere in this
repository. This is **distinct from**
`../../domain-agentic-resources/skills/marketing/referral-program/` and
`../../domain-business-strategy/go-to-market/marketing_referral_program_design.md`,
which design *programs* — incentive structures, affiliate mechanics, in-app referral
flows for a product at scale. This is a single human ask from a supplier to one
client, where the currency is the relationship rather than an incentive.

The two asks are combined in one prompt because they share timing, sequencing and
most of the drafting logic — but they are drafted as separate messages, for the
reason given in step 4.

---

## Context Gathering

1. **The relationship**
   - "Who was the sponsor? Did you work with them directly?"
   - "Is the engagement finished, ongoing, or dormant?"
   - "How did they end it — warmly, neutrally, or with friction?"

2. **The evidence**
   - "What did they say, unprompted, that was positive? Exact words if you have them."
   - "What result can they now see? When did it become visible?"
   - "Is there anything outstanding — an unpaid invoice, an open item?"

3. **What you need**
   - "Where would the testimonial be used — website, proposal, profile?"
   - "Do you need a name and title, or is anonymous acceptable?"
   - "What kind of introduction would actually help? Be specific."

4. **Their network**
   - "Who do they know that fits your ideal client profile?"
   - "Are they in a peer group, association or community relevant to your work?"
   - "Would an introduction cost them anything internally?"

The outstanding-items question is a gate. Never ask for a testimonial while an
invoice is unpaid or a deliverable is open: it reads as leverage, and it converts a
goodwill request into a transaction.

---

## Method

### Step 1 — Time it to visible value, not to completion

Close-out is the conventional moment and usually the wrong one. At close-out the
client has a deliverable; they do not yet have a result. The ask lands best at one of:

- The moment they say something positive unprompted — reply within a day
- When a result becomes measurable, typically four to twelve weeks after delivery
- At a natural milestone: a renewal, a successful launch, a review they ran themselves

The unprompted-praise trigger is the strongest and the most wasted. An email saying
"this has made a real difference" is a testimonial the client has already written;
all that remains is to ask whether you may use it.

### Step 2 — Make the testimonial ask require almost no effort

The reason testimonial requests fail is not unwillingness but the blank page. Remove
it. Three escalating levels of help, offered in order:

1. **Specific prompts.** Three questions, not "could you say a few words":
   - What was the situation before we started?
   - What was it like to work together — anything that surprised you?
   - What is different now?
2. **Their own words, returned.** Quote what they already said and ask whether you
   may use it. This is the highest-converting form of the ask and requires a one-word
   reply.
3. **A draft, offered explicitly as editable.** Offer only if they ask, and frame it
   honestly: "happy to draft something for you to change or discard." Never send a
   draft for signature unsolicited — the result reads as supplier-written because it
   is.

State where it will be used and confirm the attribution they are comfortable with:
full name and title, first name and role, or company only.

### Step 3 — Make the referral ask specific

"Do you know anyone who might need this?" fails because it asks the client to search
their entire network against a vague pattern. Specificity does the work for them:

- Name the **role and situation**, not the industry: "heads of data at companies
  around your size who are about to go through the same migration."
- Where possible, name **people or organisations** you have identified, and ask
  whether they know them.
- Ask for **one** introduction, not several. One is a favour; several is a task.

Make the introduction mechanically easy: offer a short forwardable paragraph they can
send without writing anything, and tell them you will take it from there.

### Step 4 — Separate the two asks

Do not put both in one message. Three reasons:

- A single message with two asks gets one answer, usually to the easier one.
- A client who declines the introduction may feel they have declined the whole
  message, and say nothing about the testimonial.
- The testimonial is a comment on work already done; the introduction spends their
  social capital. They are different favours of different sizes.

Sequence: testimonial first, then the introduction in a separate message a week or
two later. Agreement to the smaller ask makes the larger one easier, and the
testimonial itself gives you a natural reason to write again.

### Step 5 — Make declining easy, and mean it

Include an explicit exit: "if now is not a good time, or this is not something you
do, that is completely fine — it will not be awkward." This raises response rates
and protects the relationship, which is worth more than any single testimonial.

It must be genuine. Do not follow up twice on a testimonial request. One reminder,
then stop.

### Step 6 — Close the loop

When they provide either, thank them specifically and tell them what happened — where
the testimonial appeared, or how the introduction went. Clients who see the result of
a favour give another one.

## Output Format

```markdown
## Asks — [client], [sponsor name]

**Trigger:** [unprompted praise / result visible / milestone] on [date]
**Gate check:** outstanding invoices [none / present] · open deliverables [none / present]
→ [proceed / wait until resolved]

---

### Message 1 — Testimonial
**Send:** [date] · **Channel:** [email / message]

> Subject: [x]
>
> [body: context, the ask, the three prompts or their own quote returned,
>  where it will be used, attribution options, the easy exit]

**Level of help offered:** [prompts / their words returned / draft on request]
**Attribution requested:** [full name and title / first name and role / company only]

---

### Message 2 — Introduction
**Send:** [date — 1–2 weeks after message 1] · **Only if:** [condition]

> Subject: [x]
>
> [body: the specific role and situation, named targets if any, the single ask,
>  the offer of a forwardable paragraph, the easy exit]

**Forwardable paragraph** (they can send this unchanged):
> [2–3 sentences: who you are, what you do, why the introduction makes sense]

---

### Follow-up rule
One reminder at [date]. Then stop.

### Loop closure
When received → thank specifically, and report [where it was used / how it went].
```

## Verification

- [ ] No outstanding invoice or open deliverable at the time of asking.
- [ ] Timing is tied to visible value, not to project completion.
- [ ] The testimonial ask includes specific prompts or returns their own words.
- [ ] Where the testimonial will be used, and the attribution, are both stated.
- [ ] The referral ask names a role and situation, not an industry.
- [ ] Exactly one introduction is requested.
- [ ] A forwardable paragraph is supplied.
- [ ] The two asks are in separate messages, sequenced.
- [ ] Both messages contain a genuine, unembarrassed exit.
- [ ] The follow-up rule is one reminder, then stop.

**False-positive prevention.** The dominant failure is the combined ask, which
converts two modest favours into one large one and typically yields neither. Separate
them.

The second is the supplier-drafted testimonial sent for signature. It is efficient
and it produces text that reads as marketing copy, because it is. Readers discount
it, and some clients are uncomfortable signing words they did not write. Offer a
draft only when asked.

The third is asking at close-out by reflex. At close-out the client has a
deliverable and an invoice; the result that would make a testimonial specific has not
happened yet. A testimonial given at close-out says you were professional and
delivered on time — true, unremarkable, and true of everyone.

The fourth is persistence. A second follow-up on a testimonial converts a small
favour into an obligation and costs more relationship value than the testimonial is
worth.
