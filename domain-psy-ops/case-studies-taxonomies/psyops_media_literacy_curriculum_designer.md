# Media Literacy Curriculum Designer

**Objective:** Design a teaching sequence that builds **durable, transferable recognition** of manipulation technique — and that avoids the well-known way this teaching fails. Media literacy instruction has a characteristic failure mode: learners emerge more suspicious of everything rather than better at discriminating, dismissing credible sources with the same confidence they dismiss fabricated ones. That outcome is worse than no teaching, because a learner who trusts nothing is easier to manipulate than one who trusts carelessly — they have no basis on which to be persuaded by anything true, and they are highly available to whoever offers certainty.

The design therefore optimizes for **discrimination, not suspicion**. Two commitments carry most of that. First, the curriculum teaches **what good sourcing looks like** alongside what manipulation looks like — you cannot recognize a counterfeit if you have never studied the genuine article, and most curricula spend all their time on the counterfeit. Second, **examples are drawn from every political direction, including the one the learners and the teacher hold.** A curriculum whose manipulation examples all come from one side teaches partisanship with a critical-thinking label, and learners detect this immediately.

Assessment follows the same logic: the measure is whether a learner correctly accepts a reliable source and correctly rejects an unreliable one. A learner who rejects everything scores as poorly as one who accepts everything, and any assessment that cannot distinguish those two cases is not measuring the skill.

**When to use:**
- You are teaching media literacy, critical thinking, or information literacy in a school, university, workplace, or community setting.
- You are building a family or household approach to this with children or teenagers.
- Previous teaching produced cynicism rather than discrimination and you want to fix the design.
- You are training staff who handle information under pressure.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need to analyze a single artifact as a worked example — use `../technique-analysis/psyops_propaganda_technique_identification.md`.
- You are designing advance protection against a specific expected claim — use `../counter-messaging/psyops_prebunking_inoculation_design.md`.
- You need general instructional design rather than this subject — use `domain-education-teaching/`.

**Audience:** Teachers, trainers, librarians, parents, and communications staff running internal education.

## Inputs / Context

1. **The learners.** Age, prior knowledge, and — importantly — how much they already distrust institutions. High existing distrust changes the design completely.
2. **Time available.** Total contact hours and whether it is one session or a sequence. Recognition skills need spacing and practice.
3. **Setting.** School, university, workplace, or family, and what constrains what you can teach.
4. **Political context.** Whether the setting requires balance, and how visible the teacher's own position is.
5. **Existing curriculum.** What this connects to or replaces.
6. **Assessment requirements.** What you must be able to demonstrate, and to whom.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Teach **what good sourcing looks like** as an explicit strand, not just what manipulation looks like.
- Draw examples from **every political direction**, including the one learners and the teacher hold, and say so openly.
- Teach **transferable checking habits** — a small number of questions applied to anything — rather than a list of red flags tied to current formats.
- Build **practice with feedback** over time. One session produces recognition that decays within weeks.
- Assess **discrimination**: correct acceptance of reliable material scored alongside correct rejection of unreliable material.
- Address the **cynicism failure mode** explicitly with learners, naming it as a risk of the course itself.
- Include the learner's **own susceptibility**, since technique recognition in others without self-application produces confident, well-armed error.
- Include a strand on **what to do when you cannot tell** — the honest and most common outcome.

### Must Not
- Build a curriculum whose manipulation examples come predominantly from one political direction. Learners notice immediately and it discredits everything.
- Teach source-tier lists — trustworthy outlets versus untrustworthy ones. It does not transfer, it goes stale, and it teaches deference rather than checking.
- Teach that everything is manipulation or that all sources are equally unreliable. That is the failure mode, not a lesson.
- Assess with instruments that reward blanket suspicion.
- Fabricate examples, teaching-effectiveness research, or statistics about misinformation prevalence.
- Use real, currently circulating false claims as unlabeled practice material with young learners without careful handling — exposure without resolution can consolidate the claim.
- Position the teacher as the arbiter of truth. The goal is learners who can check, not learners who defer to you.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Assess the learners' starting distrust
Where do they begin? Learners with high institutional distrust need the good-sourcing strand first and heavily; starting with manipulation examples confirms what they already believe and produces exactly the cynicism you are trying to avoid.

### Step 2 — Set discrimination-based objectives
Write objectives about correctly distinguishing, not about identifying manipulation. "Learners can explain why they accept one source and reject another, using the same criteria for both."

### Step 3 — Build the good-sourcing strand first
What careful work looks like: sourcing, correction policies, hedged claims, named methods, published limitations. Learners need a positive model before a negative one, and this strand is what prevents the failure mode.

### Step 4 — Build the technique strand with balanced examples
Teach a small number of high-transfer moves: missing base rates, decontextualized imagery, borrowed authority, manufactured urgency, emotional selection. **Source examples from every direction**, and be explicit with learners that you have done this deliberately.

### Step 5 — Build the checking-habits strand
Three or four questions applied to anything: where did this come from, what would I expect to see if it were false, who is telling me, and what is being left out. Small, memorable, transferable — this is what survives the course.

### Step 6 — Include self-application
Practice on material learners agree with. This is the hardest and most valuable segment, and skipping it produces learners who are skilled at criticizing their opponents and defenceless against their allies.

### Step 7 — Teach the uncertain outcome
What to do when you genuinely cannot tell: suspend, seek better coverage, do not share, and be comfortable holding an open question. Most real encounters end here, and a curriculum that only teaches resolution leaves learners unequipped for the common case.

### Step 8 — Design discrimination assessment and check for cynicism
Assess with a mixed set including reliable material. Score correct acceptances. Then check the design: would a learner who rejects everything pass? If yes, redesign.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Cynicism as the outcome.** The documented failure. A learner who trusts nothing is more manipulable, not less — they have no basis for being persuaded by anything true.
2. **One-directional examples.** Manipulation examples all from one political side. Learners detect it and the whole course is discredited.
3. **Source-tier teaching.** Lists of good and bad outlets. Non-transferable, quickly stale, and it teaches deference rather than checking.
4. **Assessment rewarding suspicion.** Instruments where rejecting everything scores well. Reliable material must be in the set.
5. **Single-session design.** One workshop, no practice, no spacing. Recognition decays fast without repetition.
6. **Self-application skipped.** Teaching learners to spot technique in their opponents' material only, producing confidently armed partisans.
7. **No uncertain outcome.** Teaching resolution only, when most real encounters end in genuine uncertainty.
8. **Teacher as arbiter.** Building learners who defer to your judgment rather than apply their own checks, which fails the moment you are not there.

- [ ] Examples are drawn from every political direction, including the learners' and the teacher's own, and this is stated openly.
- [ ] Objectives and assessment measure discrimination, with correct acceptance of reliable material scored.

- [ ] Practice is spaced rather than delivered in a single session.
