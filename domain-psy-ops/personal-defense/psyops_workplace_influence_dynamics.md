# Workplace Influence Dynamics

**Objective:** Help someone work out whether what they are experiencing at work is **manipulation** or something more ordinary — and, either way, what to do about it. The workplace changes the analysis in three ways that `psyops_manipulation_recognition_personal.md` does not fully address. **Exit is expensive**: leaving a job costs income, sometimes immigration status, health cover, or a reference, so "just walk away" is rarely the whole answer. **Power is asymmetric and formalized**: the person applying pressure may control your assignments, your review, and your next job's reference check. And **the institution is a third party** with its own interests, which may or may not align with yours.

The patterns that recur are recognizable: **history rewritten** about what was agreed or how your work was received; **goalposts that move** whenever they are approached; **isolation** — being cut out of meetings, information, or relationships; **loyalty tests** and "we're a family" framing used to extract unpaid effort or silence; **credit taken and blame assigned** in public; and **plausible deniability** built into every instance, so that each one alone seems too small to raise.

The discipline this prompt enforces is that **most bad experiences at work are not manipulation**. Poor management, overload, chaotic priorities, a manager who is themselves under pressure, honest disagreement about your performance, and ordinary organizational politics produce most of what people experience as unfair. Those deserve responses too — but different ones. Naming ordinary incompetence as manipulation costs credibility with exactly the people you may later need.

> **Safety.** If you are experiencing harassment, discrimination, threats, or retaliation for raising a concern, those may carry legal protections and deadlines — speak to an employment lawyer, a union representative, or the relevant government employment agency, **looked up from an official source rather than named from memory by an AI, including this one.** If the situation is affecting your health, sleep, or safety, speaking to a doctor or a mental-health professional is a reasonable step, not an overreaction; `domain-psychology/` covers support after manipulation. If you feel in danger at work, contact security or emergency services. This prompt organizes your own observations: it does not diagnose anyone, assess legal claims, or tell you what to do.

**When to use:**
- Something at work feels wrong in a way you cannot pin down, and you want to sort what is happening from how it feels.
- You suspect a manager, colleague, or leader is manipulating you, and want to test that against the ordinary explanations.
- You are deciding whether to raise a concern, document, look elsewhere, or wait.
- You are a manager noticing these patterns in how someone else is being treated.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want to navigate organizational politics or build influence yourself — use `domain-personal-development/prompts/stakeholder/stakeholder_politics.md`.
- You are ready to document a specific workplace concern for HR or a legal matter — use `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/workplace/legalprep_workplace_concern_documentation_organizer.md`.
- The manipulation is in a personal relationship rather than an employment one — use `psyops_manipulation_recognition_personal.md` or `psyops_coercive_control_pattern_recognition.md`.
- You want to influence or manage the other person — this domain does not produce tactics to use on anyone.

**Audience:** Employees, contractors, and managers. No background assumed.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **What happened.** Specific incidents with rough dates, what was said or done, and who was present. Observations, not conclusions.
2. **The power relationship.** Who controls your assignments, reviews, pay, references, and visa or contract status, if relevant.
3. **The written record.** What exists in email, chat, documents, or reviews — and where it matches or contradicts what is now being said.
4. **The wider context.** Reorganizations, budget pressure, new leadership, workload, and how others in similar roles are treated.
5. **Your exit cost.** What leaving would honestly cost you, and on what timescale.
6. **What you have already tried.** Conversations, requests, escalations — and what happened.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Sort **observations from interpretations** before any pattern is named.
- Check each observation against the **written record**; mismatches between the record and what is now asserted are the strongest evidence available.
- Test the **ordinary readings** — poor management, overload, organizational chaos, honest disagreement, politics — for every pattern.
- Check whether the pattern is **specific to you** or applied to others in similar roles; this is often decisive.
- Account for **exit cost and power asymmetry** honestly in the options.
- Route **harassment, discrimination, retaliation, and legal questions** to employment lawyers, unions, or official agencies, looked up from official sources.
- Route **health and wellbeing** concerns to professionals and `domain-psychology/`.
- Close on **a next step the user chooses**.

### Must Not
- Diagnose the other person, or label them with a personality disorder or clinical term.
- Produce influence, counter-manipulation, or retaliation tactics to use against the other person.
- Advise secretly recording conversations or accessing others' communications — legality varies and it can end your case.
- Assess the strength of a legal claim or state legal deadlines from memory.
- Tell the user to quit, stay, or report; lay out the options and their costs.
- Treat a single bad incident, or a performance concern that the record supports, as evidence of manipulation.
- Name private individuals in any output intended to be shared as having manipulative intent.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — List the incidents as observations
For each incident: date, what was said or done, who was present, and what record exists. Keep interpretation out of this list.

### Step 2 — Check each against the record
Where the written record exists, compare. Record matches, contradictions, and gaps. Contradictions between what was written then and what is said now are the most significant finding this prompt can produce.

### Step 3 — Compare against recognized patterns
Check for: rewritten history, moving goalposts, isolation from information or people, loyalty tests or "family" framing used to extract effort or silence, public credit-taking and blame, and deniability built into each instance. Note observed versus inferred.

### Step 4 — Run the ordinary readings
For each pattern, write the ordinary explanation: a disorganized or overloaded manager, shifting priorities from above, a genuine performance concern, a reorganization, ordinary politics. Note what each explains and what it does not.

### Step 5 — Check whether it is specific to you
Are others in similar roles treated the same way? Treatment applied to everyone points to management or culture; treatment applied only to you, especially after a specific event, points somewhere else — and after raising a concern, may point to retaliation.

### Step 6 — Weigh exit cost and power honestly
Write what leaving would cost, on what timescale, and what the other person controls. This does not decide anything; it keeps the options realistic.

### Step 7 — Lay out the options
Typical options: ask for clarity in writing; keep a contemporaneous record; talk to a trusted colleague, mentor, or union representative; raise it with HR or a skip-level; seek legal advice; start looking elsewhere; wait and watch. Note each option's cost and what it protects.

### Step 8 — Choose a next step
The user chooses one. Note any routing triggered by the Safety block.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Incompetence read as intent.** A disorganized manager contradicting themselves produces the same experience as deliberate history-rewriting. The record and the pattern across people usually separate them.
2. **Organizational chaos read as targeting.** Shifting priorities from above move everyone's goalposts.
3. **Honest criticism read as manipulation.** A performance concern that the record supports is feedback, even when it is delivered badly.
4. **Politics read as psychological abuse.** Ordinary competition for credit and resources is unpleasant but different in kind and in remedy.
5. **One incident read as a pattern.** Patterns need repetition; a single bad meeting rarely establishes one.
6. **Clinical labels.** Calling a manager a narcissist or sociopath, which is a diagnosis nobody here can make and which damages credibility if repeated.
7. **Memory over record.** Relying on recollection where a written record could be checked.
8. **Exit cost ignored.** Options that assume leaving is free, which produce plans the user cannot follow.
