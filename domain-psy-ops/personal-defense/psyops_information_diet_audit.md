# Information Diet Audit

**Objective:** Establish what is **actually** forming your picture of the world, as opposed to what you would say if asked. Almost everyone names a few respectable sources they consult deliberately and omits the ambient stream — algorithmic feeds, group chats, comment sections, podcasts on in the background, headlines seen and never read — which typically accounts for most of the input. The gap between the named diet and the real one is the first finding.

The audit then asks four questions of the real diet: **who selected this for me**, **what emotional register does it operate in**, **what does it show me about people who disagree**, and **does anything in it ever cause me to change my mind**. That last one is the load-bearing question. A diet that never updates you is not informing you; it is confirming you, and it will feel identical from the inside.

The audit deliberately does not chase "balance." Consuming sources you consider wrong in the name of even-handedness is not a virtue and does not improve calibration. The distinction that matters is between **narrowness of viewpoint** and **narrowness of evidence** — the second is the actual problem, and you can fix it without adopting a reading list you find worthless.

**When to use:**
- You have noticed you are angrier or more certain than you used to be, and want to know what changed.
- You keep encountering claims that everyone around you accepts and no one has checked.
- You want to reduce how much your attention is allocated by systems optimizing for something other than your understanding.
- You are surprised by an election, a poll, or a person's view, and want to know what your inputs concealed.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want to weigh specific sources against each other — use `domain-reasoning-craft/epistemic/epistemic_source_credibility_triangulation.md`.
- You want a broader personal resilience routine — use `psyops_cognitive_security_hygiene_plan.md`.
- The problem is information volume and capture rather than information formation — use `domain-productivity/bottlenecks/bottleneck_capture_triage_system_design.md`.

**Audience:** Anyone. No background assumed.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **Your named sources.** What you would say if asked what you read or watch.
2. **Your actual screen time by app or platform**, if you can get it. Device reports are usually available and usually surprising.
3. **Your ambient inputs.** Group chats, family, colleagues, podcasts, radio, comment sections, notifications.
4. **Recent strong opinions.** Two or three things you have felt strongly about lately, and where they came from.
5. **Recent updates.** Anything you have changed your mind about in the last year, and what caused it. A blank here is itself the finding.
6. **Time available.** What you would realistically spend differently, which keeps any adjustment achievable.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Compare the **named diet against the actual diet**, and treat the gap as the first finding.
- Attribute **selection**: for each input, who or what chose it — you, an algorithm, a friend, an employer, chance.
- Assess **emotional register**: what proportion of inputs operate through outrage, threat, contempt, or vindication.
- Identify the **feedback loop**: which inputs respond to your engagement, and therefore adapt toward whatever holds you.
- Ask the **update question** directly: what has changed your mind recently, and via what input.
- Separate **viewpoint narrowness** from **evidence narrowness**, and focus the recommendations on the second.
- Check **how disagreeing people are portrayed** in your inputs — whether you encounter their strongest arguments or only their worst representatives.
- Produce **two or three small, achievable adjustments**, not a media regimen.

### Must Not
- Prescribe a balanced-diet reading list, or recommend consuming sources the user considers false for balance's sake.
- Judge the user's political position, or treat any position as the correct destination of a healthy diet.
- Treat all algorithmic curation as harmful. Algorithms surface useful things; the issue is unexamined optimization and the feedback loop.
- Fabricate research findings, statistics about media effects, or claims about how a specific platform's ranking works.
- Recommend total disengagement, digital detox, or quitting platforms as the default answer. It is rarely sustained and usually unnecessary.
- Moralize about attention or screen time.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Write the named diet
What you would say if asked. Keep it — the comparison is the point.

### Step 2 — Reconstruct the actual diet
Screen time reports, browser history, subscriptions, and an honest account of ambient inputs: chats, background audio, headlines skimmed, comments read. Estimate hours per week for each.

### Step 3 — Put them side by side
Name the gap explicitly. For most people the largest real input does not appear in the named list at all.

### Step 4 — Attribute selection for every input
Who chose it: you deliberately, an algorithm, a friend or family member, an employer, or chance. Total the proportion that was not your choice.

### Step 5 — Score the emotional register
For each significant input, the dominant register: informational, analytical, outrage, threat, contempt, vindication, entertainment. Total the share operating on activation rather than information.

### Step 6 — Identify the feedback loops
Which inputs adapt to your engagement? Those inputs are converging on whatever holds your attention, which is a different objective from informing you — and it is not a claim about anyone's motives, just about what an optimizer does.

### Step 7 — Ask the update question
What have you genuinely changed your mind about in the last year, and what caused it? If nothing, ask whether your inputs are capable of producing an update at all.

### Step 8 — Check the portrayal of disagreement, then choose adjustments
Do you encounter the best version of views you reject, or only their worst representatives? Then pick two or three specific, small, achievable adjustments — and one you will actually keep.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Balance mistaken for calibration.** Adding sources you believe are false does not improve your picture. Evidence breadth is the target, not viewpoint symmetry.
2. **Named diet audited.** Auditing the sources you would list rather than the ones consuming your hours. The ambient stream is usually the majority.
3. **Algorithms treated as uniformly harmful.** They surface genuinely useful material. The concern is the unexamined feedback loop, not curation itself.
4. **Political judgment smuggled in.** Treating a particular set of conclusions as what a healthy diet produces. The audit is about process, not destination.
5. **Detox prescriptions.** Recommending abstinence, which is rarely sustained and usually unnecessary. Small durable adjustments beat dramatic ones.
6. **Fabricated media-effects claims.** Inventing statistics about polarization, ranking algorithms, or platform internals to make the point.
7. **Update question skipped.** It is the most uncomfortable and most diagnostic item. A year with no updates is a finding, not a gap in the exercise.
8. **Ambient sources ignored.** Family, colleagues, and group chats are among the most persuasive inputs anyone has and almost never appear in a self-reported diet.

## Verification

- [ ] Named diet and actual diet are compared, and the gap is stated explicitly.
- [ ] Ambient inputs — chats, people, background audio, headlines — are included in the actual diet.
- [ ] Selection is attributed for every input, with a total for what was not the user's choice.
- [ ] Emotional register is scored, with a total for activation-based inputs.
- [ ] Feedback loops are identified without asserting anything about platform motives.
- [ ] The update question is asked directly, and a blank answer is treated as a finding.
- [ ] Viewpoint narrowness and evidence narrowness are distinguished, and recommendations target evidence.
- [ ] No balanced-diet reading list is prescribed and no political position is judged.
- [ ] No media-effects statistic or platform-internals claim was fabricated.
- [ ] Adjustments are small, specific, and achievable, with one the user commits to.
