# Coercive Control Pattern Recognition

**Objective:** Help someone work out whether what they are living with matches documented patterns of **coercive control** — a course of conduct rather than a set of incidents — and move them toward trained support. Coercive control is characterized by isolation from support networks, monitoring and surveillance, restriction of money and resources, micro-regulation of ordinary daily decisions, and threats that may never be carried out but change everything about how a person lives. It frequently involves no physical violence at all, which is exactly why people living inside it struggle to name it and often conclude their situation "isn't bad enough" to warrant help.

**This prompt's destination is a trained person, not a conclusion.** Coercive control is a recognized pattern of abuse and in some jurisdictions a criminal offence; assessing it, planning around it, and leaving it safely are all things that require people with training, and the period around leaving is the period of highest risk. Nothing here substitutes for that. What this prompt can do is help someone see a pattern they have been inside too long to see, and take the next step toward support.

> **Safety — read first.**
> - **If you are in immediate danger, contact emergency services now.**
> - Contact a domestic abuse service in your country and talk to someone trained. **Do not rely on any phone number, website, or service name stated from memory by an AI, including this one.** Look it up from an official source — a government health or police website, or a hospital.
> - **If the person you are worried about may be able to see this device**, consider how you access support. Many abuse services publish guidance on covering your tracks; use a device the person cannot access — a friend's phone, a library computer, or a work machine — if you have any doubt. Clear this conversation if that is safer for you.
> - Leaving is often the most dangerous period. **Please do not build an exit plan alone.** Trained advocates do safety planning for exactly this reason.
> - This is not therapy, not legal advice, and not a risk assessment. It is a way of organizing what you are seeing.

**When to use:**
- You keep wondering whether your situation "counts," or whether you are overreacting.
- Your world has got smaller — fewer friends, less money of your own, less freedom over ordinary decisions — and you are not sure how that happened.
- You want to describe what is happening to a doctor, advocate, or lawyer and cannot find the words.
- You are worried about someone else and want to understand what you are seeing.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are in immediate danger. Contact emergency services rather than working through a prompt.
- The situation is a difficult relationship without fear or control — use `psyops_manipulation_recognition_personal.md`.
- You need to document incidents for a legal matter — use `domain-legal/family-self-advocacy/legalprep_incident_documentation_organizer.md`, and get a lawyer.
- The controlling environment is a group or organization rather than a relationship — use `psyops_high_control_group_dynamics_assessment.md`.

**Audience:** Anyone trying to understand their own situation, or worried about someone close to them. No background assumed.

## Inputs / Context

1. **What daily life looks like now.** An ordinary day: what you decide, what you ask about first, what you avoid.
2. **What changed, and over what period.** Coercive control is usually gradual, so the comparison that matters is with several years ago rather than last month.
3. **Your contact with other people.** Who you still see, who you have lost touch with, and how that happened.
4. **Money and resources.** Whose name things are in, what you can spend without asking, whether you have your own access to funds and documents.
5. **Monitoring.** Phone, location, messages, accounts, movements — what is checked, and whether it is framed as care or as a rule.
6. **What happens when you disagree.** The consequence, said or unsaid, and how you know it.
7. **What you want right now.** Understanding, or help, or documentation, or simply to know whether this counts. Any of these is a legitimate reason to be here.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Lead and close with **safety**, including device safety and the instruction to verify any resource from an official source.
- Frame coercive control as a **course of conduct over time**, not a checklist score — and never produce a numeric risk score.
- Explicitly address **"it's not bad enough"**, which is the single most common reason people delay getting help, and note that services support people at every stage including uncertainty.
- Note that **coercive control frequently involves no physical violence**, and that this does not make it lesser or ineligible for support.
- Make **trained support the destination** of the output, not a footnote.
- Warn plainly that **leaving is the highest-risk period** and that safety planning belongs with an advocate.
- Preserve the user's **autonomy**: they decide what this means and what to do.

### Must Not
- Tell the user to leave, stay, confront, or gather evidence in a way that could be discovered.
- Suggest recording, documenting, or collecting evidence without first flagging the risk if it is found. In some situations documentation is dangerous.
- State any hotline number, website, or service name as verified. Always instruct the user to look it up from an official source.
- Diagnose or label the other person.
- Assess lethality or risk level. That requires trained assessment, and a false reassurance here could be catastrophic.
- Suggest couples counselling or mediation as a route where control is present — mainstream guidance treats this as unsafe in coercive control situations.
- Minimize because there is no violence, or escalate to frighten. Both distort the user's ability to decide.
- Continue with pattern analysis if the user indicates immediate danger. Stop and route to emergency services.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Safety and device check, before anything else
Ask whether the user is safe right now, and whether the person could access this device or conversation. If there is immediate danger, stop and route to emergency services. If the device may be monitored, address that before continuing.

### Step 2 — Describe an ordinary day
Walk through a normal day as it is now. What is decided freely, what is checked first, what is avoided. Concrete and ordinary — the texture is where the pattern shows.

### Step 3 — Compare with several years ago
The same ordinary day, before. Gradual change is invisible from inside; this comparison is what makes it visible.

### Step 4 — Walk the documented pattern areas, gently
One at a time: isolation from people, monitoring and surveillance, control of money and resources, regulation of daily decisions (clothes, food, sleep, movement, who you speak to), threats and consequences, and degradation. For each, ask what happens rather than whether it counts.

### Step 5 — Name what has narrowed
Reflect back what has got smaller — contacts, money, freedom, confidence — without interpretation or verdict. Often this is the first time it has been laid out in one place.

### Step 6 — Address "it isn't bad enough"
Directly. Services support people who are uncertain, people with no violence in their situation, and people who are not leaving. Being unsure is a normal reason to call, not a disqualification.

### Step 7 — Route to support, concretely
Identify the type of service that fits — domestic abuse helpline, advocacy service, GP or doctor, or an employer's confidential support. Instruct the user to find current details from an official source. Note that services can talk through options without any commitment to act.

### Step 8 — One next step, chosen by the user
A single step they choose: making a call, telling one trusted person, or simply keeping this description. Close with the safety and device reminder.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Checklist scoring.** Producing a tally or risk level. Coercive control is a course of conduct; a score is both unreliable and dangerous if it reassures.
2. **"Not bad enough" left unaddressed.** The main reason people delay. It must be addressed explicitly, not implied.
3. **Documentation advised without risk warning.** Telling someone to keep a log without flagging what happens if it is found.
4. **Fabricated resources.** Any number, URL, or service name stated from memory. Always route to official verification.
5. **Device blindness.** Working through this without asking whether the conversation is visible to the person concerned.
6. **Mediation suggested.** Recommending couples counselling or mediation where control is present, which mainstream guidance treats as unsafe.
7. **Ordinary conflict escalated.** Applying this frame to a difficult but non-controlling relationship. Route those to `psyops_manipulation_recognition_personal.md`.
8. **Analysis continued past a danger disclosure.** Carrying on with pattern work when the user has said they are in immediate danger.
