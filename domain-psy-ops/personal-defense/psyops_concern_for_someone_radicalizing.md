# Worried About Someone

**Objective:** Help someone who is frightened that a person they love is being drawn into something harmful to do three things: **separate what they have actually observed from what they fear**, **distinguish a changed opinion from the specific indicators that warrant outside help**, and **reach the services that exist for precisely this situation**. Countries have specialist family support and safeguarding services staffed by people who do this work daily. Most people worried about a family member do not know those services exist, and many are afraid that contacting anyone means reporting their child to the police. That fear keeps families isolated at the point where help matters most.

The prompt is built around a finding that consistently emerges from work in this area: **the relationship is the protective factor**. Confrontation, ultimatums, argument about the ideology, and cutting off contact reliably push people further in and remove the last connection to a world outside the movement. Almost everything intuition suggests here is counterproductive, and the hardest instruction in this prompt is usually "stay in touch and do not argue about the beliefs."

It also takes seriously that **most worrying changes are not radicalization**. Adolescence, political awakening, grief, isolation, a new relationship, depression, and ordinary contrarianism produce most of what alarms families. Concluding radicalization from a set of opinions you find repellent is both common and harmful.

> **Safety — read first.**
> - **If you believe someone is planning to harm themselves or anyone else, contact emergency services now.** Do not work through a prompt. This is the one situation where speed matters more than everything else in this document.
> - There are specialist family support and safeguarding services in most countries for exactly this concern, and many are confidential and non-police. **Look them up from an official source — a government safeguarding or health website — rather than relying on a service name or number stated from memory by an AI, including this one.**
> - Contacting a support service is not the same as reporting someone to the police. Ask the service what happens with the information before you share details, if that worry is holding you back.
> - If the person is a child or teenager, a school safeguarding lead or GP is a legitimate and lower-stakes first contact.
> - This is not a risk assessment and cannot tell you how dangerous a situation is. That requires trained people.

**When to use:**
- Someone you love has changed and you are frightened, and you cannot tell whether your fear is proportionate.
- You want to work out what you have actually seen before talking to anyone about it.
- You want to know what kind of help exists and whether contacting it will make things worse.
- You need to know what to do and — more importantly — what not to do.

**When NOT to use:**
- You believe harm is being planned. Contact emergency services now.
- Your concern is about a controlling group's structure rather than a person's trajectory — use `psyops_high_control_group_dynamics_assessment.md`.
- The person is a teenager and the concern is a friend group or online community generally — see `domain-parenting/caregiver-facing/ages-13-18/parenting_teen_friend_group_concerns.md`.

**Audience:** Parents, partners, siblings, friends. No background assumed. Written for someone frightened.

## Inputs / Context

1. **What you have actually observed.** Specific things said or done, with rough dates. Not impressions — observations.
2. **What has changed, and over what period.** Behaviour, routine, relationships, sleep, mood, appearance, language.
3. **Their situation.** Losses, isolation, bullying, unemployment, bereavement, illness, a new relationship, a move.
4. **Your relationship now.** How much contact, how it feels, what you can still talk about.
5. **What you have already tried.** Especially arguments — how they went, and what happened afterwards.
6. **What specifically frightens you.** Named plainly. Sometimes this is a concrete observation and sometimes it is dread, and telling them apart is much of the work.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Lead with the **immediate-harm routing**, and stop the prompt if there is any indication of planned harm.
- Separate **observations from interpretations from fears** into distinct categories.
- Distinguish **changed opinions** — however objectionable — from the specific concerning indicators: withdrawal from everyone outside, secrecy about contacts, dehumanizing language about a group of people, fixation on grievance, contact with people encouraging harm, and expressed intent or fascination with violence.
- State plainly that **the relationship is the protective factor**, and that maintaining contact matters more than winning any argument.
- Explain **what support services actually do** and address the fear that contact means police involvement.
- Address the **alternative explanations** — adolescence, depression, isolation, grief, ordinary political change — as live and usually more likely.
- Include something for the **user's own wellbeing**. People in this position are frequently not sleeping and not talking to anyone.

### Must Not
- Advise confronting, arguing about the ideology, issuing ultimatums, monitoring covertly, or cutting off contact. These are the common instincts and they are counterproductive.
- Provide any intervention script, deradicalization technique, or counter-argument set. This work is done by trained practitioners and amateur attempts reliably backfire.
- Assess risk or dangerousness, or reassure the user that a situation is safe. Neither is possible here and false reassurance could be catastrophic.
- Diagnose the person, or apply a label to them.
- State any service name, helpline, or website as verified from memory.
- Treat objectionable political or religious opinions as radicalization in themselves. Opinions are not the indicator; the behavioural pattern is.
- Encourage surveillance of the person's devices or accounts, which if discovered destroys the relationship that is protecting them.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Immediate-harm check, first
Is there any indication of planned harm to the person or anyone else? If yes, stop and contact emergency services now. Everything else in this document is for the situation where the answer is no.

### Step 2 — Sort into three columns
Take everything you are carrying and split it: **observed** (specific things said or done), **interpreted** (what you concluded), **feared** (what you imagine might happen). Most people find the feared column is the largest and the observed column the smallest — which is not a reason to dismiss the fear, but it changes what you can act on.

### Step 3 — Separate opinions from indicators
Go through the observed column and mark each item: is this an opinion you find objectionable, or is it one of the concerning behavioural indicators — total withdrawal from outside relationships, secrecy about who they are in contact with, dehumanizing language about a category of people, fixation on grievance, contact with people encouraging harm, expressed intent or fascination with violence?

### Step 4 — Consider the alternatives seriously
For the same observations: adolescence, depression, isolation, grief, a new relationship, ordinary political change, or a search for meaning and belonging. These are usually more likely and they are not mutually exclusive with your concern.

### Step 5 — Look at what is underneath
What need is this meeting for them — belonging, purpose, status, an explanation for something painful, a response to real unfairness they have experienced? This is not endorsement. It is the thing any effective help will have to work with.

### Step 6 — Protect the relationship, deliberately
Name what you can still do together that has nothing to do with any of this. Contact that does not involve the subject is the most valuable thing you have, and arguing about the beliefs is what usually destroys it.

### Step 7 — Route to support, concretely
Identify what kind of service fits — specialist family support, safeguarding, GP, school safeguarding lead, or a counsellor for you. Instruct the user to find details from an official source. Address the police fear directly: ask the service what happens with the information before sharing specifics.

### Step 8 — Choose one step, and one thing for yourself
One step toward support. Then one thing for the user's own wellbeing — sleep, one person to tell, or their own appointment. This is a long situation and they cannot do it depleted.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Opinions read as radicalization.** Repellent views are not the indicator. The behavioural pattern is — withdrawal, secrecy, dehumanization, grievance fixation, and intent.
2. **Confrontation advised.** The strongest instinct and among the most harmful. It confirms the movement's account that outsiders will reject them, and it costs the relationship.
3. **Amateur intervention.** Supplying counter-arguments or deradicalization scripts. This is skilled work and untrained attempts entrench.
4. **Reassurance offered.** Telling a frightened person it is probably fine. Risk cannot be assessed here and false comfort could be catastrophic.
5. **Alternatives skipped.** Not seriously weighing depression, adolescence, grief, and isolation, which explain most of what alarms families.
6. **Surveillance suggested.** Covert monitoring, which if found ends the relationship and removes the protective factor.
7. **Fabricated services.** Naming a helpline or program from memory. Always route to official verification.
8. **The worried person forgotten.** Producing a plan for the other person and nothing for the user, who is often not sleeping and not talking to anyone.
