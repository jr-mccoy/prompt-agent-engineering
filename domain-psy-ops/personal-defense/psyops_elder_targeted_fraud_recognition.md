# Elder-Targeted Fraud Recognition

**Objective:** Help an older adult — or someone who loves one — recognize the fraud approaches **designed around later life**, and handle the conversation that follows. The general structure is the same as any pretext (a manufactured reason, urgency, an action that cannot wait), and `psyops_social_engineering_pretext_recognition.md` covers it. What differs here is the **design of the approach** and the **shape of the family problem**.

The approaches are tuned to real features of later life: the love of grandchildren and the fear of their being in trouble; widowhood and loneliness, which companionship schemes exploit over months; unfamiliarity with changing technology; respect for authority; retirement savings concentrated in accessible accounts; and, often, living alone with no one to ask in the moment. The schemes that last longest — romance and companionship frauds — do not feel like fraud at all to the person inside them, because the relationship is real to them even when the other party is not.

The family problem is the other half, and it is where most well-meant help goes wrong. An adult child who discovers a parent has been defrauded tends to respond with alarm, blame, or a move to take control — and the person, ashamed, stops telling anyone, which is exactly what the next approach depends on. **The goal is to make it easy to ask**, not to remove the person's independence. Questions of whether someone can still manage their own affairs are real and sometimes urgent, but they belong to doctors and legal professionals, not to a family argument.

> **Safety.** **If money has been sent, codes or passwords shared, or someone given remote access to a device, act now:** contact the bank or card provider immediately using a number from the card, a statement, or an independently navigated official site, and report to the police and the relevant national fraud reporting body. **Do not use a number given by the contact, and do not rely on a number or service name stated from memory by an AI, including this one.** If an older adult is being threatened, coerced, or financially exploited by someone they know, most places have an adult-safeguarding or protective service — look it up from an official government source. If anyone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services. Being targeted is not a sign of declining judgment; these schemes are professionally built and they work on careful, capable people of every age.

**When to use:**
- An older adult has received a call, message, or visit that might be fraud, and wants to check it.
- A family member is worried about a pattern — new online relationships, unexplained withdrawals, secrecy about money.
- An older adult has been defrauded, and the family needs to respond without making things worse.
- A family wants to agree a simple shared rule before anything happens.

**When NOT to use:**
- Money, codes, or access have already been given — act first, using the safety steps above, then return here.
- The approach is a general pretext not tuned to later life — use `psyops_social_engineering_pretext_recognition.md`.
- You need to prepare a formal fraud report — use `domain-legal/personal-self-advocacy/consumer-scams/legalprep_scam_fraud_report_preparer.md`.
- The broader question is care, housing, or support for an aging parent — see `domain-personal-development/major-decisions/personal_caring_for_aging_parent.md`.

**Audience:** Older adults and their families. No background assumed.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Who is using this prompt.** The older adult themselves, or a family member. The steps differ.
2. **The approach or pattern.** What happened, through what channel, over what period, and what was asked.
3. **Money or access involved.** Anything sent, promised, shared, or installed — and when.
4. **The relationship to the contact.** A stranger, an online companion, someone claiming to be family, someone claiming authority, or someone the person knows in real life.
5. **How the older adult feels about it.** Embarrassed, defensive, certain it is genuine, frightened. This shapes the conversation more than the facts do.
6. **Existing family arrangements.** Who talks to whom about money, and what the person has said they want.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Put **already-acted steps first** when money, codes, or access have been given.
- Recognize the **later-life-specific approaches**: family-emergency impersonation, companionship and romance schemes, authority and tech-support impersonation, investment and prize approaches.
- Resolve to **out-of-band verification** — calling the grandchild, the bank, or the agency on a number found independently — as the core habit.
- Treat the older adult as **a capable adult making their own decisions**, and plan the conversation to preserve their dignity and independence.
- Make **"it's easy to ask"** the family goal, with a shared, simple rule agreed in advance.
- Route questions of **capacity, coercion by someone known, or financial exploitation** to doctors, legal professionals, and adult-safeguarding services, looked up from official sources.
- Run the **ordinary-explanation reading**: what legitimate relationship, purchase, or decision might explain the pattern.
- Close on **a next step the user chooses**.

### Must Not
- State any bank, agency, or service number, URL, or name from memory.
- Frame being targeted as evidence of cognitive decline, or suggest taking control of someone's finances as a family decision.
- Advise engaging the contact to "catch" or test them.
- Advise confronting an online companion on the older adult's behalf, or contacting them to expose them.
- Script a conversation designed to pressure, trick, or overrule the older adult.
- Shame the person, or suggest they should have known better.
- Diagnose capacity or cognitive state.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Act first if anything has been given
If money, codes, passwords, or device access have been given, stop and follow the safety steps now. Speed is what limits the loss. Return afterwards.

### Step 2 — Name the approach type
Match what happened to the approach types: a grandchild or relative in sudden trouble who asks you not to tell the parents; an online companion who cannot meet, has an emergency, and needs money; a bank, government, or tech-support caller warning of a problem only they can fix; an investment, prize, or inheritance that requires a fee first. Note what matches and what does not. Whatever the story, note how payment is being requested: a demand for gift cards, cryptocurrency, a wire transfer, cash handed to a courier, or money moved to a "safe account" is a warning sign on its own, because legitimate banks, agencies, and relatives do not ask for payment that way.

### Step 3 — Find the secrecy and urgency
Almost every later-life scheme asks for **secrecy** ("don't tell your son, he'll worry") alongside urgency. The request not to tell family is the strongest single indicator in this category, because it is what the scheme needs.

### Step 4 — Verify out of band
Call the grandchild directly on a number you already have. Call the bank on the number from your card. Call the agency on a number from its official site, found yourself. Legitimate people and organizations always accept this.

### Step 5 — Run the ordinary-explanation reading
Write the ordinary account: a real new friendship, a legitimate purchase, a considered gift, a financial decision the family would not have made but is the person's to make. Note what it explains and what it does not.

### Step 6 — Plan the family conversation
If you are family: choose a calm moment, lead with care rather than alarm, ask rather than tell, and do not open with the money. If you are the older adult: choose one person you trust enough to check things with.

### Step 7 — Agree a shared rule
A single sentence both sides agree to, such as: "Anyone asking for money or secrecy, I call you first — and you never make me feel foolish for it." Write it down.

### Step 8 — Choose a next step
From: verify out of band now; contact the bank; report to the police or fraud reporting body; have the family conversation; agree the shared rule; speak to a legal professional or adult-safeguarding service; do nothing further. The user chooses.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Every new friend read as a scheme.** Older adults form real new relationships, online and off. The indicators are secrecy, inability to meet, and money requests — not the relationship itself.
2. **Independent decisions read as fraud.** A gift, a purchase, or an investment the family disagrees with is the person's decision unless there is evidence of deception or coercion.
3. **Targeting read as decline.** Being approached, or even defrauded, says nothing reliable about someone's capacity; the schemes work on capable people.
4. **Caller ID and details trusted.** Displayed numbers, knowledge of names, and even a voice that sounds like a grandchild can all be faked.
5. **Verification through their channel.** Calling back a number the contact supplied, which reaches the fraud.
6. **Alarm as a first response.** A family reaction of shock and blame, which teaches the person to hide the next approach.
7. **Takeover as protection.** Moving to control someone's money or devices without their agreement, which damages trust and may itself be wrong.
8. **Romance frauds argued away.** Trying to argue someone out of a relationship they experience as real, which usually entrenches it; verification and trusted outside help work better than debate.
