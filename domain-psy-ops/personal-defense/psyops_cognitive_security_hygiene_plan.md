# Cognitive Security Hygiene Plan

**Objective:** Build a small set of **pre-committed rules** that hold in the states where you are actually vulnerable. This is the central design constraint: nobody is exploited while calm, rested, and unhurried. They are exploited while frightened, flattered, rushed, lonely, furious, or badly wanting something to be true. Any defense that requires good judgment in the moment fails precisely when it is needed, because the moment is engineered to remove good judgment.

So the plan does not aim at general skepticism, which is exhausting, corrosive to relationships, and ineffective anyway. It identifies **your specific exploitable states** from your own history, and attaches a small number of rules that execute mechanically without requiring you to assess anything. A rule that requires you to first decide whether you are being manipulated is not a rule; it is a hope.

Keep it small. Three to five rules that survive a real Tuesday beat fifteen aspirational ones. And the most effective single rule for almost everyone is a **delay** — because nearly every manipulation depends on compressing the time between the approach and the decision.

**When to use:**
- Something got past you and you want a durable fix rather than a resolution to be more careful.
- You are entering a period of higher exposure — a job change, a bereavement, a move, a health crisis, a new relationship, a large transaction.
- You want a personal standard that does not require you to be suspicious of everyone.
- You are helping someone else — a parent, a teenager, a friend — build one.

**When NOT to use:**
- You are in an active approach right now — use `psyops_social_engineering_pretext_recognition.md`.
- The problem is what forms your beliefs rather than how you decide under pressure — use `psyops_information_diet_audit.md`.
- You want general habit design — use `domain-personal-development/prompts/habits/`.

**Audience:** Anyone. Particularly useful during a life transition, and for helping a family member build one.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Past incidents.** Times you were manipulated, scammed, pressured into something, or made a decision you later could not explain. Near-misses count and are often more informative.
2. **The state you were in each time.** Rushed, frightened, flattered, lonely, grieving, tired, angry, hopeful. This is the input the whole plan is built from.
3. **Your current exposure.** What you hold that is worth targeting — money, access, credentials, authority, a public profile, responsibility for others.
4. **Your existing rules.** Anything you already do reflexively that has worked.
5. **Who you would call.** The person you would actually contact before a significant decision, if anyone.
6. **What you will realistically sustain.** Honestly — the plan must survive an ordinary bad week.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Derive rules from the user's **own history**, not from a generic threat list.
- Target the **states**, not the tactics. Tactics change constantly; the states in which you are exploitable are stable across a lifetime.
- Make every rule **executable without judgment** — no rule may require the user to first determine whether they are being manipulated.
- Include a **delay rule**, in some form, because time defeats nearly every pressure mechanism.
- Include a **second-person rule**: someone to contact before a threshold decision. External perspective is the strongest single defense and the least used.
- Set **explicit thresholds** — amounts, categories, and situations that trigger each rule — so no in-the-moment assessment is needed.
- Keep the set to **three to five rules**.
- Include a **review trigger**: when to revisit, usually after any near-miss or life transition.

### Must Not
- Prescribe general vigilance, suspicion, or "trust your instincts." Instincts are the attack surface, and vigilance is not sustainable.
- Build rules requiring the user to detect manipulation before acting.
- Produce a long list. Anything above five rules will not be used and its existence provides false comfort.
- Recommend isolation, disengagement, or refusing contact as a strategy.
- Fabricate fraud statistics or claims about how often specific approaches succeed.
- Shame the user for past incidents. These approaches are professionally built and work on informed, careful people.
- Make rules that are socially impossible to execute — a rule you would be too embarrassed to use is not a rule.

## Instructions

### Step 1 — List the incidents without judgment
Every time you were pressured, deceived, or made a decision you could not later explain. Near-misses too. No self-criticism; you need honest data more than you need to feel good about it.

### Step 2 — Name the state you were in each time
For each incident, what state were you in? Look for repetition — most people have two or three recurring states, and those are the entire target.

### Step 3 — Identify your recurring exploitable states
Name them plainly. "When I'm rushed and someone senior is waiting." "When I'm flattered about my expertise." "When I'm lonely." "When I want it to be true." These are not character flaws; they are the specific conditions under which anyone's judgment degrades.

### Step 4 — Map exposure to states
What could be lost while in each state? Money, credentials, access, information, a commitment, a relationship. This sets which rules are worth the friction.

### Step 5 — Write the delay rule
Some version of: no decision above [threshold] in the moment it is presented. Set a concrete threshold and a concrete delay. This one rule handles most cases.

### Step 6 — Write the second-person rule
Name an actual person and the trigger for contacting them. "Before any financial commitment over £X, I tell [name] first." The person does not need to be an expert — external perspective is what does the work, and describing it aloud is often enough.

### Step 7 — Write one or two state-specific rules
For your recurring states, one mechanical rule each. Mechanical means it executes on a trigger without assessment: a phrase you say, a step you take, a thing you always do.

### Step 8 — Test each rule against the worst case, then set the review trigger
For each rule, imagine the most convincing, most urgent version of your worst incident, and check whether the rule still executes. If it requires you to first realize what is happening, rewrite it. Then set when you will revisit.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Vigilance as a plan.** "Be more careful" is not a rule and does not survive being tired. Only mechanical rules with triggers work.
2. **Detection-dependent rules.** Any rule beginning "if this seems suspicious" fails, because a good approach does not seem suspicious.
3. **Too many rules.** Above five, none get used, and the list produces a false sense of protection.
4. **Tactic-targeted rules.** Building defenses against last year's scam. Tactics update constantly; states do not.
5. **Instinct trusted.** "Trust your gut" is exactly backwards — manipulation works by producing the right gut feeling.
6. **Socially impossible rules.** A rule you would be too embarrassed to invoke with a senior colleague or a charming stranger is not a rule. Test the social cost.
7. **Isolation as defense.** Refusing contact and disengaging is unsustainable and cuts off the external perspective that is your best protection.
8. **Shame in the analysis.** Self-criticism corrupts the incident data, and the incident data is the entire input.

- [ ] Rules target recurring states rather than specific tactics.
- [ ] No rule requires the user to first detect manipulation.
- [ ] A delay rule with a concrete threshold and period is present.
- [ ] A second-person rule naming an actual person and trigger is present.
- [ ] The rule set is three to five rules, not more.
- [ ] Every rule passed the worst-case test and the social-cost check.
