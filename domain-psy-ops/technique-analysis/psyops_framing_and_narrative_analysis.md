# Framing and Narrative Analysis

**Objective:** Recover the frame a message imposes on a situation and make its choices visible. A frame is a set of decisions, usually invisible: which metaphor organizes the subject, who is cast as agent and who as object, **where the clock starts**, what is foregrounded versus treated as unchangeable background, and which questions become difficult to even ask. Framing is not a defect — all communication frames, because unframed description is impossible. The analytic payoff is in noticing the choice and testing the alternatives, because the most consequential framing work is done not by what a message asserts but by what it quietly settles in advance.

The method is reconstruction plus substitution: recover the original frame, then re-narrate the identical facts under two different frames. What changes across the three versions is the frame's actual work; what stays constant is the factual core.

**When to use:**
- A debate feels stuck and you suspect the disagreement is about the frame rather than the facts.
- You are analyzing coverage of a contested issue and want to characterize its framing rigorously.
- You are writing about something and want to know what your own framing is doing.
- You keep losing an argument on ground you never chose.

**When NOT to use:**
- You want a technique inventory of a specific artifact — use `psyops_propaganda_technique_identification.md`.
- You are tracking how a narrative spread rather than how it is built — use `../influence-operations/psyops_narrative_lifecycle_tracker.md`.
- The question is whether specific claims are true — use `domain-reasoning-craft/epistemic/epistemic_evidence_quality_score.md`.
- You want to rebuild your own understanding from scratch — use `domain-reasoning-craft/reasoning-moves/reasoning_first_principles_reconstruction.md`.

**Audience:** Analysts, journalists, policy staff, educators, and anyone who suspects they are arguing inside someone else's frame.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The message or corpus.** One artifact, or a set of coverage on the same subject.
2. **The underlying situation.** The events or state of affairs being framed, described as neutrally as you can manage.
3. **The contested question.** What is actually in dispute, if anything.
4. **Competing frames you already know of.** How other parties describe the same situation.
5. **Your own frame.** How you would naturally describe it — recorded up front, because you cannot analyze from nowhere.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Identify the **organizing metaphor** and what it imports: war, disease, market, family, journey, machine, flood, invasion.
- Map the **cast**: who acts, who is acted upon, who is absent, and who has been made into scenery.
- Locate **where the clock starts**. Which prior events are excluded by the chosen starting point is often the single largest framing move.
- Distinguish **foreground from background**: what is presented as changeable versus what is presented as given.
- Name the **unaskable questions** — those the frame makes awkward, irrelevant, or unintelligible.
- Produce **two genuine alternative framings** of the same facts, each built in good faith rather than as a strawman.
- Identify the **invariant core**: what remains true across all three framings. That is the factual residue.
- State your **own frame** and how it differs from the one analyzed.

### Must Not
- Treat framing as inherently deceptive. Every description frames; the analysis is about which choices were made, not that choices exist.
- Build alternative frames as strawmen. If the alternatives are absurd, the comparison proves nothing.
- Assert that the framing was chosen deliberately. Most framing is inherited from a professional register, a house style, or an ambient consensus.
- Confuse framing analysis with fact-checking. A frame can be tendentious and every fact in it accurate.
- Fabricate the excluded history when identifying where the clock starts. If you do not know what preceded, say so.
- Present your own preferred frame as the neutral one. There is no view from nowhere, including yours.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Record your own framing first
Describe the situation in your own words before analyzing. This is the baseline you will need to correct for.

### Step 2 — Extract the organizing metaphor
What is this being treated as? Trace what the metaphor imports: a war frame imports enemies, fronts, victory, and traitors; a disease frame imports contagion, quarantine, and cure; a market frame imports rational actors and efficiency.

### Step 3 — Map the cast
List agents (who does things), objects (who has things done to them), and the absent (who is materially involved but does not appear). Note passive constructions that delete an actor.

### Step 4 — Find where the clock starts
Identify the first event the message treats as the beginning. Then ask what happened immediately before it, and what accepting that starting point concedes.

### Step 5 — Separate foreground from background
What is presented as a live variable, and what is presented as fixed context? Frames do much of their work by relocating contested things into the background.

### Step 6 — List the unaskable questions
Which reasonable questions would sound strange, hostile, or off-topic inside this frame? These mark the frame's boundaries better than anything it asserts.

### Step 7 — Re-narrate under two alternative frames
Take the identical facts and tell them twice more under genuinely different organizing metaphors, each in good faith. Note what becomes visible and what becomes invisible in each.

### Step 8 — Extract the invariant core and run the adversarial check
State what survives all three framings — the factual residue. Then argue that the original frame is simply the most accurate one and the alternatives distort. Settle it or say it is unsettled.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Framing treated as lying.** Concluding that a frame is deceptive because it is a frame. Unframed communication does not exist; only the specific choices are assessable.
2. **Strawman alternatives.** Building alternatives no one holds, so the original looks obviously correct. The alternatives must be ones a reasonable, informed person actually uses.
3. **Deliberateness assumed.** Reading inherited professional convention as a designed manipulation. House style and register produce most framing.
4. **Analyst's frame as neutral.** Presenting your own preferred description as the unframed version. State yours as one of the three.
5. **Fabricated prior history.** Inventing what happened before the clock started in order to establish exclusion. Unknown history is unknown.
6. **Frame-fact conflation.** Treating a tendentious frame as evidence the facts are wrong, or accurate facts as evidence the frame is fair. They are independent.
7. **Single-artifact overreach.** Generalizing from one piece to a publication's or movement's framing. One artifact establishes one artifact.
8. **Infinite regress.** Reframing endlessly without extracting the invariant core, which leaves the reader with nothing but relativism. The factual residue is the point.

## Verification

- [ ] The organizing metaphor is named and what it imports is traced.
- [ ] The clock-start is located and the excluded prior events are identified or marked unknown.
- [ ] The cast map includes the absent, and passive constructions that delete actors are noted.
- [ ] Unaskable questions are listed.
- [ ] Two alternative framings are built in good faith and would be recognized by people who hold them.
- [ ] The invariant core is extracted, so the analysis lands on facts rather than relativism.
- [ ] The analyst's own frame is stated as one framing among three, not as the neutral baseline.
- [ ] No claim is made that the framing was deliberately chosen.
- [ ] Framing assessment is kept separate from factual accuracy throughout.
- [ ] No prior history was invented to strengthen the exclusion finding.
