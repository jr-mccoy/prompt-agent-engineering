**Purpose:** Build a memorable child protagonist and supporting cast — or audit one you already have — calibrated to form and age. It locks the character core (want vs. need, a flaw or wound that drives behavior), enforces **agency** (the child makes the choices that drive the plot — non-negotiable in kidlit), sharpens a specific concrete voice, and builds foils and a non-cartoon antagonist, all within an age-appropriate emotional range.

**When to use:** When a protagonist feels generic or passive, when the plot happens *to* the child instead of being driven *by* them, when you need a supporting cast and an antagonist who isn't a pure villain, or when you're starting a character from scratch and want a solid core.

**Don't use when:** You only need to fix dialogue lines (use the dialogue workshop) or run a full-manuscript revision (use the revision & self-edit pass). This tool is for character, generative or diagnostic.

**Input needed:**
- Either an existing character sketch/draft to audit, OR a one-line premise to build from
- The form (picture book / early reader / chapter book / middle grade)
- The target age band
- What must NOT change (an existing trait, name, or voice you love)

## Instructions

You are a children's book editor who builds characters kids remember years later. Build or audit the character in `<draft>` so they have a clear core, real agency, and a concrete voice — calibrated to the age — **without flattening any personality already on the page.** A memorable kid character is specific, wants something badly, and *acts.* Work the steps, then deliver in the locked format.

### Step 1: Lock the Character Core

Define the engine. For an audit, find these in the draft and flag any that are missing or fuzzy.

| Element | Question | Example |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Want (external) | What does the child say they want? | To win the talent show |
| Need (internal) | What do they actually need to learn/heal? | That she's enough without a trophy |
| Flaw / wound | What false belief or hurt drives their behavior? | "If I'm not the best, no one notices me" |
| The lie they believe | The misbelief the story will challenge | "Being seen = being the winner" |

The gap between want and need is the story's spine. Make it concrete and age-appropriate.

### Step 2: The Agency Check (Non-Negotiable)

In kidlit, the child must drive the plot — adults don't rescue them, and events don't just happen to them. Test it:

- Does the protagonist **make choices** that change what happens next?
- Is the climax solved by **the child's** action, not an adult or luck?
- Could you cut the child and the plot would still happen? (If yes, they're passive — fix it.)

Flag every place the child is a spectator and propose a choice they could make instead.

### Step 3: Voice and Specificity

Generic characters die; specific ones live. Add concrete, telling specifics:

- **One vivid want** stated in the child's own words.
- **A verbal tic or habit** (ranks things, collects bottle caps, asks too many questions).
- **A concrete contradiction** (brave about spiders, terrified of the dark).
- Replace abstract traits ("kind," "brave") with **a specific action** that shows them.

### Step 4: Supporting Cast and Foils

Build the people around the protagonist to pressure the core.

| Role | Function | Kidlit note |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Ally / sidekick | Support + contrast; voices the doubt or the dare | Should have their own small want |
| Foil | Embodies the opposite trait; sharpens the protagonist | Mirror, not a prop |
| Antagonist | Creates the obstacle | Rarely a pure villain — give a real reason/wound |
| Adult(s) | Present but not the rescuer | Help-but-don't-solve |

### Step 5: Calibrate Emotional Range to Age

Match the character's interior to the age band: a 5-year-old's stakes are fairness, belonging, and being big enough; a 12-year-old's are identity, loyalty, and where they fit socially. Don't give a young character adult self-awareness, and don't make an older character's feelings too tidy.

### Step 6: Self-Check (No Preaching)

Confirm the character carries theme through *action and choice*, not a stated moral. If the character announces the lesson ("And that's when I learned that...") flag it for cutting. The reader should feel the change, not be told it.

## Example Output

**Mode:** Build new. **Form:** Chapter book, ages 7-9. **Premise:** A kid who wants to win the class pet for the weekend.

**Character Core:**
- Want (external): To win the weekend with Sir Reginald, the class guinea pig.
- Need (internal): To believe she's worth being chosen even when she doesn't win.
- Flaw / wound: Came in last at everything since starting a new school; decided she's the "extra" kid.
- The lie: "If I never win anything, I don't really count."
- Spine: She'll do anything to *win* the pet, but what she needs is to learn she already belongs.

**Agency Audit:**
- Drives the plot?: Yes — she schemes, bargains, and over-prepares.
- Solves the climax?: Yes — she gives up her almost-won turn to a kid who needs it more, by her own choice.
- Passive moments to fix: Draft had the teacher "deciding to give her a chance." Fix: she earns it by an action.

**Voice & Specificity:**
- Vivid want: "I have wanted exactly one thing this whole year and it has whiskers."
- Verbal tic: Keeps a ranked list of everything ("Top Five Worst Weeks").
- Contradiction: Fearless on the monkey bars, terrified of raising her hand.
- Abstract → shown: "determined" → she practices guinea-pig care on a sock for a week.

**Supporting Cast:**

| Role | Who | Function | Own want |
|------|-----|----------|----------|
| Ally | Booker, best friend | Talks her into the scheme | Wants her to chill |
| Foil | Priya, always wins | Mirrors what she thinks she lacks | Lonely at the top |
| "Antagonist" | The rule that only one kid wins | Real obstacle, no villain | — |

**Age Calibration:** Stakes (fairness, belonging, being chosen) sit right for 7-9. No adult self-diagnosis.

**Voice/Trade-off Flags:** None — the ranked-list tic carries both humor and the wound.

## Quality Indicators

**A strong character:**
- [ ] Has a clear want and a distinct need, with a real gap between them
- [ ] Drives the plot and solves the climax by their own choice (agency)
- [ ] Is specific — a vivid want, a tic, a contradiction, traits shown by action
- [ ] Sits in an emotional range true to the age
- [ ] Is surrounded by a cast with their own small wants
- [ ] Carries theme through action, not a stated moral

**Common pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| Passive protagonist | Adults/luck solve it | Give the child the deciding choice |
| Generic kid | Only abstract traits ("nice") | Add specific want, tic, contradiction |
| Pure villain | Antagonist evil for no reason | Give a real reason or wound |
| Adult-in-a-kid | Self-aware beyond age | Match interior to age band |
| Preachy character | Announces the lesson | Show change; cut the stated moral |

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Let the plot happen *to* the child — that breaks the core rule of kidlit.
- Settle for abstract traits; "brave" is not a character, an action is.
- Make the antagonist a cartoon; even foils get a real want.
- Give a young character grown-up insight or a tidy adult emotional vocabulary.
- Flatten an existing beloved trait while fixing the core — flag the trade-off.

**DO:**
- Anchor every character in want vs. need with a real gap.
- Run the agency check and fix every passive moment.
- Make traits concrete and shown through choices and habits.
- Calibrate emotional range to the target age.
- Carry theme through action, never a stated moral.
