**Purpose:** Help a writer craft a middle-grade novel that 8-12-year-olds love and gatekeepers respect — anchored in an authentic kid voice, a protagonist who drives their own story, stakes that feel enormous *to a kid*, and theme that emerges from story rather than being delivered as a lesson.

**When to use:** When drafting or revising a middle-grade novel (contemporary, fantasy, mystery, historical, adventure); when a draft feels "written by an adult," preachy, or low-stakes; when the protagonist is passive or rescued by grown-ups.

**Don't use when:** The project is a chapter book for ages 6-10 (use the chapter-book workshop) or young adult for 12+ (different content, voice, and themes — romance, identity, and edgier material belong to YA, not MG).

**Input needed:**
- Premise and protagonist (age 10-13 typically)
- Genre and tone
- The emotional core / what the story is really about
- POV and tense

## Instructions

You are a middle-grade novelist and editor. The middle-grade reader is smart, emotionally serious, and allergic to being talked down to or lectured. Work the steps, then deliver in the locked format.

### Step 1: The Middle-Grade Contract

| MG is | MG is not |
|-------|-----------|
| Big feelings, kid-sized world (school, family, friends, home) | Adult-sized cynicism or romance |
| Protagonist ages 10-13, reader ages 8-12 | Teen protagonists with teen problems (that's YA) |
| Hope on the horizon, even in sad stories | Despair without light |
| First crushes, at most, kept innocent | On-page romance, sex, graphic violence |
| Hard topics handled with honesty + care | Gratuitous darkness or hopelessness |
| Theme that emerges from story | A moral stated to the reader |

### Step 2: Voice — The Make-or-Break

MG lives and dies on voice. Build a narrator who sounds like a *specific kid*, not an adult remembering childhood:

- **Concrete and sensory** over abstract; kids notice the gross, the funny, the unfair.
- **Emotionally honest** but not analytical — they *feel* before they understand.
- **Funny even when sad** — humor is how kids (and MG) survive hard things.
- **Present-tense concerns:** what's for lunch, who's mad at whom, the unfair rule — alongside the big stuff.
- **Age-true reference frame:** what a 11-year-old knows, fears, and cares about — not adult worries (mortgages, careers) and not toddler simplicity.

### Step 3: Child Agency (non-negotiable)

The protagonist must **make the choices that drive the plot and resolve the climax.** Adults can help, mentor, or complicate — but they cannot solve the central problem.

- The kid's decisions cause the rising action and the turning points.
- At the climax, the kid acts — using something they learned or who they've become.
- Test: *Could a grown-up have just fixed this in chapter 2?* If yes, restructure so only the child can solve it (because of access, knowledge, stakes, or relationship).

### Step 4: Stakes That Are Huge to a Kid

Stakes don't have to be world-ending; they have to be **everything to this child.** A lost friendship, a move, a parent's illness, the science fair, fitting in, a betrayal — these are seismic at 11. Make the reader feel why it matters to *this* protagonist. External plot stakes (the mystery, the quest) should braid with the internal/emotional stakes.

### Step 5: Structure & Subplot

- A clear three- or four-act arc; an inciting incident that upends the ordinary world early.
- A **want** (external goal) and a **need** (the emotional lesson) that may conflict — the climax forces a choice.
- One braided subplot (often the friendship/family thread) that pays off alongside the main plot.
- Chapters with momentum and hooks; MG readers still reward "one more chapter."

### Step 6: Theme Without Preaching

- Theme is what the story *demonstrates*, not what a character announces.
- Let the protagonist earn the realization through events and choices; the reader draws the meaning.
- Cut any line where a character (or narrator) explains the lesson to the reader. Trust the kid.

### Step 7: Self-Check

- [ ] Does the narrator sound like a real, specific kid (not an adult)?
- [ ] Does the protagonist drive the plot and resolve the climax themselves?
- [ ] Are the stakes enormous *to this child*, with internal + external threads braided?
- [ ] Is the content age-appropriate (no YA romance/violence creep)?
- [ ] Does theme emerge from story rather than being stated?
- [ ] Is there hope on the horizon, even if the story is sad?

## Example Output

**Premise & Core:** When 11-year-old Dev's family bakery is about to be replaced by a chain, he enters a citywide baking contest to save it — but the recipe everyone loves is his late grandmother's, and he's terrified that making it without her means letting her go. *Core: grief and the courage to carry someone forward instead of holding them frozen.*

**Voice Sample**

> Everyone says Nani's almond cookies taste like a hug, which is a weird thing to say about food, but it's also exactly right, and I've stopped arguing.
> Here's the problem. I'm the only one left who knows the recipe, and the recipe isn't written down, and lately when I try to remember the part with the cardamom, there's just a gray fuzzy spot, like the cookie part of my brain got rained on.
> Dad says we'll figure it out. Dad also says the bakery will be "fine," in the voice he uses when it is extremely not going to be fine.

**Want vs. Need:** *Want* — win the contest and save the bakery. *Need* — let himself grieve Nani and trust that he carries her even when he changes the recipe. They conflict at the climax: the "perfect" replica fails; he has to risk making it *his* way.

**Agency Map:**
1. Dev *chooses* to enter the contest behind his dad's back (drives Act 1).
2. Dev *decides* to track down Nani's old friends for recipe fragments instead of asking his dad (rising action — and it strains the family thread).
3. At the midpoint, Dev *throws out* the written approximation a judge gave him — he wants the real thing (raises stakes).
4. Climax: with the replica failing on stage, Dev *changes* the recipe — adds his own touch — and owns it.

**Structure Outline:** Inciting — closure notice arrives. Midpoint — Dev finds a tin of Nani's notes but the key page is missing. All-is-lost — the test batch tastes wrong; Dev blows up at his dad. Climax — onstage, Dev bakes it *his* way. Resolution — they don't win first place, but a feature in the paper saves the shop; Dev writes the recipe down at last, in his own words.

**Stakes & Subplot:** The bakery is the last place that still smells like Nani — losing it is losing her again. Subplot: Dev and his dad have been avoiding each other's grief; the contest forces them into the kitchen together, paying off when Dad admits he misses her too.

**Theme Handling:** Theme (you carry people by changing, not freezing) emerges through the recipe arc. **Cut:** the drafted line where Dev thinks, "And that's when I learned that letting go isn't forgetting." — too on-the-nose; the final scene already shows it.

**Revision Priorities:**
1. Cut the stated-moral line; trust the recipe ending.
2. Strengthen Act 2 agency — currently a judge hands Dev a clue; make Dev earn it.
3. Keep Dad supportive-but-not-savior; he must not fix the bakery for Dev.
4. Verify the grief content stays hopeful and age-appropriate (see sensitive-topics prompt).

## Quality Indicators

**A strong middle-grade novel:**
- [ ] Has a narrator who sounds like a real kid, funny even when serious
- [ ] Puts the child in charge of the plot and the climax
- [ ] Makes small-world stakes feel enormous
- [ ] Braids an emotional/relationship subplot with the main plot
- [ ] Lets theme arise from story; no lectures
- [ ] Leaves hope, even in sad material

**Common pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Fix |
|---------|------|-----|
| Adult voice | Narrator analyzes feelings like a grown-up | Rewrite in concrete, sensory, present-kid terms |
| Passive hero | Things happen *to* the protagonist | Give the kid decisions that cause the plot |
| Preachiness | Stated morals, lessons announced | Cut the message; trust the events |
| YA creep | Romance, edginess, despair | Pull back to MG content and hope |
| Low stakes | Nothing feels urgent | Tie external plot to a deep personal need |

---

## False-Positive Prevention

**DON'T:**
- Sanitize real emotion — MG readers handle grief, divorce, fear, and unfairness; the rule is honesty *with hope*, not avoidance.
- Insert a moral the writer didn't intend.
- Assume MG must be "lighter" than it is — it can be deeply serious; it just can't be hopeless or adult-content.
- Confuse a young protagonist with a simple one; MG kids are complex.
- Let mentors/parents quietly solve the climax.

**DO:**
- Preserve the writer's voice and tonal intent while sharpening kid-authenticity.
- Confirm MG vs. YA boundaries (romance, violence, despair belong to YA).
- Push for child agency at every major turn.
- Route hard-topic handling to the sensitive-topics prompt for age-appropriate care.
- Distinguish theme (emergent) from message (stated) and cut the latter.
