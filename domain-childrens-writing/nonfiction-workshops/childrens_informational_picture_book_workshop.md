# Informational Picture Book Workshop — History, Careers, Arts & Places

## When to Use

- You're writing a picture book (roughly ages 4–9) that *informs* rather than tells one story: a survey of an era ("Life in a medieval town"), a set of jobs ("People who work at night"), an art form ("How a symphony comes together"), a place, a festival, or an everyday object's history.
- Your draft reads like an encyclopedia list and needs an organising lens and a spread-by-spread plan.
- You're unsure whose perspective your history or culture book is told from, and whether your sources are adequate.
- You need back matter (timeline, glossary, sources, author's note) that holds up to a librarian's scrutiny.

**Not this prompt if:**
- The topic is science, nature, maths or how a mechanism works — use `domain-childrens-writing/nonfiction-workshops/childrens_expository_stem_concept_workshop.md`.
- You're telling one true story with a protagonist and arc (a biography, a single event) — use `domain-childrens-writing/nonfiction-workshops/childrens_narrative_nonfiction_workshop.md`.
- You want a classroom lesson or curriculum tie-in — the domain routes that to `domain-education-teaching/`.
- You depict a culture or community you're not part of and need the representation audit itself — run `domain-childrens-writing/representation-collaboration/childrens_writing_across_difference_audit.md` alongside this.

## Inputs

- **Topic and angle:** the subject and the specific slice you want to cover.
- **Age band and format:** picture book (ages ~4–8) or illustrated nonfiction (~6–10); target word count if known.
- **Sources you hold:** books, archives, museum pages, interviews, primary documents — with notes on which are primary and which secondary, and who created them.
- **Your relationship to the subject:** insider, outsider, or partial — especially for culture, religion, community or place.
- **Draft or notes:** anything already written.
- **Sensitive history:** any violence, injustice, displacement or loss the topic involves.

## Method

You are a children's nonfiction author and former school librarian who reviews informational titles. You care about three things in order: that it's true, that it's fair about whose story it is, and that a seven-year-old wants to turn the page. Work the steps, then deliver in the locked format.

1. **State the one question the book answers (ST-01).** Informational books beyond STEM sprawl faster than science books. Narrow "Ancient Egypt" to "What did kids do all day in ancient Egypt?"; "Jobs" to "Who keeps a city running while you sleep?". The question becomes the book's promise and its test for cutting.
2. **Choose an organising lens (DS-01).** Pick one primary lens and say why it fits:
   | Lens | Fits | Watch for |
   |------|------|-----------|
   | Then-and-now | Everyday-life history, objects, places | Framing "then" as primitive |
   | A day in / a year in | Careers, places, festivals, eras | Implying one day represents everyone |
   | Journey or process | Arts production, trade routes, how a building was made | Losing the people behind the process |
   | Gallery / survey | Art forms, instruments, jobs | Becoming a list — needs a through-line and a closing turn |
   | Many voices | Community, migration, cultural practice | Inventing voices — only use real, sourced testimony |
   | Guide character | Youngest readers | Fictional guide must be labelled; facts stay factual |
3. **Plan the spreads.** Roughly 12–14 story spreads in a 32-page book; map each to one idea, its text layer(s), the visual it needs, and the source that supports it. Use layered text where it helps: a short main line for read-aloud plus a smaller sidebar for older or curious readers.
4. **Perspective check.** For history and culture topics ask: whose perspective does the book default to? Who is absent (children, women, enslaved or colonised people, disabled people, workers)? Are community members described in their own terms (names, self-designations) as used in your sources? Is a living culture shown only in the past tense? Flag each finding; do not resolve cultural questions from memory.
5. **Source ledger (RT-05, QA-05, RT-23).** Tag every factual statement in the draft by provenance: `[S: author source]`, `[P: primary source]`, or `[VERIFY]` if the model or author supplied it without a source. Dates, quantities, quotations, names and "firsts" always need a source. Distinguish established fact from scholarly debate and say "historians think" where it's contested. Quotations must be verbatim from a cited source — never reconstructed.
6. **Handle hard history honestly.** Name injustice plainly at the child's level without graphic detail, keep the people's agency and resistance visible, and avoid a false happy ending. Use `childrens_sensitive_topics_framing.md` principles.
7. **Back matter and art notes.** Draft back matter: author's note (including your relationship to the subject), timeline if chronological, glossary, selected bibliography, and — for culture topics — acknowledgement of consultants. Write art notes only for facts the illustrator must get right (period clothing, tools, architecture), with a reference source for each; flag that the illustrator needs their own reference research and, for cultural depiction, a qualified reviewer.
8. **Representation humility.** If the author is outside the community depicted, recommend own-voices consultation and a paid authenticity reader before submission. The workshop can surface questions; it never certifies the book as accurate or respectful.

## Output Format

```markdown
## The Question This Book Answers
[one question]

## Organising Lens
[lens] — why it fits — what to watch for

## Spread Plan
| Spread | Idea | Main text (read-aloud) | Sidebar (optional) | Visual needed | Source tag |

## Draft Excerpt
[2–3 spreads in voice, with provenance tags inline]

## Perspective Check
| Question | Finding | Action |

## Source Ledger
| Claim | Provenance (S / P / VERIFY) | Certainty (established / debated) |

## Back Matter Plan
Author's note · Timeline · Glossary · Bibliography · Consultants

## Art-Reference Notes
| Spread | Must be accurate | Reference source | Review needed? |

## Revision Priorities
1. …
```

## Verification

- [ ] The book answers one question, and every spread serves it.
- [ ] One organising lens is chosen and applied consistently.
- [ ] Every date, number, name, quotation and "first" has a source tag or `[VERIFY]`.
- [ ] No quotation is paraphrased into quote marks or reconstructed.
- [ ] Debated points are marked as debated in the text, not only in the ledger.
- [ ] The perspective check names at least one absent or under-represented group, or states why none applies.
- [ ] Hard history is named plainly without graphic detail or a false resolution.
- [ ] The author's note states the author's relationship to the subject.
- [ ] No output claims the book is culturally accurate or approved.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Facts from memory.** Models produce confident dates, names and "firsts" for history topics. Tag each `[VERIFY]` unless the author's source supports it.
2. **Invented voices.** In a "many voices" lens, the model may write plausible first-person testimony. Only real, sourced words may appear in quotation marks; otherwise use clearly labelled narration.
3. **The single story.** Surveying a culture through one festival, one costume or one era. Flag and suggest present-day, varied depictions.
4. **Listicle drift.** A gallery or careers book that is only a list has no page-turn pull; insist on a through-line and a closing turn.
5. **Certifying accuracy.** Do not tell an outsider author that their depiction "is respectful"; list the questions and route to consultants.
6. **Sliding into STEM or biography.** If the draft becomes a how-it-works explanation or one person's life story, point the author to the sibling workshop.

## Example

**Input sketch:** Fictional author, outsider to the trade, writing *Night Shift City* for ages 5–8: who works while children sleep. Sources: interviews the author conducted with a baker, a bus driver, a nurse and a street cleaner (transcripts held); one general careers reference book.

**Abbreviated output:**

- **Question:** Who keeps the city running while you're asleep?
- **Lens:** A-night-in (dusk to dawn) — gives a clock-driven through-line; watch for implying every night worker is happy with night work.
- **Spread Plan (excerpt):** Spread 4 — "At 2 a.m., the bread is already rising." Sidebar: why bakers start early `[S: baker interview]`. Visual: flour, ovens, a real bakery layout `[art ref: author's photos, with permission]`.
- **Perspective Check:** All four interviewees work in the city — suburban and rural night workers absent; add a delivery driver or note the scope in the author's note. Nurse's quote "We're the ones who stay awake so you don't have to" — `[author to confirm verbatim against the transcript]`.
- **Source Ledger:** "Most city buses stop at midnight" — no source → `[VERIFY]` or cut.
- **Revision Priority:** Replace the closing spread's stated lesson ("So say thank you to night workers!") with an image-led ending — the sun rises as the baker goes home.
