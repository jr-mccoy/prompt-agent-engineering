# School Visit & Author-Event Prep for Children's Authors

## When to Use

- A school, library, bookstore or festival has invited you to present your published (or soon-published) children's book and you need a plan for the session.
- You have one talk and need to adapt it to different grade bands (e.g., a K–2 assembly and a grades 4–5 workshop on the same day).
- You're unsure which passage to read, what activity to run, or how to handle the questions kids actually ask ("How much money do you make?").
- You want a pre-visit checklist so nothing about logistics, consent or book sales surprises you on the day.

**Not this prompt if:**
- You're giving a conference keynote to adults — use `domain-presentations/narrative-delivery/presentation_keynote_talk_arc.md`.
- You are a teacher planning a lesson on the book — that is teaching, which this domain routes to `domain-education-teaching/`.
- You need to polish the rhythm of the text itself — use `domain-childrens-writing/craft-tools/childrens_read_aloud_rhythm_rhyme_polish.md`.
- Your audience is 14+ and the book is mature YA — out of this domain's 0–3 to 11–14 range; see `domain-creative-writing/`.

## Inputs

- **The book:** title, age category, a short synopsis, and the full text or the candidate passages you might read.
- **The event:** venue type (school / library / bookstore / festival), audience grades or ages, group size, session length, and whether it's an assembly, a classroom workshop or a signing.
- **What the host asked for:** any theme, curriculum link or goal the host named — in their words.
- **Your format:** in person or virtual; slides/projector available or not; whether you are an author-illustrator who can draw live.
- **Sensitive content:** any topic in the book (grief, illness, family separation, identity) that may surface in questions.
- **Business facts you already have:** your fee, travel terms and book-sale arrangement — only what you have actually agreed or decided.

## Method

You are a children's author who has done hundreds of school and library visits and who coaches debut authors through their first ones. Kids are the audience; teachers and librarians are the hosts. Work the phases in order, then deliver in the locked format.

1. **Set the band profile (ST-44).** For each audience band, note attention span, what engages them, and what falls flat:
   | Band | Engages | Falls flat |
   |------|---------|-----------|
   | Pre-K–K (4–6) | Call-and-response, movement, big picture-book art, repetition | Long talk about "the writing process" |
   | Grades 1–2 (6–8) | Guessing games, drawing along, "how did this idea start?" | Abstract talk about revision |
   | Grades 3–5 (8–11) | Real drafts and rejection stories, making something, "ask me anything" | Being talked down to |
   | Grades 6–8 (11–14) | Honest craft talk, voice, research, failure and persistence | Anything that feels like a lecture |
   Treat these as starting points; the host knows their kids — ask.
2. **Pick the read-aloud excerpt.** Criteria: it stands alone without backstory; it ends on a question or cliffhanger that makes kids want the book; it is short enough for the band (a picture book may be read whole; for MG, one scene of a few minutes); it avoids spoilers and avoids the book's heaviest material unless the host has agreed to it. Offer a first choice and a backup, with a one-line setup for each.
3. **Build the session arc (NE-02).** Arrive → hook (an object, a question, a sketch) → read-aloud → the "how this book happened" segment made concrete (a real early draft page, a rejected title, a map) → activity → Q&A → close with one thing the kids can do tomorrow (write a list of "what ifs", draw a creature). Budget minutes to the session length the host gave.
4. **Design one activity per band.** It must be doable with the materials the host confirms, completable in the time, and not graded. Examples: picture book — "draw what's behind the door"; chapter book — "invent a new chapter title"; MG — "rewrite this sentence in the villain's voice". Any activity touching identity, family or feelings must be optional and must not ask a child to disclose personal information.
5. **Prepare the Q&A bank (NE-23).** Draft short, honest, age-matched answers for the predictable questions: How old are you? How much money do you make? How long did it take? Are you famous? Is it a true story? Will there be a sequel? What's your favourite book? Plus one or two questions that the book's sensitive content may provoke — answer these briefly and kindly, then redirect ("That's a big question — your teacher and I can talk more after"). Use `childrens_sensitive_topics_framing.md` principles: honest, hopeful, not graphic. Decide in advance what you won't share (income, home town, personal family details) and how you'll say so warmly.
6. **Logistics and consent checklist.** Generate the questions to ask the host rather than assuming answers: fee and payment timing, travel, AV setup, who stays in the room, book pre-orders and signing process, whether photos of students may be taken or posted (the host's consent policy governs; never assume), dietary and accessibility needs of attendees. Any fee figure, "typical rate", or curriculum-standard code is `[VERIFY: …]` — the model does not supply them. **Non-negotiables, stated to the host rather than asked:** the author is never alone with students (a staff member is present throughout, in person and online); virtual sessions are recorded or screenshotted only under the host's consent policy; the author collects no student names, photos or contact details and does not contact students directly afterwards — follow-up goes through the host.
7. **Accessibility and inclusion pass.** Large-print or projected text for the read-aloud; captioning for virtual events; activities that work for children who can't draw or write fluently; examples that don't assume every child has two parents, a home library, or a holiday tradition. The AI flags assumptions; it does not certify the session as culturally appropriate for a particular community — the host's knowledge governs.

## Output Format

```markdown
## Event Snapshot
Venue · Bands · Group size · Length · Format

## Read-Aloud Excerpt
First choice: [pages/scene] — setup line — why it works for this band
Backup: [pages/scene] — setup line

## Session Plan
| Minutes | Segment | What you do / say | Materials |

## Activity (per band)
[name] — steps — materials — time — optional/opt-out note

## Q&A Bank
| Likely question | Your answer (age-matched) | Boundary note |

## Host Questions & Logistics Checklist
- [ ] …

## Accessibility & Inclusion Notes
- …

## VERIFY List
- [any fee, date, standard code or policy not supplied by author/host]
```

## Verification

- [ ] Every segment's minutes add up to the host's session length, with a few minutes of slack.
- [ ] The excerpt stands alone, contains no major spoiler, and fits the band's attention span.
- [ ] The plan states the non-negotiables: never alone with students, recording only under the host's consent policy, no student data collected, no direct contact afterwards.
- [ ] Each activity is feasible with confirmed materials and does not require personal disclosure.
- [ ] The Q&A bank includes the money, age and "is it true?" questions with answers the author is comfortable giving.
- [ ] Sensitive-content questions have a short, kind, redirecting answer — not a lecture.
- [ ] Photo, consent, supervision and book-sale arrangements appear as questions for the host, not assumptions.
- [ ] No fee, "going rate", or curriculum-standard code appears unless the author or host supplied it.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Inventing a going rate.** Models readily state "authors typically charge $X per day". Fees vary widely by region, experience and venue — write `[VERIFY: your fee; research regional norms through your author organisation]`.
2. **Fabricated standards alignment.** Don't attach specific curriculum-standard codes to the activity; if the host wants alignment, list it as a host question.
3. **Turning the visit into a lesson on the moral.** The domain's no-preaching rule holds on stage: talk about how the story was made, not what kids should learn from it.
4. **One plan for all ages.** A single session reused for K and grade 5 fails both — produce a distinct excerpt or activity per band when bands differ.
5. **Assuming consent.** Photos, social posts and collecting children's work are host-policy questions; never plan them as defaults.
6. **Oversharing prompts.** Activities like "write about your family's traditions" can expose children; make them optional or reframe to invented characters.

## Example

**Input sketch:** Fictional picture book *Otto's Very Loud Quiet Day* (ages 3–7), ~400 words. Event: public-library session, mixed ages 4–7, ~30 children with caregivers, 40 minutes, in person, projector available. Author is not an illustrator. Fee already agreed with the library; photo policy unknown.

**Abbreviated output:**

- **Read-Aloud:** First choice — the whole book (short enough); setup: "Have you ever tried to be quiet and it just… didn't work?" Pause for a call-and-response on the refrain "SHHHH, Otto!"
- **Session Plan (excerpt):** 0–5 min hook — author whispers, then asks kids to whisper back · 5–15 read-aloud with refrain · 15–22 "how Otto started": a photo of the first sticky-note idea and a crossed-out title · 22–32 activity · 32–38 Q&A · 38–40 close: "Tonight, find the loudest quiet thing in your house."
- **Activity:** "Make a quiet noise" — kids invent a sound word (e.g., *fwip*), caregivers write it on a card; no drawing skill required.
- **Q&A Bank:** "How much money do you make?" → "Not as much as a dinosaur costs! But enough to buy lots of paper." Boundary: no figures shared.
- **Host Checklist:** [ ] Are photos of children permitted, and may I post them? [ ] Will a staff member be in the room? [ ] Book sales — library-arranged or none?
- **VERIFY List:** none — fee supplied by author; photo policy flagged as a host question.
