# Investor Pitch Narrative

**Objective:** Produce the narrative spine of a fundraising deck before any slide is designed: a one-sentence investment thesis, a slide order chosen by where the company's evidence is strongest, a headline claim plus supporting proof for every slide, the objections a sceptical investor will raise and where each is pre-empted, and an ask expressed as money-for-milestones. The output is what a founder rehearses from and what a designer builds from. It is not a generic template: the order changes with the company, because a pre-revenue team with a rare founder-market fit should not open the same way as a company with 20% month-on-month growth.

---

## When to Use

- You are raising a pre-seed, seed, or Series A round and have a deck that lists facts but does not argue.
- Investors say "interesting" and do not follow up; you suspect the story, not the business.
- You need both a send-ahead version (read without you) and a presented version.
- You are advising a founder and want a structured critique of their pitch logic.

**Distinct from:**
- `domain-agentic-resources/skills/marketing/sales-enablement/SKILL.md` — sales decks persuade a buyer to purchase. An investor is buying a share of the future company; the questions (market size, return potential, team) differ.
- `domain-professional-writing/domain-specific/domain_writing_founder_investor_update.md` — monthly or quarterly updates to investors you already have.
- `domain-presentations/powerpoint_financial_storyteller.md` — builds financial slides as PowerPoint; use it for the financials slide once this narrative is set.
- `domain-presentations/board-decks/boarddeck_tam_sam_som.md` — the market-sizing visual itself.

**When NOT to use:**
- Later-stage rounds with data rooms and banker-led processes; the deck is a small part of those.
- You need securities, valuation, or term-sheet advice — see `domain-legal/` and `domain-finance/` and qualified counsel.

## Inputs / Context

1. **Company basics.** What you do, for whom, stage, round size sought.
2. **Evidence.** Traction metrics with dates, customer quotes, pilots, retention, revenue — with sources.
3. **Team.** Why these people, specifically, for this problem.
4. **Market.** Your sizing and how it was built (bottom-up preferred).
5. **Competition and alternatives.** Including "do nothing" and spreadsheets.
6. **Use of funds and milestones.** What the money buys and by when.
7. **Target investors.** Stage, thesis, and anything known about the partners.

---

## Method

### Step 1 — Write the thesis in one sentence
"[Company] will become [outcome] because [unfair insight or advantage], and [evidence] shows it is starting." If the *because* is generic ("great team", "huge market"), stop and find the specific claim.

### Step 2 — Rank the evidence
Rate each asset — traction, team, insight, market timing, product, technology — as strong, adequate, or weak, with the proof. The strongest asset appears in the first three slides.

### Step 3 — Choose the order
Start from a standard set — problem, solution, why now, market, product, traction, business model, competition, team, financials, ask — and reorder: traction early if strong; team early if pre-traction with rare fit; "why now" early if the timing shift is the insight. Cut any slide that carries no claim.

### Step 4 — Write every slide as claim plus proof
Each slide gets a full-sentence headline stating the claim ("Customers who try it keep using it: 92% month-3 retention"), then the evidence and its source. Topic labels ("Traction") are not headlines.

### Step 5 — Label numbers
Mark every figure as **actual** (with date and source), **estimate** (with method), or **projection** (with assumptions). Projections are never presented as commitments or returns.

### Step 6 — Anticipate and place objections
List the 6–10 questions a sceptical partner will raise (market too small, why won't an incumbent copy this, why this team, churn, unit economics, dependence on one channel). For each, say whether the deck pre-empts it (which slide) or holds it for Q&A, and the evidence-based answer.

### Step 7 — Frame the ask
State amount, what it funds, the 2–4 milestones it reaches, and the runway in months. Tie milestones to what makes the next round possible.

### Step 8 — Produce two versions
**Presented** (sparse slides, the narrative carried by the speaker) and **send-ahead** (self-explanatory headlines and short supporting text). Note differences slide by slide.

## Output Format

```
# Pitch narrative — [company], [round]

Thesis: [one sentence]

## Evidence ranking
| Asset | Strength | Proof |

## Slide spine
| # | Headline claim (full sentence) | Proof + source | Number labels | Send-ahead adds |

## Objections
| Objection | Pre-empted on slide / held for Q&A | Answer + evidence |

## The ask
Amount · Use of funds · Milestones · Runway

## Gaps to close before sending
- ...
```

---

## Verification

- [ ] Thesis names a specific because, not a generic virtue.
- [ ] Strongest evidence appears in the first three slides.
- [ ] Every slide headline is a full-sentence claim with proof and source.
- [ ] Every number labelled actual / estimate / projection.
- [ ] 6–10 objections listed with placement and answer.
- [ ] Ask ties funds to milestones and runway.
- [ ] No invented metric, customer, or investor name.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Template order as strategy.** A standard slide order is a checklist, not a story. If the order was not changed for this company, Step 3 was skipped.
2. **Invented or rounded-up traction.** Do not fill gaps with plausible numbers. Missing evidence becomes a "gap to close", not a slide.
3. **Top-down market sizing alone.** "1% of a $50B market" persuades nobody. Show how the number is built from customers and prices.
4. **"No competitors."** Every problem has an alternative, even if it is a spreadsheet or doing nothing. Claiming none signals naivety.
5. **Projections as promises.** Forecasts are assumptions; do not present them as expected returns or guarantees.
6. **Topic-label headlines.** "Market" tells the reader nothing. A headline states what the slide proves.
7. **An ask with no milestones.** "Raising $2M" without what it achieves invites the question "for what?".

## Example

**Company:** a scheduling tool for independent physiotherapy clinics. **Raising:** $1.5M seed. **Evidence:** 140 paying clinics, $31k MRR (actual, June 2026, billing system), 3% monthly logo churn, founders are a physiotherapist who ran a clinic for eight years and an engineer from a booking platform.

- **Thesis:** "ClinicFlow will become the operating system for independent physio clinics because it is built around no-show recovery, which is where these clinics lose the most money, and 140 clinics paying within 14 months shows it is starting."
- **Evidence ranking:** traction — strong; founder-market fit — strong; market — adequate (bottom-up: ~9,000 independent clinics in target countries × $220/month ≈ $24M ARR serviceable); technology — weak (not a moat).
- **Spine (first four):** 1 "Independent clinics lose about one appointment in eight to no-shows" (clinic survey, n=62, estimate) · 2 "140 clinics pay us after 14 months, with 3% monthly churn" (actual) · 3 "We recover no-shows by filling gaps from a waitlist automatically" · 4 "Our founder ran one of these clinics for eight years".
- **Objection:** "Why won't a big practice-management vendor add this?" — held for Q&A; answer: incumbents sell to multi-site groups; independents are unprofitable for their sales model (evidence: pricing pages start at 5 seats).
- **Ask:** $1.5M → 18 months runway → 600 clinics and $130k MRR (projection; assumes current churn and two sales hires), the threshold for a Series A.
- **Gap:** no cohort retention chart yet; build from billing data before sending.
