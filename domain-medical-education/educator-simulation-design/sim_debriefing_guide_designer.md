# Simulation & Clinical Experience Debriefing Guide Designer

**Objective:** Produce a structured, facilitator-ready debriefing guide for a simulation scenario or clinical experience, calibrated to the correct framework, total time available, and learning objectives, with facilitation scripts and psychological safety guidance.

## When to Use
- ✅ After a high-fidelity simulation scenario (sepsis, code, trauma, obstetric emergency, airway management)
- ✅ After an interprofessional or team-based simulation exercise requiring TeamGAINS or PEARLS
- ✅ After a standardized patient (SP) encounter requiring structured learner reflection
- ✅ After a clinical event (resuscitation, critical incident, difficult family meeting) that warrants real-time reflection as a team
- ✅ When a simulation educator wants to build a reusable debrief guide for a recurring scenario
- ❌ Do not use for debriefing a single learner in isolation without modification — the guides below are primarily designed for groups; solo learner debrief requires different pacing and prompting
- ❌ Do not use as a performance review instrument — debriefing is a learner-generated reflection process, not a grading event
- ❌ Do not use when the team or learner is in acute distress following a critical incident; psychological first aid precedes debriefing

## Inputs Required
- **Scenario or experience just completed:** (e.g., "Septic shock management in a 62-year-old presenting to the ED," "Difficult airway in the OR," "Goals-of-care family meeting SP encounter")
- **Learning objectives addressed by the scenario:** (list 2-4 specific objectives — these drive Phase 2 questions)
- **Participant composition:** (e.g., "4 IM residents PGY1-2," "interprofessional team: 2 nurses, 1 physician, 1 pharmacist," "6 M3 students")
- **Total time available for debriefing:** (15 / 30 / 45 / 60 minutes)
- **Debriefing framework preference:** (see Step 3 for selector; leave blank to use the selector logic)
- **Video-assisted debriefing:** (yes/no — if yes, specify which events were recorded for replay)
- **Emotional charge of the scenario:** (low / moderate / high — e.g., pediatric death scenarios are high; routine IV placement skills lab is low)

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Begin every debrief with a reactions phase before any analysis or teaching — unprocessed emotion blocks cognitive learning
- Use advocacy-inquiry phrasing in the analysis phase whenever surfacing a mental model or decision point
- Allocate time explicitly across phases — the guide must include recommended minutes per phase
- Include at least one synthesis question that consolidates learning to a transferable principle

**Must Not:**
- Skip or compress the reactions phase to save time — this is a predictable cause of ineffective debriefs
- Provide answers during the analysis phase before learners have had the opportunity to surface their own reasoning
- Use judgmental, shaming, or rhetorical question language (see False-Positive Prevention for specific phrases to avoid)
- Use the debriefing guide verbatim — it is a scaffold, not a script; facilitators must adapt to what actually happened

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Capture Scenario and Debrief Context
Before designing the guide, establish the full context:

1a. **Scenario summary (1-3 sentences):** Describe what occurred in the scenario — the clinical situation, the key decision points, and how it ended. The debrief guide will be organized around these decision points.

1b. **Learning objectives (2-4):** Restate the specific learning objectives the scenario was designed to address. Phase 2 questions will map one-to-one to these objectives. If you have more than 4 objectives, select the 3-4 that generated the most observable behavior during the scenario.

1c. **Emotional temperature assessment:** Rate the emotional charge (low / moderate / high). High-charge scenarios (pediatric death, unexpected arrest, mass casualty) require a longer reactions phase, explicit normalization language, and in some cases, a structured defusing step before the debrief proper.
   - Low (routine skills, clean run): 3 minutes for reactions
   - Moderate (complex case, team communication breakdown): 5 minutes for reactions
   - High (distressing outcome, patient death, ethical conflict): 8-10 minutes for reactions; consider using the 3D Model

1d. **Team composition:** Note the professional mix. Interprofessional teams require prompts that invite all disciplines to speak — not just the physician lead. Plan specific questions for nursing, pharmacy, or allied health perspectives if present.

### Step 2 — Select Debriefing Framework
Choose the framework that best fits your context:

| Framework | Best For | Total Time Fit | Key Distinguishing Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PEARLS** (Promote Excellence And Reflective Learning in Simulation) | Adaptable; comprehensive; any simulation; solo or team | 30-60 min | Combines multiple debriefing strategies in one adaptable structure |
| **GAS** (Gather, Analyze, Summarize) | Shorter debriefs; single learning focus; low-moderate complexity | 15-30 min | Simple three-phase structure, easy for novice facilitators |
| **Advocacy-Inquiry** | Surfacing mental models without confrontation; any framework can use this method | Embedded in other frameworks | Facilitator states an observation then asks an open question about the learner's thinking |
| **3D Model** (Defusing, Discovering, Deepening) | High emotional charge scenarios; critical incidents; scenarios with distressing outcomes | 30-60 min | Dedicated defusing phase before any analysis |
| **TeamGAINS** | Interprofessional team scenarios requiring structured multi-discipline input | 30-60 min | Structured rounds that solicit input from each professional role |

**Selection logic:**
- Total time < 20 minutes → **GAS**
- Total time 30+ minutes, single profession → **PEARLS**
- Interprofessional team → **TeamGAINS**
- High emotional charge → **3D Model**
- Any framework benefit from embedding → **Advocacy-Inquiry** technique throughout Phase 2

### Step 3 — Design Phase 1: Reactions (3-10 Minutes)
The reactions phase exists to decompress emotional responses before cognitive processing begins. It is not optional.

3a. **Opening statement (facilitator reads or paraphrases):**
"Before we get into what happened clinically, I want to take a moment for everyone to decompress. This is a space where it's safe to share what you're thinking and feeling. There are no wrong answers here."

3b. **Reactions-phase question set (choose 2-3 based on emotional temperature):**
   - "How is everyone feeling right now? Just one word or a short phrase — go around the room."
   - "What was going through your mind in those first moments?"
   - "What part of that scenario is still with you right now?"
   - "Is there anything that happened in there that you want to name before we move on?"

3c. **For high-charge scenarios (3D Model, Defusing phase):** Add a structured defusing step before moving to any analysis:
   - "Before we look at the clinical content, I want to acknowledge that this scenario was designed to be difficult. The feelings you're having — frustration, uncertainty, sadness — are expected and appropriate responses. Let's take two full minutes to just sit with that before we shift gears."
   - Normalize without dismissing: "Feeling distressed after a pediatric death scenario — even a simulated one — is not a sign of weakness. It's a sign you're taking the work seriously."

3d. **Transition statement (signals end of reactions phase):**
"Thank you for sharing that. Now let's shift into looking at what happened in the scenario and what we can learn from it."

### Step 4 — Design Phase 2: Analysis / Discussion (10-20 Minutes)
This is the core learning phase. Write one discussion question per learning objective. Each question should be framed using advocacy-inquiry when the objective involves a clinical decision point, mental model, or team behavior.

**Advocacy-Inquiry structure:**
1. State a specific observable behavior or event (advocacy): "I noticed that the first dose of antibiotics was ordered about 12 minutes into the scenario..."
2. Pair with an open, non-judgmental question (inquiry): "...help me understand what was driving the timing of that decision."

**What advocacy-inquiry is NOT:**
- "Why did you wait so long to give antibiotics?" (rhetorical, implies error, closes reflection)
- "You should have given antibiotics sooner — what happened?" (judgment before understanding)

**Advocacy-inquiry templates:**

Template 1 (decision point): "I noticed [specific observable event]. I'm curious what you were thinking at that moment. Help me understand the reasoning."

Template 2 (team communication): "I observed [specific communication behavior]. What was the team's shared understanding of the plan at that point?"

Template 3 (missed element): "There was a moment when [event occurred]. What information was available to you at that point, and how were you interpreting it?"

4a. Write one advocacy-inquiry pair per learning objective. Map each question to the objective it addresses.
4b. Sequence questions in chronological order of the scenario — this helps learners reconstruct their decision-making as a narrative.
4c. Prepare one follow-up probe for each primary question in case the group gives a short or surface-level answer:
   - "Tell me more about that."
   - "What were you seeing / hearing that led you to that decision?"
   - "Was there a moment when the picture started to change?"

### Step 5 — Design Phase 3: Summary / Takeaways (3-5 Minutes)
The summary phase consolidates learning into transferable principles. Learners generate the takeaways; the facilitator synthesizes.

5a. **Synthesis question (choose one):**
   - "If you had to distill today's experience into one or two things you'll carry into your next clinical shift, what would they be?"
   - "What's the most important thing we learned today that you want to remember three months from now?"
   - "If a colleague who wasn't here today asked you what you learned from this scenario, what would you tell them?"

5b. **Facilitator synthesis (1-2 minutes):** After learners respond, the facilitator names 1-3 key learning points that emerged from the discussion. Connect back to the original learning objectives. Do not introduce new content not generated by the group.

5c. **Closing statement:**
"Thank you for your honesty and engagement today. The work we did in this room — reflecting on our own thinking under pressure — is exactly the kind of learning that makes us better clinicians."

5d. **Resources/follow-up:** If any content gaps emerged in Phase 2 that could not be addressed in the debrief, name the gap and provide a resource or follow-up activity: "We identified that sepsis bundle timing is an area to revisit — I'll send the Surviving Sepsis Campaign summary by end of week."

### Step 6 — Apply Timing Allocation
Use this timing guide to allocate phase lengths based on total debrief time available:

| Total Time | Reactions (Phase 1) | Analysis (Phase 2) | Summary (Phase 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 minutes | 3 min | 9 min | 3 min |
| 30 minutes | 5 min | 20 min | 5 min |
| 45 minutes | 7 min | 31 min | 7 min |
| 60 minutes | 10 min | 42 min | 8 min |

Note: High-charge scenarios add 3-5 minutes to Phase 1 and subtract from Phase 2. Do not compress Phase 3 below 3 minutes — the synthesis is what makes the learning stick.

### Step 7 — Build the Psychological Safety Scaffold
Psychological safety is not assumed — it must be actively constructed. Include these elements in the debrief guide:

7a. **Opening frame (always read aloud):**
"What we discuss here stays here. The purpose of this debrief is learning, not evaluation. Nothing said in this room is reported to your program director or placed in your file unless a patient safety concern is identified."

7b. **Phrases to AVOID (judgmental, rhetorical, shaming):**
- "Why didn't you..." (implies a correct answer was obvious; shuts down disclosure)
- "You should have..." (judgment before understanding)
- "That was the wrong decision" (premature evaluation)
- "Didn't you know that..." (rhetorical; implies incompetence)
- "Obviously the answer is..." (invalidates learner reasoning)
- "I would have done it differently" (unhelpful unless unpacked as an advocacy-inquiry pair)

7c. **Phrases to USE (open, curious, non-evaluative):**
- "Help me understand what you were thinking when..."
- "What information were you working with at that point?"
- "What made [action] feel like the right next step?"
- "What would have needed to be different for you to..."
- "That's a really important observation — can you say more about that?"

7d. **Managing dominant voices:** If one learner dominates the discussion, use structured turn-taking: "Let's hear from someone we haven't heard from yet — [name/role], what's your perspective?"

### Step 8 — Adapt for Video-Assisted Debriefing (If Applicable)
If video capture was used during the scenario, adapt the guide as follows:

8a. **Video cue points:** During the scenario, identify 2-3 specific moments (timestamps) you plan to replay in Phase 2. These should correspond directly to the advocacy-inquiry question pairs in Step 4.

8b. **Pre-replay framing:** Before playing each clip, state the observation you want learners to notice: "I'm going to play 45 seconds from about the 8-minute mark. Watch for the team communication around the antibiotic order."

8c. **Post-replay inquiry:** After the clip, use an open question rather than leading: "What did you notice?" or "What was happening from your perspective at that moment?"

8d. **Video replay limit:** Limit to 2-3 clips maximum per debrief. Video review extends the time required — add 5-7 minutes per clip to your total budget.

8e. **Consent reminder:** Confirm all participants consented to video capture before the session. Do not replay footage if any participant withdraws consent.

## Worked Example

**Scenario completed:** Septic shock management in a 62-year-old presenting to the ED with confusion, fever, and hypotension.
**Learning objectives:** (1) Identify sepsis using qSOFA or SIRS criteria within 5 minutes of arrival; (2) Initiate the 1-hour sepsis bundle (blood cultures, IV fluids, antibiotics) in correct sequence; (3) Communicate the clinical picture to the incoming team using SBAR format.
**Team:** 4 EM residents (PGY1-3), 2 nurses
**Total time:** 30 minutes
**Framework:** PEARLS with embedded advocacy-inquiry
**Emotional charge:** Moderate (scenario ends with clinical improvement but bundle delayed by 14 minutes)

---

**DEBRIEF GUIDE — 30-Minute Septic Shock Debrief (PEARLS)**

**Phase 1 — Reactions (5 minutes)**

*Opening statement (read aloud):*
"Before we look at what happened clinically, I want everyone to take a breath. What we discuss here stays here — this is a learning space, not an evaluation. Let's start with a quick check-in."

*Questions:*
1. "How is everyone feeling right now? Just a word or short phrase — go around the room."
2. "What part of that scenario is still with you?"

*Transition:* "Thank you. Let's shift into looking at what happened and what we can take from it."

**Phase 2 — Analysis (20 minutes)**

*Objective 1 — Sepsis recognition (7 minutes):*
Advocacy-Inquiry: "I noticed that the team documented a heart rate of 118, a respiratory rate of 24, and an altered mental status within the first three minutes of the scenario. Help me understand what the team's shared assessment was at that point — had sepsis entered the differential?"
Follow-up probe: "What would have needed to be different for you to call it sepsis at minute three rather than minute eight?"

*Objective 2 — Bundle sequencing (7 minutes):*
Advocacy-Inquiry: "Blood cultures were drawn at minute 9, antibiotics were ordered at minute 12, and fluids were started at minute 14. Walk me through the team's decision-making about sequencing — what was driving the order of those steps?"
Follow-up probe: "At what point did the team have a shared mental model of the sepsis bundle?"

*Objective 3 — SBAR handoff (6 minutes):*
Advocacy-Inquiry: "I observed the handoff to the incoming team at the end of the scenario. I noticed the situation and background were communicated clearly — help me understand what happened with the assessment and recommendation portion."
Follow-up probe: "What information would the incoming team have needed to take immediate action on the antibiotic timing?"

**Phase 3 — Summary (5 minutes)**

*Synthesis question:* "If you were talking to a colleague who wasn't here today and they asked what you learned from this scenario, what would you tell them? What's the one thing you'll carry to your next shift?"

*Facilitator synthesis (after group responds):*
"What I heard the team identify is: early pattern recognition for sepsis, parallel processing of bundle steps rather than sequential, and a complete SBAR that explicitly includes the recommendation. Those three things are exactly what the evidence shows separates the teams that hit the 1-hour bundle most consistently. Well done surfacing that yourselves."

*Resources:* "I'll send the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 bundle update by Friday. It has a one-page visual that maps directly to what we just discussed."

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Common Mistake | ✅ Correct Approach |
|---|---|
| Skipping the reactions phase to save time for content | Unprocessed emotion blocks cognitive learning; 3-5 minutes on reactions saves 10+ minutes of unfocused analysis and reduces the risk of learners disengaging |
| Telling learners what they did wrong in Phase 2 | Advocacy-inquiry surfaces mental models before judgment; "I noticed X — help me understand your thinking" is more effective than "you should have done Y" |
| Using the script verbatim regardless of what actually happened | The guide is a scaffold, not a teleprompter; if the team immediately volunteers the key learning point, skip to synthesis — don't re-ask questions the group has already answered |
| Treating the debrief as a performance review | Debrief is learner-generated reflection; facilitators ask questions and synthesize — they do not grade, rank, or prescribe answers during the session |
| Designing the same debrief guide for a skills lab and a high-stakes interprofessional emergency | A 15-minute minor skills lab debrief uses GAS with a short reactions check-in; a 45-minute pediatric arrest debrief uses 3D Model with extended defusing — the emotional charge, team composition, and objectives change everything |
| Allowing one voice to dominate Phase 2 | Use structured turn-taking and explicit role-based invitations: "We've heard from the physicians — what's the nursing team's perspective on the communication at minute 9?" |

## Output Format

The completed debriefing guide should be structured as a facilitator-ready document with the following sections:

1. **Header:** Scenario name, framework selected, total time, team composition, learning objectives
2. **Psychological Safety Frame** (read aloud at opening): Verbatim facilitator statement
3. **Phase 1 — Reactions:** Timing, opening statement, 2-3 reaction questions, transition statement
4. **Phase 2 — Analysis:** Timing, one advocacy-inquiry pair per learning objective with follow-up probes, facilitator notes on what to listen for
5. **Phase 3 — Summary:** Timing, synthesis question, facilitator synthesis script, follow-up resources
6. **Timing Grid:** Visual allocation of minutes per phase
7. **Psychological Safety Language Guide:** Phrases to avoid and preferred alternatives
8. **Video Cue Points** (if video-assisted): Timestamps, pre-replay frames, post-replay questions
9. **Facilitator Notes:** What worked in prior runs of this scenario (if applicable), likely points of learner difficulty, anticipated resistance

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Reactions phase is present and time-allocated — not skipped or folded into analysis
- [ ] One advocacy-inquiry question pair is written per learning objective in Phase 2
- [ ] Advocacy-inquiry pairs follow the correct structure (observation + open question) — no rhetorical or leading questions present
- [ ] Timing allocation is explicit and totals the full available debrief time
- [ ] Synthesis question is present and opens with a transfer-to-practice frame
- [ ] Psychological safety frame is present and written for verbal delivery at the opening
- [ ] Judgmental and shaming phrases are absent from all facilitator scripts
- [ ] Video-assisted debriefing section is included if video was used, with timestamped cue points
- [ ] Framework selection is justified against scenario type, team composition, and emotional charge
- [ ] Guide includes a reminder that facilitators must adapt in real-time — it is a scaffold, not a verbatim script
