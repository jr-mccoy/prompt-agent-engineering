# Team-Based Learning Application Exercise Designer

**Objective:** Design a complete, facilitation-ready TBL module — from a Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT/tRAT) through a 4S-validated application problem with simultaneous group reporting and inter-team debate — for a specified clinical topic and learner level.

## When to Use
- ✅ Designing a TBL session that follows pre-class assigned readings or pre-work
- ✅ Replacing a large-group lecture with an active-learning format that still rewards pre-class preparation
- ✅ Building clinical reasoning depth when learner groups are 5-7 members and multiple teams can debate simultaneously
- ✅ Creating a module that generates inter-team disagreement to surface reasoning, not just answers
- ❌ When learner groups are fewer than 3 teams — inter-team debate requires comparison across groups to be generative
- ❌ When learners have no assigned pre-class preparation — TBL readiness assurance presupposes pre-work; without it, the iRAT/tRAT is invalid
- ❌ When the topic requires procedural skill demonstration rather than clinical decision-making — use simulation or OSCE formats instead

## Inputs Required
- **Learner level:** M1, M2, M3, M4, Resident PGY-1 to PGY-3, Fellow, or Advanced Practice Learner
- **Clinical topic:** the specific domain the TBL session addresses (e.g., "Acute kidney injury staging and management," "Antibiotic selection for community-acquired pneumonia")
- **Pre-class assigned content:** titles of readings, chapters, guidelines, or videos learners were expected to complete before class
- **Learning objectives:** 3-5 specific learning objectives for the session (the iRAT/tRAT and application exercise will be built to these)
- **Session time allocation:** typical TBL session is 75-120 minutes; specify how much time is available for iRAT, tRAT, application exercise, and debrief
- **Number of teams:** specify expected number of teams (minimum 3 for meaningful inter-team comparison)
- **Reporting method available:** colored cards (A/B/C/D), numbered paddles, letter cards, whiteboard simultaneous write, or clicker system

## Constraints

**Must:**
- Pass all 4 components of the 4S framework before writing the application exercise (see Step 4)
- Write exactly 10 iRAT items at recall/application level with full answer keys and distractor rationale
- Specify the simultaneous reporting mechanism for every application problem
- Include an inter-group debate facilitation guide with scripted pivots for derailment
- Deliver conceptual closure: the educator's synthesis statement that learners could not have generated from pre-work alone
- Calibrate iRAT items to the specified pre-class assigned content — items must be answerable from the assigned reading

**Must Not:**
- Write application exercises with multiple equally defensible answers — TBL requires one-best-answer with explicit trade-off reasoning
- Use essay, short-answer, or open-ended format for application tasks — simultaneous reporting requires a specific visible choice
- Write iRAT items that test application-level clinical decisions before readiness assurance is confirmed — iRAT/tRAT tests recall and comprehension of pre-class content only
- Design application problems that individual teams can resolve without inter-team comparison — the scenario must be rich enough that teams arrive at different defensible answers that debate can expose
- Allow the facilitator guide to substitute for real application design — if the 4S check fails, redesign the problem, do not add facilitation notes to compensate

## Instructions

1. **Collect inputs from the educator.**
   - Confirm: learner level, clinical topic, pre-class assigned content, 3-5 learning objectives, session time allocation, number of teams, and available reporting method.
   - Ask whether this is a full TBL session (RAP + application) or application exercise only (assuming RAP was completed in a prior session).
   - Confirm whether the topic warrants a single application problem or a sequence of 2-3 shorter problems across the session.

2. **Map learning objectives to RAP vs. application.**
   - Categorize each learning objective: does it test recall/comprehension (RAP territory) or application/synthesis in a novel clinical context (application exercise territory)?
   - The iRAT/tRAT should cover 60-70% of LOs at foundational level; the application exercise should require all LOs to be integrated in a realistic clinical decision.

3. **Write the Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT/tRAT).**
   - Write exactly 10 four-option MCQs (A/B/C/D) at Bloom's Recall or Comprehension level.
   - Each item must:
     a. Be answerable from the specified pre-class content — do not introduce new information
     b. Have one clearly best answer with three plausible distractors
     c. Include a distractor rationale note (why each wrong answer is tempting and what misconception it targets)
   - After the 10 items, write a complete answer key with:
     - Correct answer letter
     - One-sentence explanation of why it is correct
     - One-sentence explanation of the most common wrong answer and the concept it tests
   - Mark 2-3 items as "tRAT discussion anchors" — items where teams are most likely to disagree and where inter-team discussion will deepen understanding.

4. **Apply the 4S Framework to your application problem before writing it.**
   - **Significant problem:** Is the clinical scenario meaningful enough that learners care about the decision? (Not a textbook factoid — a real trade-off with patient consequences)
   - **Same problem for all teams:** Will every team work on exactly the same clinical scenario? (Required for inter-team comparison to be valid)
   - **Specific choice required:** Can the answer be expressed as a single, visible selection (a drug, a management approach, a diagnosis, a recommendation level)? (Open-ended answers cannot be reported simultaneously)
   - **Simultaneous reporting possible:** Can all teams reveal their answer at the same moment without seeing others' choices first? (Required to prevent anchoring)
   - If any 4S element fails, redesign the problem before proceeding. Document the 4S check result for the educator.

5. **Write the Application Problem.**
   - Write a clinical scenario of 150-250 words that presents a realistic patient context requiring integration of the session's learning objectives.
   - Frame the task as a specific clinical decision with 3-5 answer options, each representing a clinically defensible but non-equivalent choice.
   - Specify the best answer with full trade-off reasoning: why this option is best AND why each other option is defensible but inferior.
   - Write the answer options so that Option A is not trivially wrong — every option must be one that a reasonable, not-yet-expert learner might genuinely choose.
   - Specify the exact reporting instruction: "When I say 'reveal,' hold up your card simultaneously. Do not show your team's answer before the signal."

6. **Write the Facilitator Guide for the Application Exercise.**
   - **Opening the problem:** scripted language to present the scenario, confirm all teams have read it, and give teams 5-7 minutes for team deliberation.
   - **Simultaneous reveal:** scripted language for the reveal moment and how to read the room (which teams chose which option, where disagreement lies).
   - **Probing inter-team disagreement:** 3 scripted questions to use when teams disagree (e.g., "Team 3 chose B and Team 1 chose D — Team 1, what reasoning led you away from B?"). These questions must probe reasoning, not just answer.
   - **Redirecting derailment:** 2 scripted pivots for when discussion drifts to tangents (e.g., teams debating a fact rather than a trade-off, or one team dominating discussion).
   - **Handling premature consensus:** scripted language for when all teams choose the same answer — how to generate productive tension anyway (e.g., "Everyone chose B — convince me that C is wrong. Not just wrong — dangerously wrong.").
   - **Pacing:** time allocation for team deliberation, reveal, inter-team debate, and closure.

7. **Write the Inter-Team Debate Structure.**
   - Specify the opening prompt after reveal (not "who chose what?" but "Team X, walk us through your reasoning for choosing Y").
   - Write 2-3 "pivot to second team" prompts that bring in a dissenting team without devaluing the first team's reasoning.
   - Include a "steelman" prompt for the least-selected answer: "Before we close, what is the strongest argument for Option A — even if none of you chose it?"
   - Write a facilitator signal for when debate has been sufficiently productive and closure should begin.

8. **Write Conceptual Closure.**
   - Write a 150-250 word educator closure statement that synthesizes the application exercise.
   - The closure must:
     a. Name the correct answer explicitly and explain why it is best
     b. Acknowledge the strongest competing option and explain the trade-off
     c. Introduce one conceptual frame or clinical pearl that learners could not have derived from the pre-class reading alone — this is the educator's value-add
     d. Connect the decision back to at least one learning objective explicitly
   - Write a 3-sentence "take-home message" that the educator delivers verbally at the end.

9. **Optional: Design a Second Application Problem.**
   - If session time and the topic warrant a second problem, apply the 4S check again independently.
   - The second problem should advance learner decision-making to a new scenario — not simply repeat the first with different numbers.
   - Common pattern: Problem 1 tests individual patient management; Problem 2 tests a population-level, resource-allocation, or systems-level version of the same competency.

10. **Deliver the complete TBL module.**
    - Use the output format below.
    - Include an educator implementation note: recommended room setup (cluster seating), team size, materials needed (reporting cards/paddles), and timing breakdown.

## False-Positive Prevention

| ❌ Common Mistake | ✅ Correct Approach |
|---|---|
| Application exercises with multiple acceptable answers ("it depends on the patient") | TBL requires one-best answer with explicit trade-off reasoning; if it genuinely depends, reframe to "given these specific constraints, which option is best?" |
| Essay-format or open-ended application tasks | Simultaneous reporting requires a specific visible choice (card, letter, number, paddle); open-ended answers cannot be revealed simultaneously |
| iRAT items that test application-level clinical decisions rather than recall of pre-class content | iRAT/tRAT tests comprehension of assigned pre-class material; application comes after readiness is assured — mixing the two invalidates the structure |
| Skipping the 4S check and writing an application problem that generates no inter-team disagreement | A problem that all teams answer identically wastes the TBL format; 4S compliance predicts productive inter-team debate |
| Facilitator guide focused on "giving the answer" rather than probing reasoning | TBL facilitation after reveal should surface *why* teams chose differently, not validate the correct team first |
| Writing the closure before the debate | Closure synthesizes what the debate surfaced; writing it first means the educator lectures rather than integrates learner reasoning |

## Output Format

The output should be organized in the following sections, each clearly headed:

### TBL Session Overview
- Topic, learner level, session time allocation, number of teams, reporting method, educational rationale

### Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT/tRAT)
- 10 MCQs, numbered, with answer options A-D
- Answer Key: correct answer + one-sentence rationale + most common wrong answer + concept it tests
- tRAT Discussion Anchors: which 2-3 items are flagged for team discussion and why

### 4S Framework Validation
- Table with four rows (Significant / Same / Specific / Simultaneous), pass/fail status for this problem, and brief rationale for each

### Application Problem
- Clinical scenario (150-250 words)
- Answer options (3-5 options, each 1-2 sentences)
- Best answer with full trade-off rationale
- Reporting instruction (verbatim, ready for the educator to read aloud)

### Facilitator Guide
- Opening the problem (scripted)
- Simultaneous reveal protocol (scripted)
- Inter-team disagreement probes (3 scripted questions)
- Derailment pivots (2 scripted)
- Premature consensus handling (scripted)
- Pacing: time allocation table

### Inter-Team Debate Structure
- Opening prompt (after reveal)
- Pivot-to-second-team prompts (2-3)
- Steelman prompt for least-selected answer
- Facilitator closure signal

### Conceptual Closure
- Educator closure statement (150-250 words)
- Take-home message (3 sentences, verbatim for the educator)

### Educator Implementation Note
- Room setup, team size, materials, timing breakdown

## Example Output Snippet

The following is an example of a **4S Validation + Application Problem** for an M3 Internal Medicine TBL session on acute kidney injury:

---

**4S Framework Validation**

| 4S Element | Status | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Significant Problem | ✅ Pass | AKI management decision with real patient-outcome consequences — not a textbook factoid |
| Same Problem for All Teams | ✅ Pass | All teams receive identical scenario text; no team-specific variations |
| Specific Choice Required | ✅ Pass | Task is "choose one management approach" — expressible as a single lettered option |
| Simultaneous Reporting Possible | ✅ Pass | Colored cards (A/B/C/D) can be revealed simultaneously; teams instructed to hold cards face-down until signal |

**Application Problem — AKI Management**

> Mr. R.V. is a 67-year-old man with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and stage 3 CKD (baseline creatinine 1.8 mg/dL) who is admitted with community-acquired pneumonia. On admission day 2, his creatinine rises to 3.1 mg/dL and urine output drops to 18 mL/hour. His blood pressure is 102/64 mmHg. Chest X-ray shows a right lower lobe consolidation without pulmonary edema. Urine sodium is 8 mEq/L and urine osmolality is 510 mOsm/kg. He is currently on vancomycin, piperacillin-tazobactam, lisinopril 10 mg daily, metformin 1000 mg twice daily, and IV normal saline at 75 mL/hour.
>
> **Your team's task:** Identify the single most important medication management action to take immediately.
>
> **A.** Hold lisinopril, continue metformin, increase IV saline to 125 mL/hour
> **B.** Hold lisinopril and metformin, increase IV saline to 125 mL/hour, check vancomycin trough
> **C.** Hold all nephrotoxic agents, initiate emergent hemodialysis consult
> **D.** Continue current medications, add IV furosemide to restore urine output

**Best Answer: B** — Lisinopril causes afferent/efferent arteriolar dilation impairing renal autoregulation in the setting of volume depletion; holding it is highest priority. Metformin must be held because lactic acidosis risk rises sharply when GFR drops acutely. Volume expansion is appropriate given pre-renal physiology (FeNa < 1%). Vancomycin trough monitoring is required because vancomycin is renally cleared and toxic levels compound injury. Option A misses the metformin hold. Option C overreacts — emergent HD is not indicated without refractory fluid overload or severe electrolyte instability. Option D is actively harmful.

## Verification Checklist
- [ ] Learner level explicitly specified and iRAT items calibrated to pre-class assigned content for that level
- [ ] Competency framework alignment named and reflected in at least one learning objective (ACGME/CanMEDS/EPA)
- [ ] 4S Framework check completed and documented before application problem was written
- [ ] All 10 iRAT items include distractor rationale, not just answer keys
- [ ] Application problem has one-best answer with full trade-off reasoning for each option
- [ ] Simultaneous reporting method specified and reporting instruction written verbatim
- [ ] Facilitator guide includes scripted probes for disagreement, not just general facilitation advice
- [ ] Conceptual closure introduces a frame or pearl not available from pre-class reading alone
