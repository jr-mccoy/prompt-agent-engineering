# Crazy Eights — Eight Ideas in Eight Minutes

**Objective:** Run a hard time-box that forces eight distinct ideas at roughly one per minute, each captured as a single sentence, with no deliberation and no editing during the sprint. The artificial pace is the mechanism: it starves the inner editor of the time it needs to reject ideas, and it pushes past the first two or three obvious answers that everyone reaches for. Crazy Eights is a *ritual*, not an analysis — its job is to produce a fast spray of raw material and a short debrief on what surprised you, which then feeds a separate selection step.

**When to use:**
- Opening a workshop or ideation session — it warms up divergent thinking and breaks the politeness that suppresses odd ideas.
- A solo unblock when staring at a blank page; the clock does the work the willpower won't.
- You need many starting points fast, not a few polished ones.
- Resetting a group that has anchored on one idea too early.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need depth or feasibility analysis on a small number of options — this produces breadth, not depth.
- The task rewards careful, deliberate construction (e.g., writing a contract clause). Speed is counterproductive there.
- The group is already past divergence and needs to converge. Use a kill-list or dot-vote instead.

**Audience:** Designers, PMs, founders, and workshop facilitators running divergence sprints; individuals fighting a blank page.

---

## Inputs / Context

1. **The prompt / brief.** One sharp sentence: "ideas for X." Specific enough that an idea is recognizably on-topic.
2. **The time-box.** Default 8 ideas in 8 minutes. Scale to 16-in-16 or 24-in-24 for longer sprints or when warming up a large group across rounds.
3. **Format.** Sketch, one-liner, or both. In text mode, each idea is one sentence; the "sketch" is the one-line descriptor.
4. **Solo or group.** Group runs are individual-first (everyone generates alone, shares after) to prevent anchoring.
5. **Any off-limits ideas.** Already-decided directions to exclude so the sprint stays fresh.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Produce **exactly the target count** (8, 16, or 24). Hitting the number is the discipline; a blank slot is a failure of the ritual.
- One **single sentence** per idea — a recognizable, distinct concept, not a paragraph.
- **No editing or deletion** during the sprint. A weak idea stays on the list; it may seed a strong one.
- Keep ideas **distinct** — no relabeling idea #2 as #5. If you stall, apply a switch heuristic (see below) rather than repeating.
- End with a **debrief block**: what surprised you, where the obvious ideas stopped and the interesting ones started, which idea you'd never have reached deliberately.
- In group mode, generate **individually first**, then collate — do not generate aloud as a group during the sprint.

### Must Not
- Deliberate, rank, or critique mid-sprint. The whole point is to outrun the editor.
- Pad the count with variants of one idea to "finish." A variant is allowed only if it's genuinely distinct.
- Polish wording. A rough, clear sentence beats a perfect, slow one.
- Skip the debrief — the surprise audit is where the sprint's intelligence lives.
- Stop early because "the good ones are out." The last two or three are often the point.

---

## Switch heuristics (use only when stalled)

If you stall before hitting the count, switch frames rather than stopping:
- **Opposite:** the inverse of the idea you just wrote.
- **Persona:** what would a child / a competitor / a regulator do?
- **Scale:** the same idea at 100× or 1/100th the size.
- **Remove:** the idea with its most central feature deleted.
- **Combine:** two earlier ideas fused into one.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — State the prompt and set the clock
Restate the brief in one sentence. Declare the count and the time-box. Note any off-limits directions.

### Step 2 — Generate at pace
Produce ideas one at a time, roughly one per minute. Each is a single sentence. Do not look back, do not refine, do not judge. Keep moving.

### Step 3 — Hit the number
If you stall, apply a switch heuristic and keep generating until the full count is reached. Do not leave blanks.

### Step 4 — Stop the clock
The instant the count is hit, stop generating. Resist the urge to "add one more good one" — the ritual is closed.

### Step 5 — Mark the turn
Identify where the obvious ideas stopped (usually #1–3) and where the non-obvious ones began. This tells you how deep you had to go.

### Step 6 — Surprise debrief
Write the debrief: which idea surprised you, which one you'd never have reached by deliberation, which one you want to keep poking at, and any pattern across the list.

### Step 7 — Hand off (no selection)
Hand the raw list and debrief to a convergence step (`ideation_idea_kill_list.md` or `ideation_idea_convergence_dot_voting.md`). Do not select a winner inside the sprint.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Mid-sprint editing.** Refining or deleting while generating reintroduces the editor the time-box was meant to silence. Capture everything, judge later.
2. **Blank slots.** Stopping at 6 because "ideas ran out" forfeits the point. Apply a switch heuristic and reach the number.
3. **Variant padding.** "App, app with dark mode, app with widgets" is one idea. Force distinct concepts; use the combine/opposite heuristic instead of relabeling.
4. **Polish creep.** Spending two minutes wording one idea breaks the pace. Rough and clear wins.
5. **Group anchoring.** Brainstorming aloud as a group makes the first speaker's idea contaminate everyone's. Generate solo, share after.
6. **Debrief skip.** Producing eight ideas and walking away discards the most useful output: knowing which idea you'd never have reached deliberately.
7. **Premature convergence.** Picking a favorite at the buzzer collapses the sprint into a decision. The sprint diverges; a separate step converges.
8. **Quality anxiety.** Treating each idea as a commitment slows everything. They are disposable raw material; treat them that way.
