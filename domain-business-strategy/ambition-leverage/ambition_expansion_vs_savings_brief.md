# Expansion vs Pocketing AI Savings: A Board-Ready Brief

**Objective:** Produce a board-ready brief (3–5 pages) that frames the question "should we reinvest AI-driven productivity gains into expansion, or pocket them as cost savings and margin?" The brief must present both cases with honest numbers, name the dominant strategic factor, give a recommendation, and specify the conditions under which the recommendation would flip.

**When to use:** When the CFO is modeling a margin bump from AI adoption and the CEO suspects that's leaving growth on the table. When the board is asking how AI changes the company's strategy, not just the cost line. When a competitor's aggressive reinvestment forces the question. At annual planning before budget is frozen.

**Audience:** Board, CEO, CFO. Tone: serious, numerate, unflinching about both paths. Readers should be able to defend the case they disagree with after reading.

---

## Inputs Required

1. **Estimated AI-driven productivity gains.** Realized and credible projected. Specifically: which functions, how much cost freed up, what confidence level.
2. **Current strategic state.** Growth rate, margin profile, market position, balance-sheet strength.
3. **Competitive landscape.** Where competitors are investing their AI gains — if known. Public signals are fine; mark as inference.
4. **Capital discipline constraints.** Debt covenants, investor expectations, board prior commitments on margin or cash position.
5. **Expansion options the CEO / strategy team has already identified.** Even rough — new segment, new product, geographic expansion, capacity increase. If none, the brief will flag that as a constraint.
6. **The CFO's current plan** for how AI savings flow through (margin expansion, debt paydown, share buyback, headcount reduction). Needed as the baseline against which expansion is compared.

If estimated gains (input 1) are speculative without any realization yet, the brief's ranges will widen. Flag this explicitly — a savings-vs-reinvestment decision on projected gains is different from one on realized gains.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1 — Quantify the pool

One clean number (with range) for the annual pool of AI-driven productivity gains available to redeploy. Distinguish:
- **Realized gains:** savings already captured, reflected in current quarter cost base.
- **Credible projected:** savings expected within 12 months with the current rollout plan.
- **Speculative:** savings that require capability or adoption assumptions that haven't been proven.

Board briefs usually want the realized + credible-projected number. The speculative portion is a separate row, not ignored.

### Step 2 — The "pocket" case

Write the case for taking savings as margin / cash. Cover:
- **Margin impact:** how much does margin expand at current volume.
- **Cash use:** debt paydown, buyback, dividend, balance-sheet strength.
- **Signaling:** what this signals to investors, employees, and competitors.
- **Talent implications:** headcount action, if any. Be specific. "Right-size" is not specific; "reduce 80 roles in X function over 12 months" is.
- **What this case assumes:** the market is mature, growth is expensive, capital discipline is the right posture, or the company has specific capital-return commitments.

### Step 3 — The "expand" case

Write the case for reinvesting savings into expansion. Cover the three expansion channels separately:
- **Same market, more capacity** — hire more of what we have, do more of what we do.
- **Adjacent market / segment** — new customer segment, new geography, new product line.
- **Category-redefining** — use AI leverage to do something the company couldn't have done pre-AI. Higher risk, potentially higher return.

For each, quantify (with ranges): investment required, timeline to revenue, expected return, probability of success based on organizational readiness.

Name what each channel assumes about the company's capabilities and the market's tolerance.

### Step 4 — The competitive angle

One or two paragraphs. If competitors are taking one path and this company takes the other, what happens?

- If competitors reinvest and this company pockets: do they out-grow us? By when?
- If competitors pocket and this company reinvests: do we out-grow them? Are we over-building ahead of demand?

Honest assessment. Competitive response isn't the whole decision, but it's a serious input.

### Step 5 — The dominant factor

One paragraph. Across all the inputs, what actually decides this? Common dominants:

- **Capital position:** if the company needs cash for balance-sheet reasons, pocket dominates regardless of opportunity.
- **Market trajectory:** if the market is growing and AI opens new segments, expansion dominates.
- **Organizational readiness:** if the team can't execute expansion (talent, leadership, systems), pocket dominates by default; the reinvestment case is theoretical.
- **Competitor aggression:** if competitors are making a multi-year bet and we don't respond, expansion becomes necessary defense.

Naming the dominant factor prevents the brief from becoming a neutral "both have merits" non-answer.

### Step 6 — Recommendation with scope

The recommendation should be specific:
- **Mix** — if splitting, what share goes to expansion vs pocket, and by when.
- **Sequencing** — expansion-first or pocket-first over a 12–24 month window.
- **Triggers** — what observable events shift the mix.

"Invest in expansion" without specifying the mix is not a recommendation.

### Step 7 — Invalidation conditions

List 3–5 conditions under which the recommendation flips:
- "If realized AI gains come in >20% below current forecast by Q3, the pocket case strengthens materially."
- "If the [named] adjacent segment shows [specific weakness] in the pilot, the expansion case narrows to [alternative channel] only."
- "If [named] competitor's announced expansion falters, the defensive case for expansion weakens."

Without these, the brief is advocacy.

### Step 8 — What this does not decide

One short paragraph naming what the brief explicitly leaves to a separate decision:
- The specific expansion target.
- The specific capital-return mechanism.
- Headcount actions.
- Timing of specific hires.

A strategic brief shouldn't masquerade as a plan.

---

## Constraints

### Must
- Quantify the pool with realized / projected / speculative distinction.
- Present both cases with numbers and named assumptions.
- Evaluate at least three expansion channels.
- Name the dominant factor explicitly.
- Recommendation specifies mix, sequencing, and triggers.
- Invalidation conditions are specific.

### Must Not
- Conclude "both have merits" without naming the dominant factor.
- Use vendor / consultant projections of AI gains as if they were realized.
- Treat buyback as a non-option if the board has raised it; treat expansion as a non-option if the CEO has raised it — the brief should evaluate both seriously.
- Recommend expansion into a channel the organization does not have the capability to execute.
- Exceed 5 pages.

---

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **Don't conflate "we should expand" with "we can expand."** Expansion requires capability. An honest readiness check can flip the recommendation.
2. **Don't over-discount the pocket case.** Margin expansion is real value; a company that takes margin can reinvest later from a stronger position.
3. **Don't let competitive paranoia drive the recommendation.** "They're doing it so we must" is a weak case; "their move changes the market in [specific way]" is a real case.
4. **Don't use speculative AI gains as the basis for the expansion case.** If the gains aren't realized, reinvesting them is reinvesting future savings that may not appear.
5. **Don't bury the talent / capacity requirement.** The expansion case has a talent cost. Name it.
6. **Don't let "invest in expansion" read as a blank check.** Specific mix, specific triggers.
