# Newsletter Positioning and Cadence

**Objective:** Produce a newsletter definition someone can act on this week — a
one-sentence promise, a named reader, a repeatable format, and a cadence derived
from the hours actually available rather than the hours wished for.

**When to Use:**
- You are starting a newsletter and have not decided what it is.
- You have a newsletter that publishes irregularly and you cannot say why.
- Open rates are fine and nothing compounds, because each issue is about a
  different thing for a different person.
- You are about to promise "weekly" and want to know whether you can hold it.

**When NOT to use:**
- You need blog or SEO topic architecture — that is
  `domain-agentic-resources/skills/marketing/content-strategy/`, which owns
  pillars and topic clusters and has no email mode.
- You are an app developer marketing your own software on 2–4 hours a week —
  `go-to-market/marketing_content_strategy_solo_dev.md` is scoped to exactly
  that.
- You need to *write* an issue. That is
  `domain-professional-writing/writing/writing_newsletter_issue.md`.
- You need channel mix and growth-rate planning — that is
  `creator_list_growth_plan.md`.

## Inputs / Context

The user supplies:

1. **What they know that someone would pay attention for.** Not credentials —
   the specific thing they can see that a reader cannot.
2. **Who the reader is**, as a person with a situation: what they are trying to
   do, what they have already read, what they would forward.
3. **Hours per week available for writing**, measured from the last four weeks
   rather than estimated.
4. **What already exists** — drafts, a blog, a body of client work, a podcast.
5. **What the newsletter is ultimately for** — audience for a product, inbound
   for services, a body of work, or genuinely nothing commercial.
6. **Constraints** — employer restrictions, anonymity, subject-matter limits.

## Method

1. **Write the promise, in one sentence, in the reader's words.**
   Form: *"Every [cadence], [reader] gets [specific thing] so they can [do
   what]."* If the sentence needs an "and", the newsletter has two promises and
   one of them is not the newsletter.

2. **Name the reader tightly enough to exclude people (RT-05).**
   A reader definition that excludes nobody is decoration. State two people who
   are *not* the reader and would reasonably think they are.

3. **Derive the cadence from measured hours, not ambition (DS-06).**
   - Estimate hours per issue honestly: research, draft, edit, ship.
   - Divide available hours by hours per issue. That is the ceiling.
   - Take the tier **below** the ceiling. A cadence held for a year beats a
     faster one abandoned in six weeks, and the abandonment is the thing
     readers remember.
   - State the bad-week version: the shortest issue that still keeps the
     promise. If there isn't one, the format is too heavy.

4. **Fix the format (CM-02).**
   One repeating structure, named sections, a stated length band. Format is
   what makes the newsletter writable on a Tuesday when nothing feels
   interesting, and what makes it recognisable in an inbox.

5. **Decide what each issue must contain to be worth sending.**
   A single non-negotiable element — one idea the reader had not had, one
   thing they can do, one artifact. This is the quality floor, and it is what
   you check before hitting send.

6. **Verify against the promise (QA-01).**
   Draft three issue titles. If any of them would be strange to a subscriber who
   read the promise, either the title is wrong or the promise is.

## Output Format

```
# Newsletter definition — [working name]

## Promise
Every [cadence], [reader] gets [thing] so they can [outcome].

## Reader
- Is: [situation, not demographics]
- Is not: [two people who would wrongly think they are]
- Would forward it to: [whom, and why]

## Cadence
| Available hrs/week | Hrs per issue | Ceiling | Chosen cadence | Why below ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Bad-week version: [the shortest issue that still keeps the promise]

## Format
| Section | What it does | Length |
|---|---|---|

Quality floor — an issue does not ship without: [the one non-negotiable]

## Three test issues
1. [title] — keeps the promise because [...]
2. ...

## What this newsletter is for
[audience for a product | inbound for services | a body of work | nothing commercial]
— and what would tell you it is working, in 90 days.

## Open questions
- [what the user could not answer, and what it blocks]
```

## Verification

- [ ] The promise is one sentence with no "and".
- [ ] Two people who are *not* the reader are named.
- [ ] Cadence is derived from measured hours, and sits below the ceiling.
- [ ] A bad-week version of the format exists.
- [ ] The quality floor is one checkable thing, not a list of aspirations.
- [ ] Three test titles all sit inside the promise.

## False-Positive Prevention

1. **A promise that excludes nobody promises nothing.** "Insights for people who
   want to grow" is not a promise. If you cannot name someone it is wrong for,
   rewrite it.
2. **Ambition cadence is the most common failure and the most expensive.**
   Weekly is a commitment to 52 things. The measured ceiling is the fact; the
   chosen cadence must sit below it.
3. **Do not promise a number you have not measured.** "Grow to 10k subscribers"
   is not positioning and is not something this prompt can support.
4. **A format is not a template you fill.** If the format makes an uninteresting
   week publishable but boring, it is the wrong format — the bad-week version
   should be *shorter*, not emptier.
5. **Repositioning an existing newsletter has a cost.** Existing subscribers
   subscribed to the old promise. Say what the change costs and to whom, rather
   than treating the list as carried over for free.
