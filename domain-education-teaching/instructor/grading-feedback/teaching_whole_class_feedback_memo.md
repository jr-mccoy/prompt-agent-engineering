# Whole-Class Feedback Memo

## Objective

Synthesize a stack of student responses (essays, lab reports, problem sets, projects) into a one- or two-page memo delivered to the whole class. The memo names patterns, lifts up exemplar moves (anonymized), surfaces common errors, and gives every student a next-action menu — without per-paper comments.

## When to Use

- Stack of 25–150 papers where individual feedback is impractical
- Mid-unit drafts where students will revise
- Common assessments across sections
- After a quiz/test where everyone will work on the same misconceptions
- Reducing grading load while keeping feedback quality

## When NOT to Use

- Final summative work that must be individually scored — also use `grading_essay_feedback_by_rubric_criterion.md`
- One-off pieces of student work — too little signal for a class memo
- Sensitive assignments (personal narratives, etc.) where peer comparison is uncomfortable

- **Memo length cap:** [1 page / 2 pages]

## Instructions

### Step 1: Pattern Mining

From the sample, identify:

| Pattern type | Count this sample shows |
|--------------|------------------------|
| **Exemplar moves** — specific things multiple students did well | At least 3 |
| **Common errors** — same mistake or misconception in multiple papers | At least 3 |
| **Surface gaps** — formatting, citations, conventions | List separately |
| **Outliers** — interesting moves only one or two students made | At least 1 |

Quantify when possible: "About a third of you…", "Roughly half…", "A handful of you…"

### Step 2: Frame the Memo

Choose a single throughline — the one or two ideas that will actually drive revision. The memo is not a comprehensive critique. It's a directed lever.

State the throughline in one sentence at the top.

### Step 3: Memo Structure

```
TO: [Class period or course]
FROM: [Teacher]
RE: Feedback on [assignment]
DATE: [...]

THE BIG PICTURE
[1–2 sentences naming the throughline. What you saw across the stack. What we're going to do about it.]

WHAT YOU DID WELL (with anonymous examples)
1. [Move name]. About [proportion] of you did this. Example: "[anonymized quoted student move]."
2. [Move name]. Example: "[...]"
3. [Move name]. Example: "[...]"

THE COMMON ERRORS
1. [Error name]. About [proportion] of papers had this. What it looks like: "[anonymized example]." Why it's a problem: [in rubric terms]. The fix: [verb + object].
2. [Error name]. ...
3. [Error name]. ...

YOUR NEXT-ACTION MENU
Choose ONE based on what you know about your own draft:

A. If your essay [diagnostic — e.g., "doesn't have a clear thesis"]:
   → Try [specific revision move]. Look at [where in your essay].

B. If your essay [diagnostic]:
   → Try [...]

C. If your essay [diagnostic]:
   → Try [...]

D. If you scored at the top of the rubric:
   → Stretch by [...]

WHAT'S NEXT
[Class plan — when revision happens, when due, what conferring you'll offer]
```

### Step 4: Anonymization Discipline

For every quoted student move (positive or negative):

- Strip names, identifiable details, and any personal content
- Paraphrase if the quote could identify a specific student
- Mix exemplars from across the performance range — not always the top scorers
- For sensitive topics, avoid quoting at all; describe the move generically

### Step 5: Tone Calibration

The memo is to the whole class. Adjust:

- **Warm but direct** — "About a third of you skipped the analysis step. Let's fix that." (Not: "Many students failed to…")
- **Plural, not finger-pointing** — "we" and "many of you," not "some of you"
- **Specific, not preachy** — name the move, not the moral
- **Action-oriented** — every error has a fix; every fix is a verb

### Step 6: Equity in the Exemplar Set

Audit the exemplar moves you cited:

- [ ] Came from a range of papers, not the same 2 students
- [ ] Lift moves from emerging writers as well as advanced ones (a sentence move, an organizational choice)
- [ ] Don't reinforce a single "voice" or topic preference

If your exemplars cluster on the high-scoring papers, deliberately add 1–2 from middle papers showing real improvement or interesting attempts.

### Step 7: Conferring Schedule (Optional)

If the memo points students to revise, offer:

- Sign-up for 5-minute conferences during [time]
- Office hours at [time]
- Peer-feedback partners with this protocol: [...]

### Step 8: Length Audit

Count: is the memo at or below the cap? If over, cut the lowest-leverage error or move. Most stacks have 3 patterns worth naming, not 7.

---

## Output Format

1. The memo, formatted as it will reach students
2. Pattern-mining notes (teacher-facing, not for distribution)
3. Anonymization confirmation
4. Conferring schedule (optional)

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Quote a student in a way that identifies them
- List every error you saw — the memo gets ignored
- Praise without specificity ("great work everyone!")
- Use shaming language ("I was disappointed…")
- Forget the next-action menu — feedback without actionability is wallpaper

✅ **DO:**
- Quantify patterns with proportions
- Anonymize ruthlessly
- Pull exemplar moves from a range of papers
- End with a menu so every student has a next step
- Cap at 1–2 pages

---

## Quality Indicators

- [ ] Throughline named in one sentence at top
- [ ] 3 exemplars + 3 errors with quoted examples
- [ ] Every error paired with a fix
- [ ] Next-action menu has at least 4 paths
- [ ] Anonymization audit completed
- [ ] Within length cap

| Technique | How It's Applied |
