**Purpose:** Design age-appropriate AI literacy lessons that teach students how AI works, how to evaluate AI outputs critically, how to use AI tools responsibly, and how to reason about the ethical implications of AI — aligned to emerging AI literacy frameworks (UNESCO, ISTE, CSTA).

**When to use:** Teaching students about AI for the first time; integrating AI literacy into existing subjects; designing AI acceptable use policies with students; preparing students for AI-integrated assignments; building digital citizenship around AI tools; professional development sessions for educators.

**Input needed:**
- Grade band (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12, Higher Ed)
- Specific AI literacy focus area
- Prior AI experience of students
- Available AI tools (if any)

## Instructions

Design AI literacy lessons through these structured phases:

### Step 1: Select Age-Appropriate Learning Objectives

**K-2 (Ages 5-8) — "AI Is a Tool, Not a Person"**

| Objective | Activity Type |
|-----------|--------------|
| Explain that AI is a computer program, not a living thing | Sorting: "Is it alive?" (plants, animals, robots, AI) |
| Identify AI in everyday life (voice assistants, recommendations) | Scavenger hunt: find AI in our day |
| Understand that AI learns from data (examples/patterns) | Train a "robot" classmate to sort objects by giving examples |
| Recognize that AI can make mistakes | Play "AI says" (like Simon Says) — AI gives wrong instructions sometimes |
| Express that humans are in charge of AI decisions | Role play: "You're the boss, AI is your helper" |

**3-5 (Ages 8-11) — "AI Learns From Data"**

| Objective | Activity Type |
|-----------|--------------|
| Explain how AI uses training data to make predictions | "Guess my rule" classification game |
| Identify bias in AI (bad data = bad results) | Train a biased classifier with unbalanced data |
| Evaluate AI-generated text for accuracy | Fact-check an AI response about a familiar topic |
| Understand what prompts are and how they affect output | Prompt experiment: same question, different wording |
| Discuss when to use AI and when to use your own brain | Sorting activity: "AI helper or human thinker?" |

**6-8 (Ages 11-14) — "How AI Decides and Why It Matters"**

| Objective | Activity Type |
|-----------|--------------|
| Describe how machine learning works (training, patterns, prediction) | Interactive: train a simple classifier (Teachable Machine) |
| Analyze bias in AI systems with real examples | Case study: biased facial recognition, biased recommendations |
| Critically evaluate AI-generated content for accuracy, bias, and completeness | Side-by-side comparison: AI answer vs. verified source |
| Write effective prompts and iterate based on results | Prompt engineering challenge with structured criteria |
| Debate ethical questions about AI use in society | Structured debate: "Should AI grade student essays?" |

**9-12 (Ages 14-18) — "AI Citizenship and Responsible Use"**

| Objective | Activity Type |
|-----------|--------------|
| Explain AI model architectures at a conceptual level (neural networks, LLMs) | Interactive visualization + analogy building |
| Evaluate AI outputs for hallucination, bias, and reasoning errors | Structured audit of AI responses in their subject area |
| Apply AI tools effectively and ethically in academic work | Complete a task with AI assistance + reflection on what AI did vs. student did |
| Analyze societal impacts of AI (labor, privacy, equity, environment) | Research project: AI impact in a chosen industry |
| Develop and defend a personal AI use framework | Write and present personal AI ethics statement |
| Understand and apply AI citation and disclosure practices | Practice citing AI-assisted work using school/disciplinary standards |

**Higher Ed / Professional Development — "AI as Professional Tool"**

| Objective | Activity Type |
|-----------|--------------|
| Evaluate AI capabilities and limitations in their discipline | Discipline-specific AI output evaluation |
| Develop course or classroom AI policies | Policy drafting workshop using AI Assessment Scale |
| Design AI-integrated assignments that maintain rigor | Assignment redesign workshop |
| Model responsible AI use for students or colleagues | Create and present demonstration lesson |

### Step 2: Design the Lesson

**Lesson Structure:**

| Phase | Time | Activity | Purpose |
|-------|------|----------|---------|
| **Hook** | 5 min | [Surprising AI demo, question, or myth-busting] | Engage curiosity |
| **Explore** | 10-15 min | [Hands-on AI interaction or unplugged activity] | Build experience before explanation |
| **Explain** | 10 min | [Key concept instruction — what AI is, how it works, why it matters] | Vocabulary and conceptual framework |
| **Apply** | 15-20 min | [Guided practice with AI or scenarios] | Apply understanding |
| **Reflect** | 5-10 min | [Discussion, journal, exit ticket] | Metacognition and critical thinking |

**Unplugged Activities (No Technology Needed):**

| Activity | Concept Taught | Grade Band | Materials |
|----------|---------------|-----------|-----------|
| **"Train the Robot"** | AI learns from examples | K-5 | Index cards with images, a volunteer "robot" |
| **"Biased Basket"** | Training data bias | 3-8 | Two baskets, colored objects (unbalanced sets) |
| **"AI or Human?"** | AI capabilities/limitations | 3-12 | Cards with text samples (some AI, some human) |
| **"Prompt Telephone"** | Prompt specificity matters | 2-8 | Paper, pencils (draw based on verbal instructions) |
| **"Algorithm Walk"** | Algorithms = step-by-step instructions | K-5 | Maze or obstacle course, blindfolded partner |
| **"Data Detective"** | AI needs data to learn | 3-8 | Survey sheets, sticky notes for pattern-finding |

**Hands-On AI Activities (Technology Needed):**

| Activity | Concept Taught | Grade Band | Tool |
|----------|---------------|-----------|------|
| **Prompt experiment** | Prompt engineering basics | 3-12 | Any chatbot |
| **Fact-check challenge** | AI accuracy evaluation | 4-12 | Any chatbot + reference sources |
| **Teachable Machine** | Training a classifier | 5-12 | Google Teachable Machine (free) |
| **AI art comparison** | AI creativity and authorship | 3-12 | Any image generator |
| **Bias audit** | AI bias detection | 7-12 | Chatbot + structured audit rubric |
| **AI debate prep** | AI as research tool | 6-12 | Chatbot + primary sources |

### Step 3: Build Critical Evaluation Skills

**The CLEAR Framework for Evaluating AI Output:**

| Letter | Step | Student Action |
|--------|------|----------------|
| **C** | Claim check | What claims does the AI make? Are they stated as fact or opinion? |
| **L** | Look for sources | Does the AI cite sources? Can you verify them? (Watch for fabricated citations) |
| **E** | Examine for bias | Whose perspective is represented? Whose is missing? |
| **A** | Accuracy verify | Cross-reference key facts with a reliable, non-AI source |
| **R** | Reasoning assess | Does the logic make sense? Are there gaps or contradictions? |

**Evaluation Practice Template:**

| Evaluation Criterion | AI Response | Your Assessment | Evidence |
|---------------------|-------------|-----------------|----------|
| Factual accuracy | [AI claim] | [Correct / Incorrect / Partially correct] | [Source that confirms/contradicts] |
| Completeness | [What AI covered] | [Missing anything important?] | [What was left out] |
| Bias / perspective | [Whose view is represented] | [Is this balanced?] | [Missing perspectives] |
| Reasoning quality | [AI's logic chain] | [Sound / Flawed / Incomplete] | [Where the logic breaks down] |

### Step 4: Ethical Reasoning Scenarios

**Age-Appropriate Ethical Discussion Prompts:**

**K-2:**
- "Is it fair if a computer picks who gets to play a game and it always picks the same kids?"
- "If a robot draws a picture, who made the art — the robot or the person who told it what to draw?"

**3-5:**
- "A student uses AI to write their book report. They turn it in as their own work. What do you think about this?"
- "An AI suggests that only boys like sports and only girls like art. What's wrong with this?"

**6-8:**
- "Your school starts using AI to predict which students might fail a class. What are the benefits and risks?"
- "An AI chatbot is trained on the internet and sometimes says offensive things. Whose fault is it — the AI, the company, or the users who wrote the training data?"

**9-12:**
- "A company uses AI to screen job applications. The AI was trained on past hiring data where the company mostly hired men. What should happen?"
- "You used AI to help brainstorm and outline your essay, but you wrote every sentence yourself. Should you disclose the AI use? Where is the line?"
- "AI-generated deepfakes can create realistic videos of anyone saying anything. Should this technology exist? How should it be regulated?"

**Discussion Protocol:**
1. Present the scenario
2. Individual thinking time (1 minute)
3. Partner share (2 minutes)
4. Whole-class discussion using structured format
5. Reflection: "Did your thinking change? How?"

### Step 5: AI Acceptable Use Guidelines (Co-Created with Students)

**Framework for Class AI Policy:**

| Category | Questions to Decide Together |
|----------|----------------------------|
| **When to use** | Which assignments allow AI? Which don't? Why? |
| **How to use** | What's the difference between AI as a brainstorming tool vs. AI writing for you? |
| **How to cite** | How do we acknowledge AI use? What needs to be disclosed? |
| **What to share** | What personal information should never go into AI tools? |
| **How to verify** | How do we check AI output before using it? |

**AI Use Levels (adapted from the AI Assessment Scale):**

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|------------|---------|
| **Level 1: No AI** | Assignment completed entirely without AI | In-class essay, personal reflection |
| **Level 2: AI as Idea Generator** | Use AI for brainstorming, then do all work yourself | Use AI for topic ideas, then research and write independently |
| **Level 3: AI as Editor** | Complete work first, then use AI for feedback/revision | Write draft → ask AI for feedback → revise yourself |
| **Level 4: AI as Collaborator** | Use AI throughout the process with clear documentation | Co-draft with AI, revise extensively, annotate what AI contributed |
| **Level 5: AI as Tool** | Use AI as you would a calculator or search engine | Analyze data with AI, verify results, interpret meaning yourself |

Structure the AI literacy lesson as:
1. **Lesson Overview** (objectives, standards alignment, materials)
2. **Lesson Procedure** (phased with timing)

## Quality Indicators

**Effective AI literacy lessons include:**
- [ ] Age-appropriate conceptual accuracy (not oversimplified to the point of being wrong)
- [ ] Hands-on experience (not just lecture about AI)
- [ ] Critical thinking emphasis (not just "AI is cool" or "AI is scary")
- [ ] Ethical reasoning practice (not just rules to follow)
- [ ] Connection to students' real lives and experiences
- [ ] Balanced perspective (acknowledges benefits AND risks)
- [ ] Actionable skills students can apply immediately
- [ ] Student voice in developing AI use norms

**Common AI Literacy Pitfalls:**

| Pitfall | Sign | Prevention |
|---------|------|------------|
| **Anthropomorphizing AI** | "AI thinks..." / "AI wants..." | Use precise language: "AI processes," "AI generates," "AI was trained to" |
| **All hype or all fear** | Lesson is entirely positive or entirely cautionary | Present balanced, evidence-based view |
| **Too abstract** | Students can't connect to their experience | Use examples from their daily lives (recommendations, autocomplete, filters) |
| **Adults-know-best framing** | Policies handed down without student input | Co-create guidelines with students |
| **One-and-done** | Single lesson on AI with no follow-up | Integrate AI literacy across subjects throughout the year |
| **Ignoring equity** | Assuming all students have equal AI access | Address the AI divide; include unplugged activities |
