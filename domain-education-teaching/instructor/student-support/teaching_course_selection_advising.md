# Student Course-Selection Advising Conversation

## Objective

Run a structured one-on-one course-selection conversation with a student (high-school or higher-ed) that surfaces goals, constraints, and tradeoffs; tests options against requirements (which the user supplies); and produces a schedule the student can defend, with documented rationale and a fallback plan.

## When to Use

- Pre-registration advising for next term/year
- Major / pathway selection conversation
- Course-load adjustment after a tough term
- Transfer student integrating prior credits
- Student in distress about schedule (anxiety about over/under-loading)
- Senior-year final schedule with graduation in scope

## When NOT to Use

- Multi-year graduation audit — use `advising_graduation_tracker.md`
- Career counseling beyond course choice — different scope; refer
- Mental-health distress that exceeds advising scope — refer to counseling

- **Student level:** [HS grade / undergrad year / grad / non-traditional]
- **Authoritative requirements (user-supplied):** [Graduation requirements / major requirements / pathway rules — paste or summarize]
- **Available courses next term:** [Catalog or course list with times, credits, prerequisites]
- **Transcript / progress to date:** [Completed, in progress, GPA]
- **Stated goals:** [Major, career, post-secondary aspiration, personal interest]
- **Constraints:** [Work hours, family responsibilities, transportation, mental/physical health, athletics, extracurriculars]
- **Strengths & struggles:** [Subjects historically strong / hard]
- **Time available for advising:** [10 / 20 / 30 / 60 minutes]
- **Decision authority:** [Student decides / parent involved / advisor approval required]

## Instructions

### Step 1: Open With Intent

The first 1–2 minutes set the tone. Ask:

- "What's on your mind for next term?"
- "What do you want to leave this conversation with?"

Listen for: anxiety, locked-in plan, total uncertainty, family pressure, recent setback. Adjust the rest accordingly.

### Step 2: Surface Goals at the Right Altitude

Don't jump to courses. Clarify:

- **Long-horizon goal** — graduation, major, career direction, life context
- **Mid-horizon goal** — what this academic year achieves
- **Term-level goal** — what next term needs to do for the mid- and long-horizon

If the student doesn't have a long-horizon goal, that's fine — note it; build a portfolio that keeps options open.

### Step 3: Inventory Constraints

Some students under-disclose constraints. Probe:

- Work hours (current and changes)
- Family / caregiving
- Transportation, housing
- Health (chronic, recent, mental health)
- Sleep / energy patterns
- Activities / commitments
- Course-load history (what's worked, what's overwhelmed)

A schedule that ignores constraints fails by week 4.

### Step 4: Map Requirements Against Inventory

From the authoritative requirements the user supplied, list:

| Requirement | Status | Must address this term? |
|-------------|--------|--------------------------|
| [E.g., 4 English credits] | [Earned 3, in 1] | [Yes / No / Optional] |

Identify which slots next term *must* fill vs. *may* fill.

### Step 5: Generate Options

For each open slot, produce 2–4 candidate courses with:

- Course title and code
- Credits
- Time slot
- Prerequisites met / unmet
- Difficulty estimate (workload, instructor-known reputation, prior data if available)
- Schedule fit with other commitments
- Goal-alignment note

Avoid pre-recommending; let the student weigh.

### Step 6: Build 2–3 Schedule Scenarios

Scenarios force choice clarity:

| Scenario | Schedule | Pros | Cons | Risk |
|----------|----------|------|------|------|
| A — Stretch | [4 hard + 1 easy] | Maximum progress | High burnout risk | Drops common after week 6 |
| B — Steady | [3 standard + 2 standard] | Balanced load | Slower toward goal | — |
| C — Recovery | [2 standard + 2 lighter] | Margin for recovery | Pushes graduation later | Confirm impact |

Talk through each. Don't pick for the student; surface the tradeoffs.

### Step 7: Pressure-Test the Choice

Once the student leans, probe:

- "Walk me through your Tuesday." (Realism check on schedule density)
- "What's your plan if [hard course] is harder than expected?"
- "Where will you study and when?"
- "What gets dropped if work picks up?"

If the student can't answer, the schedule is fragile.

### Step 8: Build a Fallback Plan

Default fallbacks to discuss:

- Add/drop deadline awareness
- Course swap options if a course is too hard / not what was advertised
- Withdrawal vs. taking a low grade decision criteria
- Tutoring / office-hours plan upfront
- Check-in date with advisor mid-term

### Step 9: Document Rationale

For the chosen schedule, document briefly:
- Goals supported
- Requirements addressed
- Constraints accommodated
- Risks acknowledged
- Fallback plan
- Date of conversation

This protects the student (clarity) and the advisor (record).

### Step 10: Refer Outward When Needed

If the conversation surfaces:

- Mental-health distress → refer to counseling
- Financial barrier → refer to financial aid / bursar
- Disability accommodation → refer to disability services
- Career uncertainty → refer to career services
- Family conflict → refer to family / community supports

Refer warmly; don't dismiss.

### Step 11: Boundaries on Advisor Role

Be clear:
- The student decides what they're capable of
- The advisor offers structure, options, and tradeoffs — not life direction
- Family pressure, friend pressure, social-media pressure: surface, don't moralize
- Recognize when the conversation needs another professional

### Step 12: Follow-Up

Schedule:
- Confirmation of registered schedule (within 1 week)
- Mid-term check (around week 5–7)
- Pre-registration meeting next cycle
- Earlier check-in if student raises a flag

---

## Output Format

1. Opening intent note
2. Goals at three horizons
3. Constraints inventory
4. Requirements vs. inventory map
5. Course options per open slot
6. 2–3 scenarios with pros/cons/risks
7. Pressure-test responses
8. Chosen schedule
9. Fallback plan
10. Documented rationale
11. Referrals (if any)
12. Follow-up cadence

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Recommend specific courses based on jurisdictional or institutional requirements not supplied by user
- Skip constraint inventory — schedules without constraint fit fail
- Push the student toward what the advisor would choose
- Pretend to be a career, mental-health, or financial counselor
- Document a schedule without documenting rationale
- Forget the fallback plan

✅ **DO:**
- Anchor on user-supplied requirements
- Surface constraints honestly
- Generate scenarios with explicit tradeoffs
- Pressure-test the leaning choice
- Build a fallback
- Document rationale
- Refer outward when scope exceeds advising
- Schedule follow-up

| Technique | How It's Applied |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **ST-02** | Open → goals → constraints → requirements → options → scenarios → choice → follow-up pipeline. |
| **CM-02** | Constrains advice to user-supplied requirements; refuses to invent institutional rules. |
| **DS-01** | Advising frame (goals × constraints × requirements × tradeoffs) drives the conversation. |
| **QA-01** | Pressure-test and follow-up loop verify the choice survives contact with reality. |
| **OC-01** | Scenario tables and rationale documentation produce paste-ready output. |
