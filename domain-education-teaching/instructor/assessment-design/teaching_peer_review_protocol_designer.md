# Peer Review Protocol Designer

## Objective

Produce a classroom-ready peer review protocol that prevents the two most common failures — "it was good" (empty affirmation) and uncommanded criticism — by giving reviewers a calibration activity, criteria-anchored prompts, and structured sentence starters, while giving reviewees a protocol for responding.

## When to Use

- Before students submit a draft for teacher feedback — peer review as a revision step
- During a writing or project workshop
- In classes where giving feedback is a learning objective (writing courses, presentation skills)
- When you want students to develop evaluative thinking, not just receive corrections
- As part of a structured revision cycle

## When NOT to Use

- For high-stakes summative assessments where bias or social dynamics could distort results
- When students have no shared understanding of quality criteria
- In early elementary without heavy modeling and scaffolding
- When the goal is teacher-only evaluation — peer review is always formative here

## Instructions

### Step 1: Calibration Activity Design

Peer review fails without calibration — students need to practice applying criteria to someone else's work before they apply it to their peer's.

```
CALIBRATION ACTIVITY
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Time: [3–5 minutes, teacher-led]

STEP 1: Display a sample piece of student work (anonymous — not from this class if possible).
STEP 2: Teacher models one piece of feedback using the reviewer checklist:
  "[Read criterion aloud] — Let me look for this in the sample… I notice [specific observation].
   A comment I'd write: '[Example using a sentence starter].'"
STEP 3: Students practice one item together:
  "[Read second criterion aloud] — Now you try. What do you notice? Use the sentence starter."
STEP 4: 1–2 students share; teacher validates or redirects without shame.

WHAT TO TELL STUDENTS:
"Feedback is most useful when it's specific. Your job is not to grade your partner — your job
is to show them what you notice. The sentence starters will help."

SAMPLE PIECE NEEDED: [Brief description of what the calibration sample should look like —
strong enough to identify a strength, with one noticeable development area]
```

### Step 2: Reviewer Checklist

Criteria-anchored prompts — one per rubric criterion, at student reading level:

```
REVIEWER CHECKLIST
─────────────────────────────────────────────
Assignment: _______________________   Reviewer: _______________________
Author: _______________________

Read through your partner's work completely before writing anything.

For each criterion:
1. Find specific evidence in their work (a quote, a section, a step)
2. Write a comment using the sentence starter
3. Mark the column

Criterion | Evidence I found (quote or description) | Strength or Development? | Comment
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[Criterion 1 — student language] | | S / D | 
[Criterion 2] | | S / D |
[Criterion 3] | | S / D |
[Criterion 4 if applicable] | | S / D |
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

REVIEW RULE: Every piece of feedback must cite something specific.
"It was good" is not feedback. "[Quote] shows [observation]" is feedback.
```

### Step 3: Sentence Starters (Per Criterion)

Sentence starters prevent the "I don't know what to say" freeze and model academic feedback language.

```
SENTENCE STARTERS
─────────────────────────────────────────────

FOR STRENGTHS:
• "When you wrote [specific quote], I could clearly see/understand [what]…"
• "[Element from the work] is effective because…"
• "I noticed that [specific observation] — this works well because…"

FOR DEVELOPMENT AREAS:
• "I got confused when [specific moment] — I wasn't sure if you meant…"
• "[Specific element] could be clearer if…"
• "The [criterion] part of your work — I'm not seeing [what's missing] yet. One place to add it might be…"

FOR QUESTIONS (when unsure if something is intentional):
• "I'm not sure I understand [specific part] — did you mean…?"
• "Is [element] supposed to [intention]? If so, you might want to…"

WHAT NOT TO WRITE:
❌ "It was good/bad/boring/interesting"
❌ "I liked/didn't like it" (unless followed by specific evidence)
❌ "You need to fix everything in paragraph 2" (not actionable)
```

### Step 4: Reviewee Response Protocol

The author needs a structured way to receive feedback without getting defensive or dismissing it.

```
REVIEWEE RESPONSE PROTOCOL
─────────────────────────────────────────────
After receiving peer feedback:

STEP 1: Read all feedback silently before responding. Don't defend yet.
STEP 2: Mark each comment:
  ⭐ = "This is useful, I'll act on it"
  ? = "I need to think about this / ask a clarifying question"
  ✓ = "I see what they mean but I disagree — I'll explain why"

STEP 3: Ask one clarifying question:
  "You wrote [feedback] — can you show me exactly where in my work you saw that?"

STEP 4: Write one revision plan item:
  "Based on this feedback, I'm going to [specific change] in [specific part of the work]."

REMEMBER: Feedback is information, not a grade. You decide what to do with it.
```

### Step 5: Feedback Quality Rubric

Students (and teachers) need to evaluate the quality of peer feedback itself.

```
FEEDBACK QUALITY RUBRIC
─────────────────────────────────────────────
This rubric evaluates the peer feedback, not the assignment.

4 — Excellent feedback:
• Cites specific evidence for every comment
• Identifies both a strength and a development area
• Comments are actionable (author knows what to do)
• Tone is respectful and growth-oriented

3 — Good feedback:
• Most comments cite evidence
• Identifies at least one strength and one development area
• Most comments are actionable
• Tone is appropriate

2 — Developing feedback:
• Some evidence cited; some comments are vague
• May focus only on strengths or only on problems
• Some comments are actionable
• Tone is mostly appropriate

1 — Insufficient feedback:
• Little or no specific evidence
• "It was good" or similar empty responses
• Author couldn't act on this without asking for clarification
• Tone may be dismissive or unkind
```

## Output Format

1. Calibration activity (steps + sample piece description)
2. Reviewer checklist (criteria-anchored, with evidence and strength/development columns)
3. Sentence starters (strengths / development / questions / what not to write)
4. Reviewee response protocol
5. Feedback quality rubric

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Skip the calibration step — uncalibrated peer review produces the patterns it's designed to prevent
- Include more than 5 criteria in the reviewer checklist — overload produces surface compliance
- Use vague criteria ("Is the essay good?") instead of rubric-anchored criteria
- Allow anonymous reviewing unless class dynamics genuinely require it — accountability improves quality
- Treat peer feedback as a grade-replacement tool — it's formative

✅ **DO:**
- Model one piece of feedback using the sentence starters before releasing students
- Assign pairs thoughtfully (or explain randomization) — avoid pairs with significant power dynamics
- Build in 2 minutes at the end for authors to identify their one revision plan item
- Use the feedback quality rubric when peer review is a learning objective itself
- Debrief after the first round: "What feedback was most useful? Why?"

---

## Quality Indicators

- [ ] Calibration activity includes a sample piece and teacher modeling steps
- [ ] Reviewer checklist items are observable (not "shows understanding")
- [ ] Sentence starters for strengths, development areas, and questions
- [ ] Reviewee protocol includes an active decision step (⭐ / ? / ✓)
- [ ] Feedback quality rubric distinguishes 4 levels with observable criteria
- [ ] Full protocol fits within stated time window
