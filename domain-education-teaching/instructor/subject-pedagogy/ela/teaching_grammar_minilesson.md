# ELA Grammar Minilesson (10 Minutes, One Concept)

## Objective

Produce a 10-minute minilesson on a single grammar concept, taught in the context of real sentences (mentor text + student writing), so students can apply the concept during revision the same day. Output specifies the concept named in plain language, the mentor sentence(s), the noticing protocol, the rule statement, and the application move.

## When to Use

- Brief daily or every-other-day grammar instruction inside a writing unit
- Targeted re-teach when student drafts show a specific recurring error
- Building students' grammar metalanguage so they can talk about their own sentences
- Replacing isolated grammar worksheets that don't transfer to writing

## When NOT to Use

- Teaching writing craft moves that are stylistic, not structural — use `teachsubj_ela_mentor_text_close_read.md`
- A full writing minilesson — use `teachsubj_ela_writers_workshop_minilesson.md`
- Diagnosing what grammar misconceptions exist in the first place — use `teaching_misconception_diagnoser.md`

## Inputs Needed

- **Grade level:** [3–12]
- **Target concept:** [One specific concept — e.g., "comma after introductory clause," "subject–verb agreement with compound subjects," "apostrophes in possessives vs. plurals," "semicolon between independent clauses"]
- **Why now:** [What you've seen in student writing that makes this the right concept this week]
- **Mentor source:** [Author + sentence OR description if generating]
- **What students will revise after the lesson:** [Today's draft / yesterday's paragraph / a specific assignment]

## Instructions

### Step 1: Name the Concept Plainly

Write the concept name and a one-sentence definition that a student could repeat. Avoid hyper-technical jargon when a plainer name carries the meaning.

| Technical name | Student-friendly version |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| Subordinating conjunction comma rule | "When a sentence starts with *because*, *when*, or *if*, put a comma when the part before the main idea ends." |
| Restrictive vs. non-restrictive clauses | "Commas around extra info; no commas if it changes who you're talking about." |

Hard rule: if the rule can't be stated in one student-readable sentence, the lesson is too broad — narrow it.

### Step 2: Pick One Mentor Sentence (or Two Contrasting)

Provide:

- One mentor sentence that uses the concept correctly, with citation
- (Optional) A contrasting sentence that violates the concept, to highlight the difference

The mentor sentence must be short enough to display on a board and read aloud once. Don't bury the concept in a long passage.

### Step 3: 10-Minute Architecture

Use this structure with exact timing:

```
0:00–1:00 — Frame the move
"Today's quick look is at how writers handle ___."

1:00–3:00 — Notice (mentor sentence on the board)
"Read this. What do you notice about [feature]?"
[Take 2–3 noticings; record on board.]

3:00–5:00 — Name the rule
"Writers do this because ___. The rule is: ___."
[Display the rule statement; tie it to student noticings.]

5:00–7:00 — Try with another example
"Here's another sentence missing the move. Where would it go? Why?"
[Whole-class try with a fresh sentence.]

7:00–9:00 — Apply to your own writing
"Open your draft. Find one place this could go. Make the move."

9:00–10:00 — Share-out
"Two volunteers — read your sentence before and after."
```

### Step 4: Provide the Mentor Sentence(s) and Try-It Sentence(s)

Output:

- 1 mentor sentence (correctly using the concept)
- 1 contrast sentence (showing the violation, optional)
- 2 try-it sentences for whole-class practice (one straightforward, one with a small wrinkle)

For each try-it sentence, give the answer and a one-sentence rationale.

### Step 5: Anchor Chart

Output the anchor chart students will reference during revision:

```
RULE: [Concept in plain language]
WHY IT WORKS: [What it does for the reader — one sentence]
EXAMPLE: "[Mentor sentence]" — [Author or source]
COMMON MISTAKE: [What writers often do instead]
WHEN TO CHECK FOR IT: [In what kinds of sentences this matters most]
```

### Step 6: Differentiation

- **Light scaffold:** Students apply the move to their current draft independently
- **Medium scaffold:** Provide a checklist with 3 specific places in the draft to look
- **Heavy scaffold:** Pre-marked draft (teacher highlights candidate spots; student decides yes/no)
- **ELL support:** Sentence frames + bilingual rule statement if available

### Step 7: Formative Evidence

Specify what the teacher looks for during the application phase:

- [ ] Students can find at least one candidate place in their writing
- [ ] At least 60% apply the move correctly on first try
- [ ] Students can articulate the rule in their own words during share-out

If fewer than 60% apply correctly, the concept needs more practice — schedule a follow-up rather than moving on.

### Step 8: Self-Check

- [ ] Is the concept narrow enough to teach in 10 minutes?
- [ ] Is the rule statable in one student-friendly sentence?
- [ ] Does the mentor sentence isolate the concept?
- [ ] Does the application phase use the student's actual draft?
- [ ] Does the lesson end with a rule on the wall students can reference?

8. Self-check confirmation

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Teach grammar in isolation from writing — that's the failure mode this prompt exists to prevent
- Combine two concepts ("commas in lists *and* in introductory clauses") — pick one
- Use a long mentor passage where the concept is buried
- Skip application — without revising real writing, the lesson didn't transfer
- State the rule in jargon students can't repeat

✅ **DO:**
- Pick concepts triggered by actual student-writing patterns
- State the rule in one student-friendly sentence
- Anchor in mentor sentences and contrast pairs
- Apply to live drafts in the same lesson
- Build an anchor chart students can return to in revision

---

## Quality Indicators

- [ ] Single concept, one-sentence rule
- [ ] Mentor sentence isolates the concept
- [ ] Architecture fits 10 minutes with all five phases
- [ ] Try-it sentences include both straightforward and wrinkled cases
- [ ] Application uses student's own writing
- [ ] Anchor chart enables independent use in revision
