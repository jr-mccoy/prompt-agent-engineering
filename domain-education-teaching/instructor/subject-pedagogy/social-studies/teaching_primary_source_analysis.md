# Social Studies Primary Source Analysis

## Objective

Produce a 30–50 minute lesson built around a single primary source. Students engage in the four historical-thinking moves — sourcing, contextualization, close reading, and corroboration — guided by source-specific questions and discussion protocols. The output of the lesson is a defensible interpretation, not a recap.

## When to Use

- Building historical-thinking skills in middle or high school
- Replacing surface "read and answer" worksheets with disciplinary practice
- Launching or anchoring a unit on a specific event or era
- Pre-teaching for a DBQ or argumentative essay

## When NOT to Use

- Multi-source DBQ writing — use `teachsubj_socialstudies_dbq_writer.md`
- Connecting unit content to current news — use `teachsubj_socialstudies_current_events_connector.md`
- ELA-only close reading without historical thinking — use `teachsubj_ela_close_reading_minilesson.md`

## Instructions

### Step 1: Vet the Source

Confirm:

- [ ] The source rewards close attention (it's not just illustrative — it carries argument, perspective, or ambiguity)
- [ ] Provenance is verifiable (author, date, context known)
- [ ] Reading level is accessible with scaffolding for the grade
- [ ] The source has a perspective worth interrogating, not a "neutral fact" recap
- [ ] Use of the source aligns with copyright/permissions for educational use

If the source fails, propose a substitute or note what makes the source pedagogically risky.

### Step 2: Build Source-Specific Questions for Each Historical-Thinking Move

For each move, write 2–3 questions targeted to this specific source. Generic questions don't transfer; source-specific ones do.

**Sourcing — Who made this, and why does it matter?**

Generic stem | This-source version
---|---
Who is the author? | "What does it tell us that [author's specific role/identity]…?"
When was it created? | "Why does the date [specific date] make this source urgent or contested?"
What was the audience? | "Who was [author] trying to persuade, and how do you see that?"

**Contextualization — What was happening at the time?**

- "What was going on in [year/setting] that this source responds to?"
- "If you didn't know this came from [year], what would you guess and why?"

**Close Reading — What does the source actually say?**

- "Underline two phrases that surprised you. What do they reveal?"
- "What does the source not say that it might have? Why might it omit that?"
- "What word choices reveal the author's perspective?"

**Corroboration — How does this fit with what we know?**

- "How does this source agree with / contradict [other source / textbook claim]?"
- "If this were the only source, what would we miss?"
- "Whose perspective is absent from this source?"

### Step 3: Lesson Architecture

Provide the time-stamped sequence:

```
0–5 min — Hook
Display the source (or a fragment of it) with no context. Ask: "What do you notice? What's confusing?"

5–10 min — Sourcing
Reveal author, date, audience. Use sourcing questions. Students annotate.

10–20 min — Contextualization + close reading
Pair work or small group. Students respond to close-reading and contextualization questions in their notebook or graphic organizer.

20–35 min — Discussion
Whole-class or small-group discussion. Use accountable-talk moves (build, question, disagree, cite).

35–45 min — Corroboration and synthesis
Connect to one or two other sources or to prior knowledge. Students construct a one-paragraph defensible interpretation.

45–50 min — Share and challenge
Two students share interpretations; class identifies one strength and one push-back per share.
```

### Step 4: Graphic Organizer

Output the student-facing graphic organizer with these columns:

```
SOURCE: [Title]

SOURCING NOTES
- Who: ___
- When: ___
- For whom: ___
- Why I think this matters: ___

CONTEXT NOTES
- What was happening: ___
- What this source responds to: ___

CLOSE READING
- Surprising phrases: ___
- Word-choice clues: ___
- What's missing: ___

CORROBORATION
- Agrees with: ___
- Contradicts: ___
- Whose perspective is absent: ___

MY INTERPRETATION (1 paragraph)
[Frame: "Based on this source and what I know about [era], I think [author] was arguing that ___ because ___."]
```

### Step 5: Discussion Protocol

Specify:
- Opening question that gets multiple defensible answers
- Two follow-up moves the teacher will use
- Exit move: "What's one thing you're more sure of now? One thing you're less sure of?"

### Step 6: Differentiation

- **Heavy scaffold:** Pre-annotated source with key phrases highlighted; vocab pre-taught
- **Medium scaffold:** Vocab list + first sourcing question modeled
- **Light scaffold:** Source only; students apply moves independently
- **ELL support:** Bilingual key-term glossary; sentence frames for the interpretation paragraph
- **For students with reading difficulty:** Audio recording of the source; chunked source delivered one paragraph at a time

### Step 7: Assessment

Provide a simple rubric for the interpretation paragraph:

| Move | Evidence the student used the move |
|------|-----------------------------------|
| Sourcing | Cites who/when/why this matters |
| Contextualization | References specific historical context |
| Close reading | Quotes specific words/phrases from the source |
| Corroboration | Connects to other source or prior knowledge |
| Defensible claim | States interpretation clearly; takes a position |

### Step 8: Self-Check

- [ ] Are questions source-specific, not generic?
- [ ] Does the lesson move from sourcing → context → close → corroboration in that order?
- [ ] Does the graphic organizer require evidence from the source for every section?
- [ ] Does the final task require interpretation, not summary?
- [ ] Is "whose perspective is absent" addressed?

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Use generic sourcing questions that work for any source — they teach students to skip past the source itself
- Treat the source as a recap of textbook content
- Skip "what's missing / whose voice is absent" — that's a primary disciplinary move
- Accept summary as the final product
- Pick sources where the perspective is uncontested ("everyone agrees" sources don't teach interrogation)

✅ **DO:**
- Anchor every question in this specific source's text/image
- Sequence sourcing → contextualization → close → corroboration
- Include perspective-and-omission analysis
- Demand interpretation, not summary, as final output
- Prepare students for DBQ-style multi-source work

## Quality Indicators

- [ ] All four historical-thinking moves are present with source-specific questions
- [ ] Lesson sequence respects the disciplinary order
- [ ] Graphic organizer demands textual evidence
- [ ] Final task is defensible interpretation
- [ ] Whose perspective is absent is addressed
- [ ] Differentiation does not reduce the cognitive demand
