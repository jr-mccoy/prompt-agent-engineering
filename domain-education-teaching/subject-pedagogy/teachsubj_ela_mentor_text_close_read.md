# ELA Mentor Text Close Read for Craft

## Objective

Produce a 25–40 minute lesson that uses a short mentor-text excerpt (1–3 paragraphs) to teach a single, named craft move. Students leave able to identify the move in published writing, articulate why it works, and have one attempt to use it in their own writing. The goal is craft transfer, not comprehension assessment.

## When to Use

- Inside writers' workshop or a writing unit
- Building students' repertoire of craft moves they can deliberately use
- Replacing "find five literary devices" with "study how a writer made one specific choice"
- Teaching genre-specific moves (lead, transition, dialogue, image, sentence rhythm)

## When NOT to Use

- Teaching reading comprehension on the text — use `teachsubj_ela_close_reading_minilesson.md`
- Teaching a grammar concept in isolation — use `teachsubj_ela_grammar_minilesson.md`
- Whole-book study or literary analysis — use a literary analysis prompt

## Instructions

### Step 1: Name the Craft Move Specifically

Write the move as a verb-noun phrase the student could say. Bad: "good writing." Good: "the writer ends with a single short sentence after a long one."

If the move can't be named that specifically, the lesson isn't focused yet — narrow it.

### Step 2: Vet the Mentor Text Excerpt

Confirm:

- [ ] The excerpt is genuinely doing the move (don't force it onto a passage that doesn't fit)
- [ ] The excerpt is short enough to read aloud twice in under 4 minutes
- [ ] The excerpt is age-appropriate in language and content
- [ ] Other strong moves in the excerpt won't distract from the target

If multiple craft moves are equally prominent, either pick a different excerpt or split into two lessons.

### Step 3: Write the Lesson Architecture

Use the noticings → naming → trying structure:

```
1. Read aloud (3–5 min): Teacher reads twice. First read: "Listen for what hits you." Second read: "Listen for how it's built."
2. Notice (5–7 min): Students mark or jot what they noticed about the writing — not what it's about.
3. Cluster (3–5 min): Whole-class share; teacher records noticings on board.
4. Name (5 min): Teacher introduces the craft move with its name. Connects student noticings to the named move.
5. Why-it-works analysis (5–7 min): Why did the writer make this choice? What does it do for the reader?
6. Try it (8–12 min): Students apply the move to their own draft (or a shared topic if no draft yet).
7. Share-out (3 min): Two or three students share their attempts.
```

### Step 4: Teacher Script for the "Naming" Move

Provide the exact teacher language for Step 5 of the architecture, in this template:

```
"Some of you noticed [paraphrase of student noticing]. Writers have a name for this — they call it [craft move name]. Here's how it works: [definition in one sentence]. Watch how [author's name] does it: [point to the move in the text]. Notice that [the structural feature]."
```

The script must connect what students said to the formal name — never lecture the move first.

### Step 5: Sentence Frames for Why-It-Works

Provide 3–4 frames students can use to articulate the move's effect:

- "When the writer ___, it makes the reader ___."
- "If she had written ___ instead, it would feel ___."
- "This works because ___."

### Step 6: The "Try It" Task

Design the application task with these properties:

- Specific enough that the move is clearly required (not just "write a paragraph")
- Open enough that the move applies to many topics
- Linked to current student writing (revise a paragraph from yesterday) or a shared topic if it's the unit launch

Provide:
- The exact task prompt
- An example of a teacher try-it that applies the move (optional, modeled aloud)
- A success criterion: "You'll know you used the move when ___"

### Step 7: Share-Out Protocol

Specify:
- How many students share (2–3)
- What they share (the sentence or two where they tried the move, not the whole piece)
- Teacher response: name what worked in the move, not "I liked your writing"

### Step 8: Anchor Chart

Output an anchor chart students will reference:

```
CRAFT MOVE: [Name]
What it is: [One sentence]
What it does for the reader: [One sentence]
Mentor text example: "[short quote]" — [author]
When to try it: [One sentence about when this move fits]
```

### Step 9: Self-Check

- [ ] Is the craft move named specifically enough to teach?
- [ ] Does the mentor text actually exemplify the move?
- [ ] Does the architecture move from noticing → naming, not naming → noticing?
- [ ] Does the "try it" require the move?
- [ ] Will students leave with a named move they could use again next week?

## Output Format

1. Craft move name and description
2. Vetted mentor text excerpt with citation
3. Lesson architecture with timing
4. Teacher script for the naming move
5. Sentence frames for why-it-works
6. Try-it task with success criterion
7. Share-out protocol
8. Anchor chart
9. Self-check confirmation

---

## False-Positive Prevention

❌ **DON'T:**
- Choose a "great" excerpt that doesn't isolate the move
- Lecture the craft move before students notice it
- Turn the lesson into a comprehension exercise about the text's content
- Praise generally ("good writing!") instead of naming the specific move
- Skip the "try it" — without application, this is reading, not writing instruction

✅ **DO:**
- Name the move at sentence-frame specificity
- Lead with student noticings and connect them to the formal name
- Make the why-it-works analysis explicit
- Tie the try-it to current student writing whenever possible
- Build an anchor chart students can return to in revision

---

## Quality Indicators

- [ ] Single craft move named with verb-noun precision
- [ ] Mentor text genuinely exemplifies the move
- [ ] Lesson moves from notice → name, not name → confirm
- [ ] Try-it requires the move, not just adjacent skill
- [ ] Anchor chart enables future independent use
- [ ] Share-out names the move, not the piece's quality
