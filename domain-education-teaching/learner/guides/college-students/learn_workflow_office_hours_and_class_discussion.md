# Workflow: Office Hours & Class Discussion Prep

## Who This Is For

- Student confused about something but not sure exactly what
- Student going to office hours and wanting to use the time well
- Student preparing for a graded discussion section (and not wanting to be the one who didn't read)
- Student in a seminar or upper-division class where participation is part of the grade

## What You'll Have at the End

For office hours: a specific question you can actually ask, with enough context for the professor to answer in a few minutes.

For class discussion: a specific claim, the textual evidence supporting it, the strongest counterargument, and one genuine question to ask the room.

## What You Need to Bring

- The class material — assigned reading, lecture notes, problem set, slides
- A vague sense of being confused or having a thought (that's enough to start)
- 20–45 minutes the night before (or morning of)

## Workflow A: Office Hours Prep

### Step 1 — Convert confusion into specific question

**Prompt:** [`../../time-and-discussion/learn_office_hours_question_prep.md`](../../time-and-discussion/learn_office_hours_question_prep.md)

**Input:** "I'm confused about [X]" + the relevant readings or problem

**What you'll get:** Socratic process that converts vague confusion into a specific, productive question. AI does not answer the academic content; it helps you formulate the question.

**Carry forward:** a question with three parts:
1. The specific thing you don't understand (not "I don't get chapter 4")
2. What you've already tried (so the professor doesn't repeat what you read)
3. What kind of answer would help (a definition, a worked example, a conceptual reframe)

**Time:** 15–30 min

### Why This Matters

Office hours are a scarce resource. A student who shows up with "I don't understand the material" gets a generic re-explanation. A student who shows up with "I can follow the derivation through equation 4.7, but the substitution at 4.8 doesn't feel right — am I missing an assumption about the boundary condition?" gets a targeted, fast, useful answer.

The Socratic prompt forces you to identify the boundary of your understanding. That identification is itself part of learning.

## Workflow B: Class Discussion Prep

### Step 1 — Build a defensible discussion contribution

**Prompt:** [`../../time-and-discussion/learn_class_discussion_prep.md`](../../time-and-discussion/learn_class_discussion_prep.md)

**Input:** the assigned reading + your initial reaction or interest

**What you'll get:** structured prep that produces:
- A specific claim you'd defend
- Textual evidence (page numbers, direct quotes)
- A counterargument the strongest classmate might raise
- A genuine question to ask the room

**Time:** 20–45 min

### What "Genuine Question" Means

A genuine question is one you actually don't know the answer to. Not a fake-question-that-is-actually-a-statement ("Don't you think the author is wrong about X?"). A real question that the room could discuss and that you'd be intellectually interested in their answers to.

Professors notice the difference. Other students do too.

## Time Budget

| Workflow | Time |
|----------|------|
| Office hours prep | 15–30 min the night before |
| Class discussion prep | 20–45 min before class |

If you're doing both for the same class, run them in sequence — many of the same reading-engagement steps apply.

## When to Skip

| Situation | Skip / modify |
|-----------|---------------|
| Walk-in office hours for a quick "can you sign this form" | Don't run this workflow |
| Discussion section where participation isn't graded and you have nothing to add | Read the material; if no real thought emerges, that's information about whether the material engaged you |
| You're stuck on a STEM problem | Use [`learn_workflow_stem_problem_solving.md`](learn_workflow_stem_problem_solving.md) first; only escalate to office hours if Socratic step 2 doesn't unblock you |

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | What to do |
|---------|-----------|
| **Walking into office hours with no specific question.** | The prompt's whole job is to prevent this. If it didn't surface a specific question, you may not have read closely enough. Re-engage with the material. |
| **Asking the professor what's on the exam.** | Different conversation. The professor will probably deflect; doesn't help you with the actual material. Use office hours for content, not exam-info-fishing. |
| **Prepared a "discussion contribution" that's actually a monologue.** | Class discussion is dialogue, not lecturing. Your prepared claim is a starter, not a script. Listen to the conversation that develops. |
| **Didn't actually do the reading.** | The prompts assume you read. If you didn't, the questions surface that — and you can't fake the textual evidence step. Either read it or skip office hours / discussion. |
| **Prepared elaborate notes that you read aloud in class.** | The point of preparation is to be ready to think live, not to deliver prepared remarks. Use the prep, then close the notebook in class. |

## Bonus: Reading Conferences (Lit / Humanities Heavy Majors)

If your class includes 1:1 reading conferences with the instructor (common in writing-heavy programs), bring:

- One observation about the text you can defend
- One genuine question about the author's choices
- One connection to another text in the course

This workflow's prep is good preparation for those conferences.
