# Workflow: Prior Learning Articulation

## Who This Is For

- Adult preparing an application to a degree or certificate program
- Returning learner whose institution accepts CPL / PLA portfolios for credit
- Adult preparing course-equivalency or waiver petitions
- Career changer writing personal statements that need to explain the pivot
- Working professional applying for executive education or fellowships

## What You'll Have at the End

- Target artifact (SOP, application essay, CPL portfolio narrative, or petition document)
- A competency map linking your experience to the institution's specific language
- An evidence inventory you can produce on request
- A boundary-honest list of what your experience is and isn't equivalent to
- A voice-checked draft that sounds like you

## What You Need to Bring

- The specific target artifact (which essay / portfolio / petition)
- The institution / program / department's requirements
- Your full career history with key projects and outcomes
- Specific accomplishments (3–10) with dates and measurable outcomes
- The reason you're pursuing this program (the real reason)
- 3–6 hours per artifact

## The Chain

### Step 1 — Inventory residual skills (if not done)

**Prompt:** [`../../../domain-personal-development/career-transformation/career_residual_skills_inventory.md`](../../../domain-personal-development/career-transformation/career_residual_skills_inventory.md)

**Input:** career history + life experience

**What you'll get:** an honest inventory of what transfers to the new context

**Carry forward:** the inventory feeds the next step

**Time:** 60–90 min

**Skip if:** you've already done this within the last 6 months.

---

### Step 2 — Run the prior-learning articulation prompt

**Prompt:** [`../../adult-learner/adult_prior_learning_articulation.md`](../../adult-learner/adult_prior_learning_articulation.md)

**Input:** target artifact + institution's requirements + career history + specific accomplishments + your *why*

**What you'll get:**
- Competency map (experience to institution's named outcomes)
- Draft artifact in your voice
- Evidence inventory
- Boundary-honest list (what experience isn't equivalent to)
- Voice-check items
- Format confirmation

**Carry forward:** the draft artifact + supporting docs

**Time:** 90–120 min

---

### Step 3 — Voice check and iterate

The first draft will be 60–80% right. The remaining work is voice. Read the draft aloud. Mark sentences where:

- The model used a word you wouldn't use
- The structure feels academic-stiff in a way that doesn't sound like you
- A claim feels slightly inflated
- A claim is undersold

Rewrite those sentences yourself. The model's draft is scaffolding; the final voice is yours.

**Time:** 45–90 min

---

### Step 4 — Get one human read

Before submitting, get one human read. Options:

- Writing center at your school (free, usually expert)
- A friend or colleague who has applied to similar programs
- Your advisor (if you have one)
- Someone in the target field (most valuable for application essays)

Ask them: "Does this sound like me? Is anything overclaimed? Is anything underclaimed? Is there a sentence you'd want me to explain in interview?"

**Time:** the reader takes 30–60 min; your follow-up edits another 30–60 min

## Time Budget

| Step | Time |
|------|-----|
| 1. Residual skills inventory (if needed) | 1–1.5 hr |
| 2. Articulation prompt | 1.5–2 hr |
| 3. Voice check | 45–90 min |
| 4. Human read + edits | 1–2 hr |
| **Total per artifact** | **3–6 hr** |

If applying to multiple programs, the first artifact is the expensive one (3–6 hr); subsequent artifacts borrow heavily and take 1–2 hr each.

## Genre Variations

### Graduate-School Statement of Purpose

- 500–1,500 words typically
- Tight structure: trigger → trajectory → fit-with-this-program → what-comes-next
- Voice: yours, in academic register but recognizable
- Evidence: specific projects, dated, with outcomes
- Avoid: passion language, generic "always loved X"

### Undergraduate Application Essay

- Often longer; more narrative latitude
- Personal vignettes welcome; experience-as-evidence
- Voice: yours, recognizable
- Evidence: a story arc grounded in specifics
- Avoid: cliché openings, generic gratitude language

### CPL / PLA Portfolio Narrative

- Per-course narrative usually 1,000–3,000 words
- Maps experience to the named course learning outcomes
- Includes artifacts and evidence
- Voice: professional, evidenced; less personal than SOP
- Format: institution-specified; follow it exactly
- Critical: don't overclaim equivalence

### Course-Equivalency Petition

- Shorter; often 500–1,000 words plus supporting docs
- Maps experience to specific course's content
- Focuses narrowly on the course in question
- Voice: professional, factual
- Format: institution-specified

### Executive Education Application

- Usually multiple short essays
- Emphasis on leadership impact and intended use
- Voice: senior professional
- Evidence: dated, named, measurable

### Fellowship Application

- Highly variable by program
- Often emphasizes intellectual fit + impact
- Voice: yours, calibrated to the fellowship's culture

The prompt adapts to the genre based on inputs.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | What to do |
|---------|-----------|
| Inflated claims ("I transformed the company") | The prompt explicitly rejects this. Re-anchor in specifics: what did *you* do, what was the outcome, what evidence backs it? |
| Underclaiming ("I just did my job") | Equally bad. The prompt surfaces what should be claimed. Trust the framing if it lands. |
| Voice mismatch — sounds like the model, not you | Step 3 catches this. Read aloud. Rewrite sentences you wouldn't speak. |
| Generic essay that could be any candidate | The most common failure mode. The prompt forces specifics. If your draft is generic, you didn't supply enough specific input. |
| Forgot the *why* | The prompt asks for the reason early. If the draft feels hollow, the why is missing or not specific enough. |
| Claimed credit-equivalency for coursework you haven't done equivalent work to | The boundary-honest list addresses this. Take it seriously; reviewers respect self-awareness. |
| Submitted without a human read | The 30 min human read catches embarrassing mistakes and tonal issues. Don't skip. |

## What Reviewers Actually Read For

Application reviewers and CPL assessors are typically reading for:

- **Specificity.** Concrete, dated examples > abstract claims
- **Self-awareness.** What you know you don't know, what you've learned, where you're still growing
- **Fit.** Why *this* program, not a generic program
- **Trajectory.** Past → present → future arc that makes sense
- **Voice.** Reviewers read hundreds of generic essays; yours stands out by being yours
- **Evidence.** Can you back the claims if asked

The articulation prompt is designed around these.

## After the Application

If admitted, the prior-learning articulation often informs:
- Your introduction in the first class ("Hi, I'm [X], I've spent [Y] doing [Z], here for...")
- Conversations with classmates who'll ask "what's your background?"
- Faculty mentor conversations
- Course selection (advisors will calibrate to your stated background)

The articulation isn't a one-time application document. It's how you'll describe yourself for the duration of the program.
